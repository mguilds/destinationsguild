Best Time to Visit Iceland

Best Time to Visit Iceland

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated August 2026

Iceland is the rare country where the question “when should I go?” genuinely changes what you will see, and not by a little. The island swings between about twenty-one hours of daylight at midsummer and barely four at midwinter, and almost everything a visitor wants — open roads, the midnight sun, the northern lights, puffins, ice caves, empty car parks, low prices — belongs to one end of that swing or the other. You cannot have them all on one trip. What follows is an honest account of what each month actually gives you, so you can choose the trade rather than discover it on arrival.

The short answer

Four honest recommendations, depending on what you actually want from the trip.

For the whole island — late May to early September

Every coastal road open, long light to drive in, and the north and east reachable without anxiety. Inside that window, late May, early June and the first two weeks of September give you nearly all the benefit with a fraction of the crowds and cost.

For the northern lights — late September to March

You need real darkness, which means the summer is simply out. September and March are the sweet spots: dark enough for the aurora, mild enough that the roads and the weather are still on your side.

For the lowest prices — November to March, minus the holidays

Rooms and cars are dramatically cheaper outside high summer, and the sights are yours. Give up the Ring Road, base yourself in Reykjavík or Akureyri, and let somebody else drive.

If you can only pick one month — September

It is green, open, quiet, cheaper, and dark by the end of it. No month gives you more of Iceland for less trouble. Late May is the honourable second choice.

Daylight, month by month

Approximate daylight in Reykjavík around the middle of each month. The north gets slightly more in summer and slightly less in winter.

Deep winter — December and January

Roughly four to five hours. The sun rises around eleven and sets around half past three, and even then it stays low, so the whole short day looks like a long golden hour. Plan two outings, not five.

The turn — February to April

From about nine hours in mid-February to fifteen by mid-April. Iceland gains daylight faster than almost anywhere on earth — roughly six or seven minutes a day — so a week’s difference in your booking is a real difference in your trip.

The bright months — May to July

Seventeen hours in mid-May, about twenty-one at the solstice, and no true darkness at all from late May to late July. You can walk out after dinner and drive to a waterfall at ten at night in full light.

The fade — August to November

From about sixteen hours in mid-August down to under six by mid-November. The first genuinely dark nights — and the first aurora — arrive in the last week of August.

Iceland month by month

Weather, light, crowds, prices and what is actually happening, one month at a time.

January — Dark, quiet, and cheaper

Four to five hours of usable daylight, temperatures hovering around freezing, and the lowest prices of the year outside the New Year week. This is aurora season at its most reliable if the sky cooperates. It is not a month for circling the island: stay in the south-west or the capital, take tours rather than drive, and treat any clear night as a gift. Þorrablót, the midwinter feast of fermented and cured things, begins in late January and is worth seeing once.

February — Still dark, slightly kinder

The light is returning — nearly ten hours by month’s end — and the aurora season is still in full swing. Snow is likelier than in January and the landscapes are at their most graphic. Roads in the south are usually manageable; the north and east are not to be taken lightly. The Winter Lights Festival in Reykjavík gives the city a reason to be out of doors after dark.

March — The best of both

Our quiet favourite for a winter trip. You get thirteen hours of daylight by the end of the month and still get properly dark nights for the lights. Prices remain low, ice caves are still open, and the roads are improving weekly. The weather is genuinely unsettled, so build slack into any itinerary and watch road.is each morning.

April — Thawing, greening, uncrowded

The shoulder month that too few people use. Days are long, the puffins begin to return at the end of the month, whale watching restarts, and there are still dark hours early in April for the aurora. Snow lingers in the north and highland roads stay firmly shut. Prices are still modest and the famous sights are almost empty.

May — Excellent value, long light

One of the two best months in the country. Eighteen to twenty hours of light by the end of May, all the coastal roads open, puffins ashore from mid-month, waterfalls at full spring volume from the melt, and a fraction of June’s crowds. Nights are no longer dark enough for the aurora, which is the only thing you give up. Book earlier than you think for the south coast.

June — Midnight sun, and the rush begins

The solstice brings about twenty-one hours of daylight to Reykjavík and effectively no true night at all. Everything is open, the highland roads begin to unlock towards the end of the month, and the whole island is drivable. It is also when the season turns busy in earnest and prices climb. Go early in June if you can choose.

July — Warmest, busiest, dearest

The peak of everything. Warmest weather, all roads open, festivals every weekend, and the largest crowds and highest prices of the year. The south coast between Selfoss and Vík is genuinely congested at midday. The remedy is not to avoid July but to move your day: be at the famous stops before nine or after seven, and go north or east where hardly anyone does.

August — High summer easing off

The first half is still peak; the second half is one of the best fortnights of the year. Crowds thin from about the 20th, prices ease, the berries ripen, and the first genuinely dark nights — and with them the first aurora of the season — arrive in the last week. Reykjavík’s Culture Night and marathon fill the third weekend. The puffins are gone by mid-month.

September — Our pick of the year

Green landscapes, open roads, thinning crowds, falling prices and returning darkness, all in the same month. The autumn colours in the north and east are better than most people expect, and the sheep round-ups, the réttir, are a real and unstaged piece of Icelandic life. The highland roads close through the month, so check before planning anything inland.

October — Autumn, and reliable darkness

The aurora season is properly open and the crowds are gone. Days shorten quickly — from about eleven hours to under nine — and the weather grows serious, with the first snows in the north. Ring Road driving is still feasible for a confident driver early in the month; by the end of it, take tours instead. Prices are among the year’s lowest.

November — Dark, dramatic, inexpensive

Under six hours of daylight by month’s end, frequent storms, and some of the cheapest rooms of the year. It is a Reykjavík-and-day-tours month, and a good one: the Iceland Airwaves music festival fills the city, the ice caves under Vatnajökull open for the season, and the aurora is at its most likely. Do not plan a self-drive circuit.

December — Christmas and four hours of light

Midwinter: sunrise around eleven, sunset around half past three, and a long blue twilight at either end that photographers love. Reykjavík is beautifully lit, the thirteen Yule Lads come down one a night from the 12th, and the Christmas book flood fills every shop window. Expect closures on 24–26 December and 1 January, and expect to pay for flights.

Crowds, and how to dodge them

Iceland’s crowding is concentrated in a very small area at very predictable hours, which makes it unusually easy to escape.

It is two months and two hundred miles

The pressure is on the Golden Circle and the south coast between Selfoss and Jökulsárlón, in July and August, between about ten in the morning and four in the afternoon. That is genuinely it. The east fjords and the Westfjords are quiet even at the peak.

Move your day, not your dates

The coach tours leave Reykjavík after breakfast and are back for dinner. Be at Seljalandsfoss at eight in the morning or eight in the evening — both are broad daylight in summer — and you will have it nearly to yourself.

Sleep outside the capital

Staying at Selfoss, Hella or Vík rather than Reykjavík puts you an hour ahead of every day tour on the road, and costs less. It is the single most effective thing a summer visitor can do.

Go north

Akureyri, Mývatn and Húsavík receive a small fraction of the south coast’s visitors and are every bit as extraordinary. The drive up is long; the domestic flight from Reykjavík takes forty-five minutes.

The four seasons, plainly

Spring — April and May

Melting, greening and quiet. Waterfalls run hard on snowmelt, puffins return in mid-May, prices are moderate and the sights are uncrowded. The highlands remain closed and the north can still be wintry. An underrated season.

Summer — June to August

Everything open, everything light, everything busy. The only season in which the whole island, including the interior, is genuinely accessible. Book far ahead, expect the highest prices of the year, and use the early and late hours.

Autumn — September and October

The best value in the Icelandic year. Roads still open in September, colour in the birch and bilberry, crowds gone, prices falling, and darkness returning for the aurora. October turns the weather serious.

Winter — November to March

Short days, real storms, ice caves, the aurora and the lowest prices. A wonderful season for a Reykjavík-based trip with guided days out, and a poor one for an ambitious self-drive. Respect road.is; Icelanders do.

Iceland timing FAQ

What is the single best month to visit Iceland?

Early September, if you want one answer. The roads are all still open, the country is still green, the high-summer crowds have gone home, prices have started to fall, and by the last week of the month the nights are dark enough for the aurora. Late May is the close runner-up: long light, everything reopening, and far fewer people than June brings.

When can you see the northern lights?

Roughly late September through mid-April, with September to March the dependable window. You need three things at once: darkness, a clear sky and solar activity. Two of those are luck. What you can control is giving yourself enough nights — four or five in the country, away from town lights, beats two nights in Reykjavík. No operator can guarantee a sighting, and any that claims to is selling you something.

When is the midnight sun?

From late May to late July the sun barely sets. At the summer solstice Reykjavík has about twenty-one hours of daylight and never really goes properly dark at all, and the north has it brighter still. It is a strange and lovely thing, and it does interfere with sleep — bring an eye mask, because many Icelandic guesthouses have thin curtains and see no need for better.

How cold does it actually get?

Less than the name promises. Reykjavík in January averages a high just above freezing and a low a little below — milder than Chicago, Minneapolis or Boston in the same month, thanks to the Gulf Stream. July averages a high near 57°F. What punishes the unprepared is not cold but wind and wet: horizontal rain at forty miles an hour makes 40°F feel like 20.

Which months should I avoid?

None outright, but be clear-eyed about two stretches. Mid-June to mid-August is peak season: the famous car parks are full, the south-coast hotels are booked months out, and prices are at their highest. And November through January gives you very few daylight hours — barely four at midwinter — which is fine for a Reykjavík and aurora trip and quite wrong for a Ring Road drive.

When are the highland roads open?

The interior F-roads generally open somewhere between mid-June and early July, depending on the melt, and close again in September. They require a proper four-wheel-drive vehicle and they are never a casual undertaking. Landmannalaugar and Þórsmörk are the two most visitors ask about. The official opening dates are posted each spring on road.is.

When can you see puffins and whales?

Puffins come ashore to nest from roughly mid-May and most have left by the second week of August; the Látrabjarg cliffs, Dyrhólaey near Vík and the Westman Islands are the reliable places. Whale watching runs from April to October with the best months June to August, and the north coast out of Húsavík has the highest sighting rates in the country.

Is Christmas a good time to go?

It is a genuinely charming time and an underrated one. Reykjavík is lit up, the thirteen Yule Lads arrive one a night from 12 December, and the Icelandic Christmas book flood fills every shop. The trade is four hours of daylight, some closures on 24–26 December and 1 January, and airfares that are not cheap. If you want the atmosphere rather than the landscape, it is a lovely trip.
