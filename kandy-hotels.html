Kandy

Where to Stay in Kandy

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Kandy was the last capital of the Sinhalese kings, holds the most venerated Buddhist relic in the country, and sits in a bowl of green hills around a lake at about five hundred metres, which makes it several degrees cooler than the coast and a great deal greener. It is a small city with a serious traffic problem and a great deal of charm, and where you sleep comes down to one question: do you want to walk to the Temple of the Tooth, or do you want the view down over it?

Around the lake and the Temple of the Tooth — walk to everything

Most centralTemple on footEvening drumming

The lake was made by the last king in 1807, and the centre of Kandy wraps around its northern and western shores. Staying here means walking to the Temple of the Sacred Tooth Relic for the evening offering, to the market, the shops and the cultural show, and back again after dark without arranging a tuk-tuk. The temple drums carry across the water at dawn and dusk, which is either the best thing about the location or a reason to ask for a room at the back. The trade-offs are honest ones: the streets immediately north of the lake are congested and noisy by day, and the rooms are mostly older and smaller than the same money buys up on the hills.

The hills above the town — the view, and a tuk-tuk each time

Best viewsCoolerNeed transport

The ridges on every side, Aniwatte and the Rajapihilla road above the lake among them, hold most of the city’s nicer hotels and guest houses, and the reason is the view: the lake, the white temple and the whole bowl of the town laid out below with forest behind it. It is quieter, noticeably cooler at night and much the most pleasant place to sit with a drink at six o’clock. The cost is that everything is downhill and back up — ten minutes and a small fare by tuk-tuk each way, and not a walk you would make twice in an evening in the heat.

Peradeniya and the river side — gardens, and value

Botanic gardensQuieterGood value

Five or six kilometres west, where the Mahaweli river loops around the Royal Botanic Gardens at Peradeniya, is a greener and considerably cheaper side of Kandy. The gardens themselves are the finest in the country and worth half a day; the university is here, the roads are wider, and the hotels are generally newer and better value than the equivalent in town. Fifteen to twenty minutes in by tuk-tuk or bus outside the rush, and rather longer in it. A sensible base if you have a car and driver, or if you are staying several nights.

Digana and the Victoria reservoir — country quiet, well out

QuietestCountry hotels30-45 min out

East of the city, where the Mahaweli has been dammed into the Victoria reservoir, there is a run of country hotels looking over the water and the hills, some of them the nicest places to stay in the district. It is genuinely peaceful, the air is clean and the views are long. Be clear that it is thirty to forty-five minutes from the temple on a winding road, so it works as a restful night at the end of a hill-country leg, or as a base with a driver — and not at all as a way to see Kandy on foot.

Getting around

The centre of Kandy is small enough to walk — the lake circuit is about three and a half kilometres and pleasant early or late — and that is just as well, because the one-way system and the narrow colonial streets mean the town is often quicker on foot than in a vehicle. Tuk-tuks are everywhere and cheap; agree the fare or use an app before you set off.

Kandy’s railway station is the reason many people come. The line south to Ella and Badulla through the tea estates is one of the great train journeys anywhere, five to seven hours of hill country, viaducts and plantation villages. Reserved seats in the observation and first-class carriages sell out well in advance in season; unreserved second and third class are always available, often standing, and the open doorways are half the pleasure. The Colombo line takes about three hours and is the easy way in and out.

For the rest of the country — Sigiriya and Dambulla to the north, Nuwara Eliya and Ella to the south, the national parks beyond — nearly every visitor hires a car with a driver, which on these roads is a reasonable and surprisingly affordable thing to do rather than a luxury. Distances here are measured in hours, not kilometres: forty kilometres of hill road is comfortably two hours.

When to come

Kandy is in the hills, so it is cooler and wetter than the coast all year: reckon on pleasant days and cool evenings rather than heat, and carry something warm for a hill night. December to April is the driest and best stretch, with January to March the pick.

The southwest monsoon from May to September brings real rain to the hill country, usually as heavy afternoon and evening downpours rather than all-day drizzle, and the hills are at their greenest with it. October and November are the unsettled inter-monsoon weeks and the wettest time to be here.

One date dominates the calendar. The Esala Perahera, ten nights of processions with caparisoned elephants, dancers and whip-crackers, is the largest Buddhist festival in the country and falls in July or August according to the moon. The town fills completely, rooms along the route are let months ahead at several times the usual rate, and it is magnificent. Decide deliberately whether you want to be here for it, because you cannot stumble into a bed that week. Our month-by-month guide has the rest of the year.

Getting in

Most visitors need an Electronic Travel Authorisation, the ETA, arranged before you fly, and airlines check it. Sri Lanka has revised its entry rules, its fees and the list of nationalities more than once in recent years, including spells when some passports were admitted free of charge, so we deliberately do not print a figure or a list here — a number that is out of date is worse than no number. Read it off the Department of Immigration and Emigration’s own portal at immigration.gov.lk at the moment you book, and apply there rather than through one of the look-alike sites that charge a mark-up for filling in the same form. Your passport should have at least six months left on it and the officer may ask to see an onward ticket.

Two practical notes for the first hour. Draw rupees or change a little money at the airport, because tuk-tuks, markets and small restaurants want cash and small notes are useful. And remember the airport is not in Colombo: Bandaranaike International is at Katunayake, about 35 kilometres north of the capital and some ten minutes from Negombo, which is why so many itineraries begin and end there. Kandy hotels FAQ

How many nights does Kandy need?

Two is right for most people: an afternoon and an evening for the Temple of the Tooth and the lake, and a full day for the Botanic Gardens at Peradeniya and the market, or for a trip out to a tea estate or an elephant sanctuary. One night is enough to see the temple and will feel rushed. Three if you are using it as a base for the hills.

Can I walk to the Temple of the Tooth?

From anywhere around the lake, yes, in five to fifteen minutes. From the hills above the town it is a steep twenty-five to forty minutes down and a hot climb back, so plan on a tuk-tuk. The evening offering is the time to go; check the hours on the day, dress to cover shoulders and knees, and expect to leave your shoes at the gate.

Is the train to Ella worth it, and how do I book?

It is worth it, and it is the single most enjoyable thing many visitors do in Sri Lanka. Reserved seats open a limited period in advance and the first-class observation car goes almost immediately in high season, so book as early as the system allows through the railway's own channel or a reputable agent. If reservations are gone, travel unreserved in second or third class and stand in the doorway; a good many people prefer it.

Is Kandy cooler than the coast?

Yes, by a useful margin. At about five hundred metres it runs several degrees below Colombo, the nights are genuinely cool and the humidity is lower. It is not cold: that is Nuwara Eliya, higher up, where you will want a jumper and a hotel with a fireplace.

What is the traffic like?

Bad for a town this size, and concentrated in the few streets north and west of the lake. It matters mainly if you are staying out of the centre or catching a train: allow double the time you would expect between about eight and ten in the morning and four and six in the evening. In the middle of the day the centre is fine, and on foot it is irrelevant.
