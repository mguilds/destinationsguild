Where to Stay in Montreal

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Montreal is a French-speaking city on an island in the St Lawrence, and the oldest big one in Canada, which gives it something none of the others have: a genuinely old quarter, a European street life and a summer that the whole city spends outdoors. It is also easy to get around, with a good metro and a compact middle, so the choice of district comes down to whether you want the cobbles, the convenience or the neighbourhood the locals would pick.

Old Montreal — cobbles, stone and the Old Port

Most atmosphericBoutique hotelsQuiet in winter

The oldest part of the city, between the waterfront and Notre-Dame Street, is a few dense blocks of grey stone warehouses, narrow streets and small squares, with the Notre-Dame Basilica at its heart and the Old Port promenade along the river. The hotels are mostly conversions of nineteenth-century warehouses and are the handsomest rooms in the city. It is the best place to stay for a short first visit. The honest drawbacks: it is the most touristed quarter, the restaurants are priced accordingly, the cobbles are hard going with a suitcase, and from November to March it empties out and feels oddly still.

Downtown and the Golden Square Mile — the practical choice

Most hotelsMetro and museumsUnderground city

The modern centre, around Sainte-Catherine Street and up the slope towards Mount Royal, holds the big hotels, the department stores, the Museum of Fine Arts, the universities and the concert halls, with several metro lines crossing underneath and the airport bus arriving in the middle of it. It is the most convenient district and the easiest in bad weather, because much of it connects to the underground city, a signed network of passages linking metro stations, malls and hotels. It is also the least characteristic part of Montreal — a perfectly ordinary North American downtown at street level.

The Plateau Mont-Royal — where people actually live

Best eatingOutside staircasesLittle hotel stock

North-east of downtown, the Plateau is the Montreal of the postcards that are not of Old Montreal: three-storey terraces with spiral staircases on the outside, murals, little parks, and the best concentration of food in the city, from the two famous bagel bakeries and the smoked-meat counters to a long run of small restaurants along Saint-Laurent, Saint-Denis and Mont-Royal Avenue. Mount Royal park, with the view over the city, is at the top of it. The catch is accommodation: there are few hotels and the stock is mostly small guest houses and apartments, so book early.

Mile End and Outremont — quieter, and a little further out

Cafes and bakeriesResidentialMetro walk

North again, Mile End and Outremont are leafier and calmer, with the cafes, bookshops and bakeries that made the district’s name, a long-standing Greek, Italian, Portuguese and Hasidic mix on the same streets, and the enormous Jean-Talon market a little to the north-east. It suits a longer stay, or a second visit, or anyone who would rather have a neighbourhood than a sight. Be realistic about the distance: it is a fifteen-minute walk to the nearest metro from parts of it and twenty-five minutes into downtown, and there are very few hotel rooms.

Getting around

The metro is quick, clean and rubber-tyred, with four lines that between them reach every district in this guide, and it is the way to get about. An OPUS card or a single ticket covers it and the buses. In summer, the BIXI public bicycles are genuinely useful: the city is flat apart from the mountain, the separated bike lanes are among the best in North America, and the scheme runs from about April to November.

From Trudeau airport, the 747 bus runs to downtown around the clock and takes between forty-five and seventy minutes depending on traffic; one fare covers twenty-four hours of city transit, which makes it good value. A taxi or ride-hail is quicker outside the rush and costs a fixed-rate fare to the centre. There is no train from the airport.

Two seasonal notes. In winter, the underground city is not a gimmick but a genuine convenience, and the boots-and-grips question matters more than the coat, because the pavements ice up. In summer the city closes streets for festivals — the jazz festival, the comedy festival, the Francofolies and a dozen others, mostly between late June and early August — which is wonderful and does mean checking whether your street is one of them.

When to come

Late May to early October is the season, and Montreal does summer better than any other Canadian city: terraces on every street, free concerts, the festivals running one into the next from late June, and the whole population outdoors. July and August are hot and humid and the busiest weeks of the year. September is our own pick — warm days, cool nights, the students back and the festivals over.

Autumn colour peaks in the first half of October on Mount Royal and in the Laurentians an hour north, and it is worth a day. Winter, December to March, is cold and snowy and the city leans into it: skating rinks, winter terraces, the Igloofest open-air parties on the Old Port, and the Laurentian ski hills within ninety minutes. April is the month to avoid: grey, wet and between everything.

If you have the days, Quebec City is three hours away by train or bus and is a completely different place — older, smaller and walled. Our month-by-month guide to the best time to visit Canada goes through the year properly.

Getting in

Most visitors from visa-exempt countries arriving in Canada by air need an Electronic Travel Authorization, an eTA, applied for online before they fly and tied to the passport they travel on. Citizens of the United States do not need one but do need proof of citizenship. Nationals of visa-required countries need a visitor visa instead. An eTA is not required if you arrive by land or by sea. We do not print the fees here because they are revised from time to time — read them off the official pages at canada.ca when you book, and apply there rather than through an agent charging a mark-up for the same form.

Two Canada-specific points worth checking well before you travel: past criminal convictions, including an old drink-driving conviction, can make a traveller inadmissible, and there is a formal process for it that is not quick; and taking cannabis across the border in either direction is a federal offence whatever is legal at home. Montreal hotels FAQ

Do I need to speak French in Montreal?

No. Montreal is thoroughly bilingual and you will be served in English everywhere a visitor goes, often by someone who switches language mid-sentence. That said, French is the working language of the province and the signs and menus are French first, and opening with a bonjour rather than a hello is noticed and appreciated. Outside the city, in the smaller towns of Quebec, a few words of French become genuinely useful.

Which part of Montreal should I stay in?

Old Montreal for a first visit of two or three nights: it is the most beautiful part of the city and the one you came to see. Downtown if you want the widest choice of rooms, the easiest metro access and shelter in bad weather. The Plateau or Mile End if you are staying a week, or came for the food, and do not mind that the hotel stock there is thin.

How do I get in from the airport?

The 747 bus from Trudeau to downtown, which runs twenty-four hours a day and takes forty-five to seventy minutes depending on traffic; the fare covers a full day on the metro and buses afterwards. A taxi or ride-hail charges a fixed fare to the centre and is quicker outside peak hours. There is no airport train.

How many days does Montreal need?

Three nights is the right length for a first visit: Old Montreal and the Old Port, the Plateau and Mount Royal, one museum and two good dinners. Four or five if you want to eat your way through the Plateau and Mile End properly, or to take a day in the Laurentians. Add three more and go on to Quebec City, which is the natural pairing.

Is Montreal worth visiting in winter?

Yes, if you go for winter rather than endure it. January and February are cold and snowy, and the city answers with skating rinks, the Igloofest parties on the Old Port, winter terraces, the warmest museum-and-restaurant culture in the country and ski hills ninety minutes north. Bring boots with grip — the ice underfoot, not the cold, is what catches visitors out.
