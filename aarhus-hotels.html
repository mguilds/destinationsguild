Where to Stay in Aarhus

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Aarhus is compact enough that the choice is simpler than in Copenhagen: the old centre if you want to walk out into it, the station side if you want value and an easy arrival, and the rebuilt harbour if you want a modern flat with water in front of it. The whole city centre is about twenty minutes across on foot, so none of these is a wrong answer.

Midtbyen and the Latin Quarter — the old centre

Most convenientWalk everywhereRestaurants

Aarhus is small enough that the centre is the answer for most visitors. The Latin Quarter is the knot of narrow streets north of the cathedral, full of independent shops, coffee bars and restaurants, and the river that runs through the middle of town — uncovered again in the 1990s after decades under a road — is lined with tables from spring onwards. Den Gamle By, ARoS and the cathedral are all within fifteen minutes on foot, and so is the station.

Banegaardspladsen and Frederiksbjerg — the station side

Near the trainsBest valueQuieter at night

South of the centre, around the main station and up the slope into Frederiksbjerg, is where most of the city’s hotel rooms are and where the value is. Jaegergaardsgade, one street up, is the best eating street in Aarhus and a good deal less touristy than the Latin Quarter. Everything in the centre is a ten-minute walk north, and if you are arriving by train from Copenhagen you step off and you are there.

Aarhus Ø and the harbour front

New buildWater viewsBus or a long walk

The old container port north of the centre has been rebuilt over the last fifteen years into a quarter of tall, angular apartment blocks on the water, with the Iceberg building and the harbour bath at its heart. It is modern, airy and good for a view, and the walk back into town along the water is pleasant in daylight. Fewer hotels than apartments here, and a twenty-five minute walk or a short bus ride from the station — not the first choice for a short stay.

Getting around

Aarhus is small and flat in the middle, and almost everything a visitor wants is within a twenty-minute walk of the cathedral. City buses and a light rail line cover the rest on one ticket, bought in the app or at a machine. The light rail runs north along the coast and inland to Odder and Grenaa, which is useful for the beaches and for Moesgaard.

Bicycles are hired easily and the town is built for them. A car is unnecessary in the centre and awkward to park; take one only if you are driving out to the lake district around Silkeborg or north towards the coast.

When to come, and what it costs

Aarhus follows the same calendar as the rest of Denmark — long light and open terraces from late May to early September, dark and mild in winter — with one local difference worth knowing: it is a university city, so the place feels busiest and best when term is running. Late spring and September beat the middle of July for atmosphere, and usually for the price of a room.

Rates are noticeably lower than Copenhagen’s across the board, which is one good reason to build a Denmark trip around a few nights here. Our month-by-month guide to Denmark sets out the whole year.

Denmark uses the Danish krone (DKK, written kr), not the euro. Denmark is a member of the European Union but voted to keep its own currency and has set no date for changing it, so euro notes are not legal tender — though the krone is held in a fixed band against the euro, which is why the rate barely moves from one year to the next. Pay in kroner. Denmark is one of the most nearly cashless countries in the world: cards and phones are taken everywhere, including on buses, at museum desks and at market stalls, and a good many places no longer handle notes at all, so you need very little cash and may need none. Prices in shops include the twenty-five per cent sales tax, which is among the highest in Europe and is already in the figure on the shelf. Tipping is not expected and never chased — service is in the bill, and rounding up is plenty. Decline any card machine that offers to charge you in dollars; pay in kroner and let your own bank convert.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS — the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years — is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today — nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Denmark has been in the European Union since 1973 and in the Schengen area since 2001, so everything above applies here in full and an arrival from anywhere else in Schengen involves no passport control at all. Denmark did not join the euro, so the currency is the krone. If you plan to cross to Malmö in Sweden, carry your passport even though the bridge is inside Schengen: Sweden has run identity checks on that crossing on and off for years.

Aarhus hotels FAQ

Is Aarhus worth staying overnight?

Yes, and most visitors give it too little time. It is Denmark’s second city but it behaves like a large town: the cathedral, the Latin Quarter, the river, ARoS and Den Gamle By are all within a twenty-minute walk of each other, and the student population keeps the eating and drinking better than the size of the place would suggest. Two nights is about right.

Which area of Aarhus should I stay in?

The centre, if you want to walk out of the door into the old streets. The station side, around Banegaardspladsen and up into Frederiksbjerg, for better value, easier arrival and the best eating street in the city one block away. Aarhus Ø on the harbour for a modern flat with a water view, if you do not mind a bus.

How do I get to Aarhus from Copenhagen?

By train, in about three hours, with departures through the day across the Great Belt bridge. It is simpler and usually no slower door to door than flying once you add the airports at both ends. There are also direct trains from Copenhagen Airport, so you need not go into the city to change. Billund airport, an hour away by bus, is the other way in.

What is there to do in Aarhus?

ARoS, the art museum with the circular rainbow-glass walkway on its roof; Den Gamle By, an open-air museum that rebuilds a Danish town street by street across three centuries; the cathedral and the Latin Quarter; the Moesgaard museum south of the city for prehistory, including a famously well-preserved bog body; and the beaches and woods along the bay.

When is the best time to visit Aarhus?

May to September, with late May and early September the best of it for weather, light and price. The city is at its liveliest when the university is in session, so late spring and early autumn beat the middle of July for atmosphere as well as for cost. Winter is dark and mild, with a Christmas market in the old town.
