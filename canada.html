Canada

Canada Travel Guide

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Canada is the second largest country in the world and very nearly empty. About forty million people live in it, the great majority of them within a couple of hours’ drive of the United States border, which leaves the rest — the shield country, the prairies, the mountain west and the Arctic — as some of the least crowded landscape on earth. What that means for a visitor is simple and worth taking seriously: this is not one holiday. It is four or five, separated by continental distances and six time zones, and the trips that go well are the ones that pick two of them.

Why visit Canada

A country with the scenery of a wilderness and the plumbing of a rich city, and remarkably little that goes wrong.

The scale is the attraction

Lakes the size of countries, a mountain range you can drive through for two days, and a national park system that protects the best of it and runs it properly. The emptiness in the photographs is real.

Two cultures, not one

An English-speaking country with a French-speaking province the size of western Europe inside it, and a growing public place for First Nations, Inuit and Métis cultures. Crossing from Ontario into Quebec feels like crossing a border, because in most of the ways that matter it is one.

The cities are liveable and safe

Toronto, Montreal, Vancouver and Quebec City are walkable, well served by transit, strikingly multicultural and, by the standards of big cities anywhere, easy and calm to move around in.

It is easy to travel in

English nearly everywhere, drinkable water, good roads, reliable trains and buses between the cities, excellent hospitals, and a border and customs process that is orderly. The hard part is the distances, not the country.

Canada at a glance

Capital

Ottawa, in Ontario on the Quebec border. Toronto is much the largest city and the main international gateway; Vancouver and Montreal are the other two big ones.

Money

The Canadian dollar (CAD, $). Cards and phones are taken almost everywhere. Displayed prices usually exclude sales tax, which is added at the till.

Language

English and French are both official federally. Quebec operates in French; New Brunswick is bilingual; English is the working language elsewhere.

How it is divided

Ten provinces and three territories, from Newfoundland in the east to British Columbia in the west and Nunavut in the far north.

Getting about

Fly between regions; hire a car for the Rockies and the countryside; use transit in the four big cities. Via Rail is a journey, not a shortcut.

Seasons

Northern hemisphere. Summer is June to August, winter December to March, and the country spans six time zones.

The regions

Five parts of the country, and a sensible first trip takes one or two of them.

Ontario — Toronto, Niagara and the capital

The most populous province and the arrival point for most visitors. Toronto is the biggest city in the country and the main international gateway; Niagara Falls is an easy ninety minutes south-west of it, and Ottawa, the national capital, is five hours east along the St Lawrence. North of the cities the province turns into lake and granite and pine for a thousand kilometres.

Quebec — French Canada

A province the size of western Europe that speaks French and knows it. Montreal is the big, bilingual, festival-going city; Quebec City, two and a half hours north-east, is the oldest walled town in North America. Beyond them the Laurentians, the Charlevoix coast and the whale water of the lower St Lawrence.

Alberta and the Rockies

The postcard Canada. Banff and Jasper National Parks, Lake Louise, Moraine Lake and the Icefields Parkway between them, with Calgary and Edmonton on the plains to the east as the airports and the hire-car desks. This is the stretch that fills up first and costs most in July and August.

British Columbia and the Pacific

Mountains that come down to the sea. Vancouver is the warmest big city in the country and the gateway to Vancouver Island, Victoria, the Sunshine Coast and the inside-passage ferries, with Whistler two hours up the coast road and the Okanagan wine country inland.

  • Vancouver Guild guide

The Atlantic provinces and the North

Nova Scotia, New Brunswick, Prince Edward Island and Newfoundland and Labrador on the east coast, with the Bay of Fundy tides, the Cabot Trail and some of the best seafood in the country; and beyond the provinces the Yukon, the Northwest Territories and Nunavut, which are where you go for the northern lights and the long midsummer light. Guild guides to these are still being written.

Things to do in Canada

Niagara Falls

Not one fall but three, and far better from the Canadian side, which is where the bend of the river puts the view. Ninety minutes from Toronto by road or rail. The boat that goes to the foot of the Horseshoe Fall is the thing to do, and the town around it is frankly a funfair.

The Canadian, Toronto to Vancouver

Via Rail’s transcontinental train takes four nights to cross the country through the Canadian Shield, the prairies and the Rockies. It is slow, it is not cheap in a berth, and it is one of the great railway journeys on earth. No walking required.

Banff, Lake Louise and the Icefields Parkway

The drive between Lake Louise and Jasper runs for two hundred and thirty kilometres past glaciers, hanging valleys and lakes the colour of antifreeze. Go early in the day, and check what Parks Canada is running on shuttles before you set out.

Old Quebec

The only walled city north of Mexico, built on a cliff above the St Lawrence, with the Lower Town below it and a funicular between the two. In winter it is as close to a European Christmas as the continent gets.

Whales, on either coast

Belugas, minkes and blue whales in the lower St Lawrence out of Tadoussac; humpbacks and orcas off Vancouver Island; right whales and the highest tides in the world in the Bay of Fundy. Summer and early autumn on all three.

The northern lights, and the polar bears

Yellowknife and the Yukon are among the most reliable places on earth to see the aurora, from late August through the winter. Churchill, on Hudson Bay in Manitoba, is where people go to see polar bears from a vehicle in October and November.

Best time to visit & getting there

Summer, June to August, is the season almost everything is built around: warm, long-dayed, every trail, boat, gondola and park shuttle running, and the schools out from late June to early September. It is also when the Rockies and Vancouver are at their fullest and dearest, and when you must book Banff and Lake Louise months rather than weeks ahead.

September is our own pick. The weather in the mountain west holds, the crowds thin out within a fortnight of Labour Day, the water is still warm on both coasts, and from the middle of the month the hardwood forests of Ontario, Quebec and the Maritimes start to turn, running through to about the middle of October. Prices step down with the crowds.

Winter, December to March, is a proper season here rather than a gap in one. The skiing in British Columbia, Alberta and the Quebec Laurentians is world class, the city festivals and the frozen carnivals are at their best in the coldest weeks, and the northern lights are a winter business. The trade-offs are real cold away from the Pacific, short days and some mountain roads needing winter tyres. Our month-by-month guide to the best time to visit Canada goes through it properly.

Getting there is straightforward and the choice of airport matters more than usual. Toronto takes the most long-haul flights and is the natural door to Ontario, Niagara, Ottawa and Montreal; Vancouver is the door to the Pacific coast and, with Calgary, to the Rockies; Montreal is well served from Europe. The tip that saves a whole day on a two-week trip is the open-jaw ticket — in at one end of the country and out at the other, rather than doubling back — which the airlines price perfectly reasonably.

Fishing here

Canada is one of the great fishing countries, and the choice is really a choice of water. British Columbia is the famous one: Pacific salmon running the coastal rivers, steelhead on the Skeena and its tributaries, and fly water that draws people from the other side of the world — our guide to fly fishing British Columbia and Alaska covers that coast. Inland, the Alberta foothill streams hold cutthroat and bull trout; Ontario and Quebec have brook trout, lake trout, pike and walleye across tens of thousands of lakes, much of it reached by float plane or a lodge boat; the Atlantic provinces have the Atlantic salmon rivers of the Mirimichi and Gaspé. Licences are provincial rather than national, seasons and limits vary by water, and some rivers are restricted to guided anglers — buy the licence for the province you are fishing and read that water’s own rules before you cast. The whole shelf is in The Guild Fishing Library.

Travelling in your later years

An easy, comfortable country to travel slowly in, where the only things to plan around are the distances and the ice.

The distances, not the country, are the strain

Canada is easy to travel in and exhausting to travel across. Fly between regions rather than driving, give yourself two nights everywhere rather than one, and treat a four-hour drive as a full day with a stop for lunch and a view.

The best of it needs no walking

The Niagara boat, the Via Rail trains, the Banff gondola and lake cruises, the Vancouver seawall, the Quebec funicular and the whale boats on both coasts all deliver the famous sights from a seat.

Accessibility is generally good

Newer hotels, the big museums, the trains and most cruise boats are step-free, and accessible parking is widely provided. The exceptions are the cobbled hills of Old Quebec and Old Montreal, and older walk-up guest houses in both.

Health care is excellent and the distances are long

Standards are high and English is spoken almost everywhere, but a rural or mountain emergency may be a long way from a hospital. Bring prescriptions in their original packaging with a copy, and take travel insurance seriously, because visitors pay for care here.

Winter is the thing to plan around

Ice underfoot is the real risk for an older traveller, not the cold. Boots with proper grip, a hat and gloves, and a willingness to use the taxi rather than the icy footpath, make a Canadian winter trip entirely comfortable.

Getting in: what you need, and what you pay

Most visitors from visa-exempt countries who arrive by air need an Electronic Travel Authorization, an eTA. It is not a visa; it is permission to board, it is applied for online, it is linked electronically to the passport you travel on, and it is usually approved within minutes — but not always, so do it when you book rather than at the airport. If you renew your passport, you need a new eTA.

Citizens of the United States do not need an eTA and do not pay the fee, but they do need proof of citizenship, and in practice a passport is what airlines and border officers expect. Nationals of visa-required countries need a visitor visa rather than an eTA, which takes considerably longer and may require biometrics at a visa application centre. An eTA is not required if you enter by land or by sea, which is worth knowing if you are driving up from the United States or arriving on a cruise.

We deliberately do not print the fees or the processing times here, because both have been revised more than once and a stale number is worse than none. Read them off the official pages at canada.ca at the moment you book, and apply through that official site: the agencies that offer to do it for you are charging a mark-up to fill in the same form.

Two Canada-specific points that catch people out. First, past criminal convictions can make a traveller inadmissible, and that includes an old drink-driving conviction that would be considered minor at home; if this might apply to you, look into it well in advance rather than at the border, because there is a formal process for it and it is not quick. Second, cannabis is legal in Canada but taking it across the border in either direction is a federal offence, regardless of what is legal where you came from.

Canada travel FAQ

When is the best time to visit Canada?

For most of the country, June to early October. July and August are the warm, reliable, everything-open months and also the dearest and busiest, particularly in the Rockies. Our own pick is September: the summer crowds have gone, the Rockies are clear and cool, and from the middle of the month the maples of Ontario, Quebec and the Maritimes begin to turn, which is a genuine spectacle and not a tourist-board invention. Winter, December to March, is a real season rather than a dead one - skiing in British Columbia, Alberta and Quebec, the carnivals and the frozen festivals, the northern lights - but outside Vancouver and Victoria it is seriously cold, and you should plan around that rather than hope.

How big is Canada, in practice?

Bigger than people allow for. It is the second largest country on earth and it spans six time zones, and the single commonest mistake we see is a two-week trip booked as though Toronto and Vancouver were a drive apart. They are about four thousand four hundred kilometres apart; the flight is around five hours and the drive is four or five days. Treat Canada as three or four separate holidays - the Rockies, the Pacific coast, Ontario and Niagara, French Canada, the Atlantic provinces - and take one or two of them properly rather than all of them badly.

Do I need a car?

It depends entirely on which Canada you are going to. In Toronto, Montreal, Vancouver and Quebec City you do not want one: the cities have good public transport, parking is expensive and the old streets of Quebec and Montreal are not built for it. For the Rockies, the Cabot Trail, Prince Edward Island and almost anywhere rural, a car is the trip. The sensible pattern is to fly between regions, use transit in the cities and hire a car only for the stretch that needs one. Note that some national park roads now run on shuttles or timed entry in summer, so a car is not always the fastest way in.

English or French?

Both are official at the federal level, and the practical answer depends on the province. Quebec operates in French: signs, menus and service are French first, and although Montreal is thoroughly bilingual and Quebec City is used to visitors, a few words of French are appreciated and occasionally necessary outside the two cities. New Brunswick is officially bilingual. Everywhere else English is the working language, with French on federal signage and packaging. Nobody will be unkind to you for having no French, but it is their province and the effort registers.

What do I need to enter Canada?

Most visa-exempt visitors arriving by air need an Electronic Travel Authorization, an eTA, which is applied for online before you fly and linked to your passport. Citizens of the United States do not need an eTA, but do need proof of citizenship. Nationals of visa-required countries need a visitor visa instead, and may have to give biometrics. An eTA is not needed if you arrive by land or sea. The fees and the rules are revised from time to time, so read them off the official pages at canada.ca when you book and apply there directly rather than through an agent charging a mark-up for the same form.

Is Canada expensive?

Moderately, and it is the add-ons that catch people rather than the headline prices. Displayed prices generally do not include sales tax, which is charged at the till and varies by province, and tipping in restaurants, bars and taxis is customary and expected at a level North Americans take for granted and most other visitors do not. Hotel rates in Banff, Lake Louise and Vancouver in July and August are the single biggest shock. Against that, the national parks, the city beaches and lakeshores, the museums' free evenings and the best of the scenery cost little or nothing.

How cold does it actually get?

Away from the Pacific coast, properly cold. Toronto and Montreal spend much of January and February below freezing all day, the prairies and the interior colder still, and the North colder again. This is normal, the cities are built for it, and the buses, trains and underground walkways keep running. What matters is being dressed for it: a windproof coat, a hat, gloves and boots with grip are not optional extras in a Canadian winter. Vancouver and Victoria are the exception, mild and wet rather than cold, and rarely see lasting snow at sea level.

What about bears and other wildlife?

Black bears are widespread, grizzlies live in the mountain west, and moose and elk are far more dangerous than visitors assume, particularly in the autumn rut and when a cow has a calf. The rules are simple and they work: keep a long distance, never leave food or scented things in a tent or a car boot, carry bear spray where the park advises it and know how to use it, make noise on blind trails and read the warning boards at the trailhead, which are current and are there for a reason. On the roads, dusk and dawn collisions with moose and deer are a real hazard - slow down.
