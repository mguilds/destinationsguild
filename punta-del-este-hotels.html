Punta del Este — where the river becomes the Atlantic

Where to Stay in Punta del Este — the Best Areas

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Punta del Este is built on a headland with a beach on each side, and those two beaches are the whole decision. The western Playa Mansa faces the Río de la Plata and is calm, shallow, warm and where most of the hotels are. The eastern Playa Brava faces open Atlantic and has surf, wind and the famous hand rising out of the sand. Behind them sits La Península, the original town, with the sea lions in the fishing harbour and the restaurants that stay open out of season. Below is each area, who it suits, and the single most important thing about this town — that its season lasts about ten weeks.

Playa Mansa — the calm side, and the first choice

Punta del Este sits on a headland, and the western shore facing the river is the mansa, the tame one: flat, shallow, warm water with almost no surf, a long line of apartment towers and hotels behind it, and sunsets over the water. This is where most visitors are happiest and where families should be. It is walkable into the peninsula in fifteen or twenty minutes and it has the great majority of the bookable beds. If someone says they stayed in Punta del Este, this is usually where they mean.

Playa Brava — the ocean side, and the famous hand

The eastern shore is the brava, the wild one: open Atlantic, real surf, colder water and the sculpture of the fingers rising out of the sand that is the most photographed thing in Uruguay. It is a better beach to look at and a harder one to swim off, with currents that deserve respect. Sleeping on this side is quieter and more exposed to the wind; it suits people who want the ocean rather than a bathing beach.

La Península — in the old town, by the port

The tip of the headland holds the original town: a grid of low streets, the yacht harbour with its sea lions hauled out beside the fishing boats, the lighthouse, and most of the restaurants that stay open out of season. It is the only part of Punta del Este that feels like a town rather than a resort, and it is the most practical base if you are not driving. Smaller, older hotel stock than the Mansa strip, and the best choice for a shoulder-season visit when the beachfront is half shut.

La Barra — over the bridge, younger and louder

Across the wave-shaped bridge at the eastern end, La Barra is where the nightlife moved when the peninsula got respectable: beach clubs, surf, restaurants that open late and a strip that is genuinely busy in January and genuinely closed in May. Good for anyone under forty and anyone who wants to be out at two in the morning; poor for anyone who wants to sleep at midnight. You want a car here.

José Ignacio and Punta Ballena — out of town, and expensive

Twenty-five miles east, José Ignacio is a fishing village with a lighthouse that became the quiet, costly alternative to Punta del Este — sand roads, a short row of restaurants and very little else, which is exactly the appeal. Punta Ballena, ten minutes west of town, is a wooded headland above the sea best known for the whitewashed terraces of Casapueblo. Both are car-only, both are seasonal, and neither has much bookable inventory. Lovely if it fits; not a base for a first visit without a car.

What to know before you book

The season is about ten weeks and nothing we can write will change that. From roughly 20 December to the end of February the town is full, loud and expensive. From April to November a great deal of it — restaurants, beach clubs, some hotels — is shut, and that is normal rather than a sign of decline.

The price swing is the largest of anywhere we write about. The same room in the second half of January and in May are not comparable propositions. If the dates are flexible, March and November are where the value is: warm, swimmable and a fraction of the peak.

Mansa or brava is the real decision. West side for warm, calm, shallow bathing and sunsets; east side for surf, wind and the Atlantic. Families and anyone who actually wants to get in the water should choose Mansa.

You want a car for anywhere past the bridge. La Barra, José Ignacio and Punta Ballena are not walkable from town and taxi fares add up fast across a week. Inside the peninsula and along the Mansa strip you do not need one.

Out of season, sleep on the peninsula. It is the part of town where something stays open. A shuttered beachfront tower in June is a lonely place to be.

Punta del Este — questions

Mansa or Brava &mdash; which side of Punta del Este should I stay on?

Mansa, the western river-facing side, for most people. The water there is calm, shallow and warm with almost no surf, it holds the great majority of the hotels and apartments, it faces the sunset and it is a fifteen-minute walk into the old town. Brava, the eastern Atlantic side, is the more dramatic beach and the one with the famous hand sculpture, but it has real surf and currents, it is windier, and it is a better beach to walk than to swim off. Families and anyone who wants to bathe rather than look should be on the Mansa.

When does Punta del Este actually open?

The season runs from about 20 December to the end of February, with March as a warm and emptying coda that is arguably the best time of all to be there. From April to November a large part of the town closes, including many restaurants, the beach clubs and some hotels, and bus services thin out. If you are coming outside the season, stay on the peninsula, where something stays open all year, and do not book a beachfront tower on the Mansa strip expecting a resort atmosphere.

Is Punta del Este expensive?

In the second half of January, yes, by the standards of anywhere in South America &mdash; it is the holiday coast for well-off Argentines and Brazilians and it prices accordingly. Outside those weeks it is ordinary, and in the shoulder months of March and November it is good value for a warm beach. The swing between peak and off-peak on the same room is wider here than almost anywhere else we cover, which makes this the one destination in Uruguay where moving your dates by three weeks genuinely changes the budget.

Do I need a car in Punta del Este?

Not if you stay on the peninsula or the Mansa strip and are content with the town beaches, which many visitors are. You do need one if you want La Barra over the bridge, Jose Ignacio twenty-five miles east or Punta Ballena to the west, because none of those is walkable and taxis across a week add up quickly. The coast road east towards Jose Ignacio, Cabo Polonio and Punta del Diablo is the best drive in Uruguay, so a car is worth it if the coast rather than the resort is the point.

How far is Punta del Este from Montevideo?

A little over two hours by road, and frequent comfortable long-distance buses run from the Tres Cruces terminal in Montevideo. There is also a seasonal airport at Punta del Este with summer connections. Most people prefer to move between the two rather than day-trip, because two hours each way eats a beach day; with four or five nights in Uruguay, splitting them between the capital and the coast works better than basing in either.

Can I see the sea lions at the harbour?

Yes, and it costs nothing. South American sea lions haul out on the jetties and slipways of the fishing harbour on the peninsula, alongside the boats, and they are there year-round. It is one of the few genuinely free and genuinely good things to do in Punta del Este, and it is a five-minute walk from the old-town restaurants. Keep your distance: they are large wild animals, not an attraction.
