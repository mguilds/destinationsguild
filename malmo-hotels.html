Malmö

Where to Stay in Malmö

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Malmö is Sweden’s third city, at the bottom left-hand corner of the country, and the only one that is arguably easier to reach from another nation’s airport than its own. Copenhagen is thirty-five minutes away by train across the Øresund bridge, which shapes everything about a stay here: a great many visitors sleep in Malmö, where rooms cost appreciably less, and spend their days on both sides of the water. The city itself is flat, compact, young and much more mixed than the Swedish stereotype, and it divides into four plain choices.

Where to stay in Malmö, area by area

Four parts of the city, what each is actually like, and who each one suits.

Gamla Staden — the old town around Stortorget and Lilla Torg

Most attractiveFlatRestaurants on the doorstep

The historic core between the canal and the castle: two linked squares, the larger formal one at Stortorget and the small cobbled one at Lilla Torg, which is where most of the city eats and drinks outdoors in summer. The castle at Malmöhus and its park are a few minutes west, the canal ring makes a flat and very pleasant circuit, and the station is a ten-minute walk. This is the prettiest part of Malmö and the easiest to spend an evening in; the trade-off is that Lilla Torg itself is noisy until late on a summer weekend, so ask for a room off the square.

Around Malmö Central — the station side

Most convenientBest for CopenhagenStep-free

The obvious base if the point of your trip is partly Copenhagen, or if you are passing through. Trains over the bridge leave several times an hour and reach Copenhagen airport in about twenty minutes and the Danish capital in about thirty-five, which means a hotel here puts two countries inside a comfortable day. The station itself is modern, fully step-free and sits beside the water and the new Universitetsholmen quarter. The area is business-like rather than charming, and it is a five-minute walk from the old squares, so you lose very little by choosing it.

Västra Hamnen — the Western Harbour

WaterfrontModernSwimming

The old shipyard, rebuilt over the last twenty-five years into a low-carbon waterfront district of apartments, boardwalks and open-air swimming spots, with the Turning Torso tower turning above it. It is the best part of Malmö on a warm evening: people swim off the deck at Scaniabadet, the sun sets over the water, and the beach at Ribersborg runs for two kilometres just west. There are fewer hotels here than in the centre and you will walk twenty minutes or cycle ten into town, but for a summer stay with children or a swim before breakfast it is the pick.

Möllevången and the south

Markets and foodCheapestLively

South-east of the centre, Möllevången is the most mixed quarter in Sweden and the best place in the country to eat cheaply and well: a working fruit and vegetable market on the square, Middle Eastern bakeries, falafel stands that locals argue about seriously, and a great many small restaurants. Rooms and apartments are the cheapest in the city. It is a fifteen-minute walk or a short bus ride from the old town. It is livelier and scruffier than the centre, and it is a residential neighbourhood rather than a sight — which is rather the point of staying in it.

Getting around

Malmö is flat, small and built for bicycles — there are more cycle lanes than a city this size has any right to, the hire scheme is cheap, and you can cross the whole place in twenty minutes on two wheels. Walking works just as well for the centre: the old town, the station, the canal ring and the castle are all within a flat fifteen-minute radius.

Buses cover the rest and take contactless cards or the regional transport app; there is no cash on board. The local trains, the pagåtåg, are the thing to know about for everything beyond the city — Lund is thirteen minutes away, Ystad and the south coast under an hour, and Copenhagen about thirty-five minutes. One regional ticket covers buses and trains across the whole of Skåne, which makes the province unusually easy to see from one base.

A word on the bridge. The Øresund train crosses between two Schengen countries, so there is normally no passport control in the ordinary way, but identity checks have been run on that line from time to time and the Danish side has had its own periods of checking. Carry your passport rather than leaving it in the hotel safe, and allow a few extra minutes if you are connecting to a flight at Copenhagen.

When to come, and what it costs

Skåne is the mildest corner of Sweden and its season is the longest: May to September are all reliably pleasant, and the beaches and the harbour swimming spots are busy from late June. August is the best single month, with the warmest water and the longest run of outdoor evenings. July is the Swedish holiday month, so the city quietens and the business hotels near the station cut their prices.

Winter here is mild, grey and wet rather than snowy — this is the same latitude as Edinburgh and it behaves accordingly. The compensations are that the city keeps working through it, that rooms are cheap from January to March, and that the Christmas lights along the shopping streets and the markets at the castle are genuinely good.

On cost, Malmö is the best value of the three big Swedish cities by a clear margin, and it is a great deal cheaper than Copenhagen for an equivalent room — which is why so many people visiting the Danish capital sleep on this side of the water. Eat the weekday fixed-price lunch, which almost every restaurant serves, and eat at least once around Möllevången, where the best food in the city costs the least.

Sweden uses the Swedish krona (SEK, written kr), not the euro, and is one of the most nearly cashless countries in the world — a great many cafés, buses and museums take cards only and will politely refuse notes, so bring a card you can tap and do not bother changing money. Wine, spirits and stronger beer are sold only through the state shops, Systembolaget, which keep short hours and are shut on Sundays; weaker beer is in the supermarkets. Tipping is not expected and never chased — rounding up a bill is plenty. Decline any card machine that offers to charge you in dollars; pay in kronor and let your own bank convert.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS — the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years — is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today — nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave. Sweden is in both the European Union and the Schengen area, so arriving from another Schengen country there is no passport control at all.

Staying in Malmö — questions

Which part of Malmo should I stay in?

For a short visit, Gamla Staden around Stortorget and Lilla Torg: it is the prettiest part, it is flat, and the restaurants are on the doorstep. If Copenhagen is part of your plan, or you are passing through, stay by Malmo Central, where trains leave for Denmark several times an hour. For a summer stay with swimming, Vastra Hamnen on the waterfront. For the cheapest rooms and the best cheap food, Mollevangen. Malmo is small enough that all four are within twenty minutes of each other on foot or by bike.

Can I stay in Malmo and visit Copenhagen?

Yes, and a great many people do precisely that, because rooms in Malmo cost appreciably less than rooms in Copenhagen. Trains over the Oresund bridge run several times an hour from Malmo Central, reach Copenhagen airport in about twenty minutes and Copenhagen Central in about thirty-five. The practical notes: buy the cross-border ticket rather than a local one, carry your passport because identity checks are run on that line from time to time, and remember that you are changing currency as well as country - Denmark uses the krone, not the Swedish krona.

Is Malmo worth visiting?

For two or three days, yes - particularly in summer, and particularly if you pair it with the rest of Skane. It is not a grand city and it has no single must-see sight on the scale of the Vasa in Stockholm. What it has is a very pleasant flat old centre, the best and most varied cheap eating in Sweden, a rebuilt waterfront with swimming off the boardwalks, a two-kilometre beach, and a province of castles, beech woods and fishing villages within an hour by train. It is also the easiest Swedish city to combine with another country.

What is the best day trip from Malmo?

Lund, thirteen minutes away by train: a cathedral begun in the twelfth century, one of the oldest universities in northern Europe, and a small, dense, walkable old town that most visitors prefer to Malmo itself. Beyond it, the south coast around Ystad is under an hour, with long sand beaches and the Ales Stenar stone ship on the cliffs. One regional ticket covers buses and trains across the whole of Skane, which makes all of it straightforward from a single base.

How do I get to Malmo from the airport?

Most visitors arrive through Copenhagen airport, which is the practical gateway: trains run from the terminal across the bridge to Malmo Central in about twenty minutes, several times an hour. Sweden has a small airport of its own at Malmo, mainly for domestic flights, with a coach into the city. If you are flying in from elsewhere in Sweden, the train from Stockholm takes about four and a half hours and from Gothenburg about three.
