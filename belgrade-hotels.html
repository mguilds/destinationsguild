Where to Stay in Belgrade

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Belgrade stands on a bluff where the Sava runs into the Danube, and that geography explains both the fortress at the point and the fact that the city has been fought over and rebuilt more often than almost any capital in Europe. What it leaves is a place with very little of one period and a great deal of all of them: Ottoman gates, Habsburg terraces, interwar apartment blocks, socialist modernism across the river, and new towers on the waterfront. It is not a pretty city in the way Prague is pretty. It is loud, lived-in, warm to strangers, remarkably cheap, and it has a nightlife that has been a byword in this part of Europe for thirty years. Two or three nights does the centre; the hills and the rivers reward longer.

Where to stay in Belgrade, area by area

The parts of Belgrade a visitor actually chooses between, and who each one suits.

The Guild does not name a hotel until an Explorer has confirmed it on the hotel’s own site and on Trip.com. Until then these are areas, not recommendations: pick the district that suits you, then browse what our partner actually holds there.

Stari Grad: Knez Mihailova and Kalemegdan

Most centralLevel walkingFirst visit

The old town, and the obvious answer for a short stay. Knez Mihailova is the pedestrian spine, running from Terazije down to the fortress gates, lined with nineteenth-century facades, bookshops and cafes. Kalemegdan park and Belgrade Fortress are at the end of it, and almost everything a first-time visitor wants is within twenty minutes on foot. The street itself is flat, which matters in a city built on a slope. The cost is price and noise: this is the dearest part of Belgrade and the busiest, and a room facing the street will hear the city until late.

Dorcol

Best eatingQuieterWalk to the centre

The old quarter sloping down from Stari Grad towards the Danube, named from the Turkish for crossroads, and the part of Belgrade that locals send you to eat in. The upper streets are five minutes from Knez Mihailova and residential enough to be quiet; the lower streets reach the river and the Beton Hala waterfront. It has the bars and small restaurants without the full weight of the nightlife, and rooms are mostly apartments and small hotels. The slope is real: going home is uphill.

Savamala and the waterfront

Newest buildingRiver viewsNightlife

The stretch along the Sava below the old town, which was warehouses and decay fifteen years ago and is now the most changed part of the city: galleries and clubs in the old buildings, and the Belgrade Waterfront development with its tower and mall further along. It is close to the centre and to the splav rafts moored on the river. Opinions in Belgrade about the development are strong and divided. For a visitor it means new rooms, river views and a short walk or taxi to everything.

Skadarlija

AtmosphereCobblesKafana dinners

A short cobbled street of nineteenth-century houses behind the Bajloni market, traditionally the bohemian quarter and now the city's old-Belgrade dining lane, with kafanas, long tables, grilled meat and tamburica players going from table to table. It is unashamedly for visitors and still genuinely good fun. Staying here puts you in the middle of that and five minutes from Knez Mihailova. The cobbles are uneven and the street slopes, and the music runs late.

Vracar

ResidentialWhere locals liveGood value

Up the hill south-east of the centre, around the enormous Temple of Saint Sava and the Kalenic farmers' market. Vracar is the comfortable inner-city district that Belgraders themselves rate: tree-lined streets, good bakeries and coffee, no nightlife to speak of, and prices below Stari Grad for a bigger room. It is a fifteen to twenty-minute walk to Knez Mihailova, or a few minutes by tram. The hill is the catch, and it is a proper hill.

New Belgrade and Zemun

CheapestAirport sideSpace

Across the Sava. New Belgrade is the planned modernist city of numbered blocks begun in 1948, where a great deal of Belgrade actually lives, with the Usce tower, the arena and the big hotels; it is cheaper, greener and better for parking, and it is the right side for the airport. Zemun beyond it was a separate Habsburg town until 1934 and still feels like one, with a fishermen's quarter on the Danube, fish restaurants and the Gardos tower on the hill. Both need a bus, tram or taxi into the old city.

Getting around Belgrade

On foot

The centre is walkable and the old town is compact, but Belgrade is built on hills and the map flatters it. Knez Mihailova and Terazije are level; nearly everything leading off them drops or climbs sharply, and the walk up from the rivers is a genuine haul. Pavements in the centre are good, cobbles in Skadarlija and parts of Dorcol less so.

Trams, buses and trolleybuses

A dense and very cheap surface network, with no metro — a Belgrade metro has been promised for decades and is under construction. Buy and validate a ticket rather than riding without one; inspectors do check. Vehicles get extremely crowded at peak times. Demonstrations can reroute or suspend lines at short notice, so allow for that if you have a train or a flight to catch.

Taxis and apps

Ride-hailing apps work well and are the simplest way to pay a fair price. For street taxis use a marked rank, check the meter is running, and be wary of a driver who approaches you rather than the other way round — the fixed-zone desk at the airport exists for exactly this reason. Fares across the city are low by European standards.

To and from the airport

Belgrade Nikola Tesla Airport is about eighteen kilometres west of the centre, on the New Belgrade side. There is a city bus and an airport minibus into town, and a taxi desk in the arrivals hall that issues a fixed fare for your zone — use it rather than accepting an offer in the hall. Allow extra time at peak hours; the airport road carries the main westbound traffic.

Trains, coaches and getting out of town

The Soko fast trains leave Belgrade Centre, known to everyone as Prokop, for Novi Sad in well under an hour and on to Subotica — the easiest day trip in the country. Intercity coaches go from the main bus station for everywhere else, including Sarajevo, Zagreb, Skopje and Sofia. Smederevo, Viminacium and Golubac are all within a half-day by car, and Fruska Gora about an hour.

When to come

Belgrade is a working capital rather than a resort, so it has a season only in the mildest sense and rooms cost much the same most of the year. May, June and September are the pleasantest months, with the cafe tables out and the river terraces open. July and August are hot and can be very hot, with spells over 35 degrees; the city thins out towards the coast and the rivers, which is its own kind of good time to visit, and the splavovi are at full tilt. October is the best of it: dry, golden, mild, and quiet. December to February is cold, often grey or foggy, with January averaging about 1 degree — a perfectly good time for museums, kafanas and cheap rooms. The dates that fill the city are Serbian Orthodox Christmas on 7 January and New Year, which Belgrade keeps twice.

Getting in: what applies, and what does not

Serbia is not in the European Union and not in the Schengen area, so it runs its own border. British passport holders may visit without a visa for up to 90 days in any 6-month period, with a passport valid for at least 90 days beyond the day they leave — that is what the UK government’s Serbia travel advice said when we read it on 26 November 2026. Many other nationalities including the United States are admitted visa-free for comparable periods; we could not open the American source on the day we wrote this, so check your own. Two Serbian rules catch people out: make sure your passport is actually stamped on entry, because leaving without an entry stamp can mean charges of illegal immigration, a heavy fine and a prison sentence; and you must register with the local police within 24 hours, which a hotel or registered apartment does for you automatically. If Kosovo is also in your plans, read the Kosovo paragraph in The Serbia Travel Guide before you book anything — entering Serbia from Kosovo without an existing Serbian entry stamp usually means being refused.

If your trip also crosses into Hungary, Croatia, Bulgaria, Greece or anywhere else in the Schengen area — and most flights from North America reach Serbia through a Schengen hub — the following applies to that crossing and not to Serbia:

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Belgrade — questions

Which part of Belgrade should I stay in?

Stari Grad around Knez Mihailova for a first short visit — it is central, flat and close to everything. Dorcol if you care most about eating well and want somewhere a little quieter five minutes away. Vracar for better value and a residential feel, at the cost of a hill. Savamala for new rooms, river views and nightlife on the doorstep. New Belgrade or Zemun if you are driving, want space, or have an early flight.

Is Belgrade a walkable city?

The centre is, with a caveat that matters if walking is difficult: the old city sits on a bluff, so the pedestrian streets along the top are level while almost everything off them is steep. Knez Mihailova, Terazije and the park paths in Kalemegdan are the flat walking. The climbs from the Sava or the Danube up into the old town, and the hill up to Vracar, are the ones to plan around or take a taxi for.

What are the splavovi?

Floating bars and clubs moored on rafts along the Sava and the Danube banks, and the most Belgrade thing there is — open-air in summer, loud, cheap by Western standards, and running very late. They change names, owners and moorings from season to season, which is why we name none: ask at your hotel on the night rather than trusting any list, including ours.

Is it safe to walk around Belgrade?

Yes, with ordinary city sense. There is no advice against travel to any part of Serbia. Pickpockets work the tourist streets, buses and the stations, and criminals may assume a foreigner is carrying cash. The two local specifics are demonstrations, which are called at short notice and are mostly peaceful but can escalate and can knock out transport and mobile networks, and Red Star against Partizan match days, when it is worth staying away from the stadiums.

Do I need cash in Belgrade?

Cards are widely taken in the centre, but carry some dinars. The UK government's own advice is to keep a reasonable amount of cash on you because electronic payment networks have been disrupted during demonstrations, and cash is in any case what works at the Kalenic market, on the buses and at smaller museums. The dinar is the only legal tender, whatever currency a listing quotes.

How do I get from the airport to the city?

Nikola Tesla Airport is about eighteen kilometres west. Use the official taxi desk in the arrivals hall, which gives you a fixed fare for the zone you are going to, or a ride-hailing app, or the city bus and airport minibus which are much cheaper and slower. Do not take a ride from someone who approaches you in the hall.

Is Belgrade worth more than a weekend?

A weekend covers the fortress, Knez Mihailova, Skadarlija, Saint Sava and a river evening. Four nights lets you add Zemun and the Gardos tower, the Nikola Tesla Museum, the Kalenic market, a day in Novi Sad on the fast train and an evening on a splav without rushing. Beyond that you are using Belgrade as a base for Fruska Gora, Smederevo and the Danube, which it does well.
