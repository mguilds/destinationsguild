Zagreb

Where to Stay in Zagreb

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated October 2026

Most visitors to Croatia change planes in Zagreb and never look at it, and that is a mistake worth correcting. The capital is inland, continental and nothing like the coast: a handsome Austro-Hungarian city of yellow façades, horse-chestnut parks and an unembarrassed café habit, where Saturday morning is spent sitting outside watching other people walk past. It is also, by a wide margin, the best value in Croatia — a good central hotel room here costs what a modest one costs in Split in August. The city divides neatly in two: the flat nineteenth-century Lower Town, where you should sleep, and the cobbled hill of the Upper Town, where you should walk. This page goes through the neighbourhoods honestly, then names hotels by the kind of stay you want.

Where to base yourself in Zagreb

Six neighbourhoods, five of them within a tram stop or two of the main square, and they suit quite different travellers.

Lower Town (Donji Grad)

Where almost everybody should stay. It is the planned nineteenth-century city — a flat grid of grand blocks laid out around a horseshoe of parks and museums, running from Ban Jelačić square down to the railway station. Nearly every real hotel in Zagreb is here, along with the trams, the restaurants and the walk to everything. The streets are level, the pavements are wide, and you can reach the funicular to the Upper Town in ten minutes on the flat.

Upper Town (Gornji Grad)

The oldest and prettiest part: cobbled lanes, gas lamps still lit by hand each evening, St Mark’s church with its tiled coat-of-arms roof, and long views over the rooftops. It is wonderful to walk and awkward to sleep in — mostly apartments rather than hotels, almost no parking, and a steep cobbled approach with a suitcase. Stay below and come up by the funicular, which is one of the shortest public railways anywhere and takes about a minute.

Kaptol — the cathedral and the market

The small, old, busy quarter around the cathedral and Dolac, the open-air market under its red umbrellas that still supplies the city’s kitchens every morning. There are a few hotels and guest houses, you are two minutes from the main square, and you wake up to market traders rather than traffic. Lively in the evening around Tkalčićeva street, which is a row of bars — charming, and not silent.

Britanac — the bohemian square

A leafy square about ten minutes west of the centre, known for its weekly antiques market and a run of good cafés. There are essentially no hotels here, but plenty of apartments, and it is where you stay if you would rather feel like a resident than a guest. Flat walking into the middle of town along Ilica, the city’s long shopping street.

Maksimir and the east

Green, residential and quiet, built around Maksimir park — woods, lakes and the city zoo — about fifteen to twenty minutes from the main square by tram. Accommodation is mostly apartments and small guest houses at prices well below the centre. Good for a longer stay, for families, and for anyone who would rather have trees than nightlife outside the window.

Trešnjevka and the west

An ordinary working quarter south-west of the centre with its own market, its own cafés and almost no tourists, now with one or two genuinely good modern hotels. You are a short tram ride from everything and paying noticeably less for it. The trade is atmosphere: these are plain residential streets, not postcard ones.

Croatia uses the euro. Zagreb is the cheapest place in the country to sleep, eat and drink well, and unlike the coast it barely has a high season — rates move far less between July and January here than they do in Split. A small nightly tourist tax per person is collected at check-out and is usually not in the rate you booked. Cards are taken everywhere; when the card machine offers to charge you in dollars, decline and pay in euros.
Entering Croatia

Croatia is in both the European Union and the Schengen area. US and Canadian citizens need no visa for stays up to 90 days in any 180. The EU’s Entry/Exit System has been fully live since April 2026, so expect a photograph and fingerprints on first arrival and no passport stamp. The separate ETIAS travel authorisation has been postponed repeatedly, is not yet in operation and has no confirmed start date — check the position close to your departure rather than trusting last year’s advice.

Luxury

Zagreb’s top end is small, central and — by the standards of any other European capital — startlingly reasonable.

Boutique & heritage

The most interesting beds in Zagreb are in converted townhouses rather than in anything purpose-built.

Budget & value

This is where Zagreb really earns its keep. The same money that buys a small room in Dubrovnik in August buys a comfortable one here with room to unpack.

Hotel Zonar Zagreb

A modern hotel in the ordinary western quarter of Trešnjevka with the one thing no central Zagreb hotel has: a rooftop pool, with the city and the hills beyond it. The neighbourhood is residential rather than scenic and the tram into the middle takes a few minutes. For a summer stay, or for anyone who likes to swim at the end of a day of walking, it is the most interesting value in the city.

Best Western Premier Hotel Astoria

A 1930s building in the Lower Town restored into a dependable, comfortable four-star a few minutes from both the railway station and the main square. Nothing about it is a surprise, which on a short city stay is a virtue: a lift, a proper breakfast, double glazing, and a flat walk to everything you came to see. It is also one of the easier hotels in Croatia to book on points or a corporate rate.

Family-friendly

Zagreb is an easier city with children than its reputation suggests: flat pavements, trams that count as a ride, a funicular, a zoo and a lake.

The Westin Zagreb

$$$ FamilyLower Town, westIndoor poolLarge roomsLiftWalk to the square

A large international hotel on the western side of the Lower Town, which with children buys you the things small heritage hotels cannot: an indoor pool, rooms big enough for a cot or a third bed, a lift that takes a pushchair, and a breakfast that will feed a fussy eight-year-old. The main square is about fifteen minutes on foot or three stops on the tram, and the Mimara museum and the park horseshoe are between the two.

Sheraton Zagreb Hotel

$$$ FamilyLower Town, eastIndoor poolFamily roomsLiftNear the station

The other reliable choice for a family in the centre: a big hotel on the eastern side of the Lower Town with an indoor pool, a gym and rooms that connect, five minutes from the railway station and ten on the flat from the main square. It is a business hotel most of the week, which in practice means it is quiet and well organised at the weekend, and a tram from the door reaches Maksimir park and the zoo.

A great many people pass through Zagreb for a single night. These two make that night a pleasure rather than a chore.

Zagreb in your later years

Of the three Croatian cities in this book, Zagreb is much the easiest on the legs — if you sleep in the right half of it.

Sleep in the Lower Town, visit the Upper

The Lower Town is a flat grid with wide pavements, benches, and a lift in nearly every hotel. The Upper Town is cobbles and a hill. Book below, and treat the old quarter as an outing rather than a commute.

The funicular does the climb for you

The little railway from Tomićeva street up to the Upper Town is level to board, takes about a minute and costs small change. It removes the only real hill in central Zagreb. Going down again is the same ride in reverse, and there is no shame in it whatsoever.

Trams, not taxis

Zagreb’s trams are frequent, cheap and low-floor on most lines, and they stop every few hundred yards across the whole centre. Buy a ticket at a kiosk, validate it on board, and the city shrinks to nothing. It is the single most useful thing to learn in your first hour here.

The park horseshoe is the nicest flat walk in the city

A chain of linked green squares runs from the railway station up towards the main square, lined with museums and plenty of benches. It is shaded, level and full of other people doing exactly the same thing at exactly the same pace.

Spring and autumn, or December

Zagreb is continental, so July and August are hot and humid and January is properly cold. May, June, September and October are close to ideal. December is an exception worth making: the Advent markets fill the parks and squares for the whole month, and the city is at its most charming in the cold.

Ask one question before you book

Is there a lift? Zagreb is full of handsome nineteenth-century buildings, and a handsome nineteenth-century building often means a grand staircase and nothing else. Hotels almost always have one; apartments frequently do not, and the listing may not say.

Zagreb hotels — questions

Upper Town or Lower Town — which should I book?

Lower Town, for almost everyone. It is the flat grid of nineteenth-century blocks, parks and museums between the main square and the railway station, and it holds nearly all the actual hotels, the trams, the restaurants and the station. Upper Town is the old hill quarter with the cathedral, the tiled church roof and the gas lamps, and it is beautiful — but it is cobbled, steep, mostly apartments rather than hotels, and hard to reach with a suitcase. Sleep in the Lower Town and walk or ride the funicular up.

Is Zagreb worth more than one night?

Yes, and the common mistake is treating it as an airport with a cathedral attached. Zagreb is a handsome Austro-Hungarian city with a serious museum habit, a café culture that genuinely stops the city on a Saturday morning, and the best food in Croatia outside the top coastal restaurants. Two or three nights is right. It is also the most affordable city in the country by a wide margin, so those nights cost less than the equivalent in Split or Dubrovnik.

How do I get from Zagreb airport into town?

Franjo Tuđman airport is about 10 miles south-east of the centre. The airport shuttle bus runs to the main bus station in roughly half an hour and is inexpensive; from the bus station a tram or a ten-minute walk reaches most Lower Town hotels. A taxi or ride-hailing car takes about 25 minutes and is reasonable by European standards. There is no train to the airport.

Do I need a car in Zagreb?

No. The centre is flat, compact and walkable, and the tram network is excellent, frequent and cheap — a single ticket from a kiosk or the driver covers a generous time window. Parking in the centre is metered, zoned and genuinely annoying. If you are collecting a hire car to drive to Plitvice or the coast, pick it up on the morning you leave rather than keeping it parked for three days.

What is Zagreb like in winter?

Cold, grey and, in December, delightful. This is a continental city rather than a Mediterranean one — proper winters with snow, and hot humid summers — and it throws itself into Advent, when the parks and squares fill with wooden stalls, hot wine and music for the whole of December. Hotel rates are low from January to March, which is the cheapest time to see a capital city that costs little to begin with.

Is Zagreb good for families?

Better than people expect. Maksimir park on the eastern side has woods, lakes and the city zoo; the funicular is a short, thrilling ride for a small child; the tram system is an entertainment in itself; and Jarun lake to the south-west has swimming, paths and room to run. The Lower Town hotels are mostly lift-served and a short tram ride from all of it.
