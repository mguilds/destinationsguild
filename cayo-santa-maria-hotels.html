Where to Stay in Cayo Santa María

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Three low, scrubby cays off the Villa Clara coast, joined to the mainland by a long stone causeway, with miles of pale sand and no town of any kind. Every bed out here belongs to a state hotel. The casas particulares — and the two best reasons to come to this corner of Cuba, Remedios and Caibarién — are back across the water.

The cays, and the towns behind them

There are no neighbourhoods out here in the way there are in Havana or Trinidad. There are three cays that are all hotel, and two mainland towns that are all Cuba, and the twenty minutes of causeway between them is the most important decision on this page.

Cayo Santa MaríaTHE BIG ONE

The largest and furthest out, and where most of the rooms are. A long north-facing beach of fine pale sand, shallow water, and a line of all-inclusive resorts set back behind the dunes. Two built-for-visitors plazas — faux-colonial shopping and entertainment complexes — stand in for a town centre, and they do not really manage it. Come for the sand; do not expect anywhere to walk to.

Cayo EnsenachosTHE QUIET ONE

The smallest of the three, between the other two, with two sheltered crescent beaches that are the prettiest on this stretch. Very few rooms and an expensive, closed-in feel. If what you want is a quiet beach and you do not mind that there is nothing at all beyond it, this is the one — subject, as everywhere here, to the list.

Cayo Las BrujasNEAREST THE ROAD

First of the three as you come out along the causeway, lower-rise and lower-key, with the little Las Brujas airstrip and a diving centre. Playa Salinas is the known beach. Being nearest the mainland matters more than it sounds: a taxi back to Caibarién for dinner is a shorter and cheaper errand from here than from Santa María.

Caibarién & Remedios — the Guild’s answerTHE MAINLAND

Caibarién is a working fishing town at the foot of the causeway, unpolished and real. Remedios, twenty minutes inland, is one of the oldest towns in Cuba and one of the loveliest, with a double-churched plaza and almost no visitors. Both have casas particulares. This is where a Cuban-owned bed, a private restaurant and an actual street are, and from here the beach is a taxi ride.

What kind of stay suits you

The all-inclusive, if you want the beach and nothing else

That is what these cays are for. You will get a great deal of very good sand and a week in which nothing is asked of you. Understand what you are buying: a gated compound on an island with no town, in a year when the chains that ran these hotels have left and supply is thin. Read recent reviews rather than the brochure, confirm the property is open — and, if you are American, check it against the prohibited list first, because almost everything out here is on it.

A casa in Remedios is what we would actually do

It costs a fraction of the resort, the money goes to a Cuban household, and it gives you what the cays cannot: a plaza, a market, a private restaurant, and a town that was there three hundred years before the causeway. The beach is under an hour away by taxi. You give up the swim-up bar and you gain a country. For an American traveller it is also the route that squares with the law.

Caibarién if you want the sea and the short hop

Less pretty than Remedios and more ordinary, which some people prefer. It is a fishing town with a long seafront, cheaper again, and it is the closest mainland bed to the causeway — worth something if you plan to be on the sand every day. Fewer casas than Remedios, so book ahead rather than turning up.

Do not plan on mixing the two

This is the practical trap, and it is the same one as at Cayo Coco. From a hotel on Cayo Santa María there is nowhere to walk, nothing to buy that is not the hotel’s, and no restaurant that is not the hotel’s. The taxi back across the causeway is a long and expensive errand. If you want both the sand and some Cuba, base on the mainland and come out — not the other way round.

Getting about, and what is genuinely walkable

On foot

On the cays, your own resort and its stretch of beach. That is the whole of it. The plazas are a shuttle or a taxi ride, the next hotel along is a walk on sand, and there is no pavement, no shop and no bus stop in the ordinary sense. Remedios, by contrast, is entirely walkable and small enough to cross in ten minutes; Caibarién is strung along its seafront and takes a little longer.

The causeway, the airports and taxis

The causeway runs out from Caibarién across the Bahía de Buena Vista, carried on dozens of small bridges that let the water through, and it is the better part of an hour’s drive; there is a toll and a checkpoint at the mainland end. Most people arrive through Abel Santamaría airport at Santa Clara (SNU), about ninety minutes away; the Las Brujas airstrip (BWW) on the cays handles very little. A hired car gives you Remedios and Caibarién in the evenings, which is the main argument for one.

Things worth doing

Remedios, and the Parrandas

A colonial plaza with two churches on it and barely a visitor in sight. On 24 December the town holds the Parrandas — rival neighbourhoods, enormous floats, and a great deal of firework — a tradition going back to about 1820 and added to UNESCO’s Intangible Cultural Heritage list in 2018. If your dates are anywhere near Christmas, build the trip around it.

The beach, which is the point

Miles of fine pale sand and shallow, very warm water, backed by low dunes and sea grape. It is calmer and shallower than the Atlantic beaches further east, which makes it good with children. Early morning is yours; the loungers fill later.

Caibarién’s seafront

A working fishing town that makes no effort for anybody, which is exactly its appeal after a few days behind a resort gate. Boats, a long malecón, a giant crab statue on the way in, and somewhere to eat that is not an all-inclusive buffet.

The bay, and the birds

The causeway crosses the Bahía de Buena Vista, a Ramsar wetland of international importance designated in 2002 and part of a wider biosphere reserve. Flamingos and waders feed in the shallows on either side. Early and late are the times, and binoculars earn their place in the bag.

Santa Clara

An hour and a half inland, a university city with the Che Guevara mausoleum and memorial, and the monument to the armoured train his column derailed in 1958 — the engagement that effectively ended the war. Worth a day whatever you make of the politics.

Diving and the reef

A long reef lies off the north side of the cays, with a dive centre at Las Brujas and a well-known wreck dive. Snorkelling straight off the beach is worth doing in calm weather. Ask what the visibility has been doing before you pay for a boat.

Practicalities

Power, water and the everyday

Cuba’s electricity supply has been unreliable since 2024 and remained so through 2026. Resorts run generators and mostly ride it out; the mainland towns do not, and a casa in Remedios will have hours without power. Take a torch and a power bank, and expect the air conditioning to stop sometimes. Bring any medicine you might need, because the pharmacies are empty.

Money, food and the booking question

Bring cash, in euros or dollars, and bring more than you think. Visa and Mastercard have not been processed anywhere in Cuba since 6 June 2026, for cardholders of any nationality — assume no card works. On the cays everything is paid for in the package; on the mainland you will want small notes. Book a casa directly with the household or through a Cuba specialist, get your dates confirmed in writing, and check any hotel against the current prohibited list before you pay.

Cayo Santa María — FAQ

Can you stay in a casa particular on Cayo Santa María?

No. There is no town on Cayo Santa Maria, Cayo Las Brujas or Cayo Ensenachos, and there are no private guest houses anywhere on the cays. Every bed out there belongs to a state hotel, almost all of them all-inclusive and almost all of them built since the causeway opened in 2000. The casas particulares are back on the mainland, in Caibarien at the foot of the causeway and in the colonial town of Remedios about twenty minutes further on, and that is the only way to combine these beaches with a private, Cuban-owned stay.

Can Americans stay on Cayo Santa María?

This is one of the hardest places in Cuba to answer yes to, and harder than Cayo Coco. U.S. law does not permit tourism to Cuba, so travel has to fall within an OFAC licence category, and the most-used one, Support for the Cuban People, expects you to stay in privately owned lodging and spend with private businesses. There is no private lodging on these cays at all. Worse, when we read the State Department's Cuba Prohibited Accommodations List in November 2026 it named essentially every hotel on Cayo Santa Maria, Cayo Las Brujas and Cayo Ensenachos, and the state hotels in Remedios and Santa Clara with them. That list bars a U.S. person from lodging at, paying for lodging at, or booking a listed property, wherever in the world the booking is made. The route that works is a genuine casa particular in Remedios or Caibarien, and the beach by day. Check the current list yourself before you commit money; it is revised from time to time.

How do you get to Cayo Santa María?

Overland, almost always. A stone causeway runs out from Caibarien across the Bahia de Buena Vista, with dozens of small bridges let into it to let the water through, and it takes the better part of an hour to drive end to end; there is a toll booth and a checkpoint at the mainland end. Sources disagree on its exact length, so we are not going to print a figure we cannot stand behind. Most visitors fly into Abel Santamaria airport at Santa Clara (SNU), about ninety minutes away by road. There is also a small airstrip, Las Brujas (BWW), on Cayo Las Brujas itself, but it handles very little. Cuban flight schedules have been unsettled through 2026 by the fuel shortage, so confirm rather than assume.

What is there to do besides the beach?

On the cays themselves, honestly, not much - that is the nature of the place, and the two faux-colonial shopping and entertainment plazas built out there are not a substitute for a town. The real answer is behind you on the mainland. Remedios is one of the oldest and prettiest colonial towns in Cuba and almost nobody goes; on 24 December it holds the Parrandas, a street festival of fireworks and rival floats that dates to about 1820 and that UNESCO added to its Intangible Cultural Heritage list in 2018. Caibarien is a working fishing town. Santa Clara, an hour and a half away, holds the Che Guevara mausoleum and the derailed armoured train.

How many hotels on the cays are open in 2026?

Fewer than the brochures suggest, and the number moves month to month. Hotels on these cays were among those suspended when the fuel shortage bit at the start of 2026 - Canadian tour operators were publishing closure lists for Cayo Santa Maria in February - and in July 2026 Cuba's prime minister told the National Assembly that about 73 per cent of the country's hotel capacity was closed. The international chains have gone as well: Blue Diamond wound up earlier in the year, and Iberostar, Melia and Barcelo all ended their Cuban operations in July 2026, so the properties they ran are now under direct state management and some have been renamed. We are not going to print a count that will be wrong next month. Get written confirmation that your hotel is open on your dates.

Cayo Santa María, Cayo Coco or Varadero?

Varadero, if the law matters to you or you want to be able to walk out of your room into a Cuban town. It has a real town full of casas particulares, private restaurants, and Havana two hours away. Between the two cay groups there is less in it than the brochures pretend: both are flat, scrubby and hotel-only. Cayo Santa Maria has the better mainland behind it, because Remedios and Caibarien are far more interesting than Moron and they are closer. Cayo Coco has Playa Pilar, which is probably the finest single beach in Cuba. Neither has a street.
