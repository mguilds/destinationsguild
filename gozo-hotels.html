Gozo

Where to Stay in Gozo

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Gozo is the second island, a twenty-five minute ferry from the top of Malta, and it is quieter, greener and about a third the size. It has one small town, Victoria, in the middle of it, a ring of villages around that, and a coastline of cliffs and small bays with a few short beaches. People come for a day and wish they had come for a week, which is the one piece of advice worth giving: the island changes at about five in the afternoon when the day visitors go back to the ferry, and staying the night is the whole point. Rooms here are mostly small hotels, boutique conversions and - the Gozo speciality - converted farmhouses with their own pools, which for two couples or a family are usually better value than a hotel.

Victoria and the Cittadella

Most centralAll the busesQuiet at night

Victoria, which Gozitans mostly still call Rabat, sits in the middle of the island under the walls of the Cittadella, the fortified hilltop that everyone on Gozo once retreated into when corsairs came. It has the market square, the shops, the bus hub from which every route on the island runs, and the cathedral and museums inside the citadel walls. Staying here means you can reach any corner of Gozo without a car and walk out to dinner. It is not on the sea, and in the evening it is genuinely quiet - which for a good many visitors is the attraction rather than the drawback.

Xlendi

Sheltered swimmingRestaurants on the waterSmall and steep

A narrow inlet on the south-west coast with a short promenade of restaurants around a sheltered swimming spot, cliffs on both sides and a cluster of small hotels. It is pretty in the evening, the water is clear and calm, and you can eat well within fifty yards of your room. It is also compact almost to the point of being cramped in August, and the approach from Victoria is a steep valley road that is a bus ride rather than a walk. For a short stay where you want the sea and somewhere to eat outside, it is the easiest choice on the island.

Marsalforn

Most roomsLongest promenadeBusiest in summer

The main resort on the north coast and the biggest concentration of beds outside Victoria, with a bay, a long waterside strip of restaurants and cafes, and the salt pans - shallow squares cut into the rock and worked for centuries - stretching along the coast to the west. It is the liveliest place on Gozo in summer, which on Gozo means busy rather than loud, and the quietest kind of resort town in winter, when a good deal of it closes. The coastal walk out along the salt pans at either end of the day is one of the best things to do on the island.

Mgarr and the ferry end

Best for the ferryHarbour viewsAway from the sights

The harbour where the ferry lands, with the yacht marina below and the church of Our Lady of Lourdes on the hill above it. A small number of hotels look down over the harbour and across the channel to Comino and Malta, and the view is the reason to be here. It is the obvious address for a short visit or an early crossing, and for a trip where you are coming and going from Malta. Buses meet the boats and run to Victoria in about fifteen minutes, but there is little within walking distance besides the harbour itself.

Ramla, Nadur, Xaghra and the farmhouse villages

Farmhouse rentalsMost localCar strongly advised

The eastern villages - Nadur, Xaghra, Qala, Zebbug - are where most of Gozo actually lives and where most of the converted farmhouses are: thick-walled stone houses around a courtyard, usually with a pool, let by the week. Xaghra has the Ggantija temples, which are among the oldest free-standing stone buildings anywhere and older than the pyramids; below Nadur and Xaghra is Ramla Bay, the red-sand beach that is the best on either island. This is the Gozo people come back for. It is also the Gozo where a car makes the difference between an easy week and a lot of waiting at bus stops.

Getting around

Gozo is small - you can drive across it in half an hour - and every bus route starts and ends at Victoria, which means a trip from one village to another usually goes in and out through the middle. The buses are cheap and reliable enough for the main places, less so for the cliffs, the western bays and the temples, and they thin out considerably in the evening and in winter. For most visitors a hired car is worth it here in a way it is not on Malta: roads are quiet, parking is easy and the island’s best corners are the awkward ones to reach. It is usually cheaper and simpler to hire on Gozo than to take a car across on the ferry. Remember Malta drives on the left. The Cirkewwa to Mgarr ferry runs several times an hour day and night and takes about twenty-five minutes; foot passengers buy the ticket on the return leg from Gozo. A fast passenger ferry also runs between Mgarr and Valletta in season. Allow about an hour and a half from Gozo to Malta International Airport, ferry included.

When to come, and what it costs

Gozo’s best months are April, May, late September and October: warm, green in spring, the sea still swimmable at the back end, and the island working normally rather than catering. July and August are hot and busy, and the Blue Lagoon on Comino in particular is so crowded in those months that it is worth going first thing or not at all. Winter is the real difference between the two islands. Gozo goes properly quiet from November: many of the restaurants in Xlendi and Marsalforn close, bus services thin, and some small hotels shut for the season, while the countryside turns green and the walking becomes the best in the Maltese islands. Check directly that your hotel and somewhere to eat near it are open if you are coming between November and March. Village festas run through the summer, and Nadur’s carnival in February is famous, unruly and quite unlike the official one in Valletta.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Malta is as straightforward as Europe gets for an American or Canadian visitor. It has been in the European Union since 2004, in the Schengen area since 2007 and on the euro since 2008, so an arrival from another Schengen country has no passport control at all, and an arrival from outside it is a single check at Malta International Airport. There is no land border to cross. The ferry between Malta and Sicily is an internal Schengen crossing; the one to Tunisia is not, and is checked.

Staying in Gozo — questions

Is Gozo worth staying on, or is a day trip enough?

Stay if you can. A day trip gives you the Cittadella, Ggantija and lunch somewhere on the water, and most people are back on the ferry by five. What they miss is the island after that, which is the whole character of the place: villages that belong to their own people again, restaurants without a queue, and a dark sky. Two or three nights is the right length for a first visit, and a week is not too long if you have a farmhouse and a car.

Where are the beaches on Gozo?

Ramla Bay on the north coast is the best of them - genuinely red sand, backed by fields, with a steep walk or a bus to reach it and no development to speak of. San Blas, further east, is smaller, redder and reached down a very steep path. Hondoq ir-Rummien near Qala and the inlets at Xlendi and Marsalforn are rock and shingle swimming rather than beaches. The Blue Lagoon on Comino, between the islands, is the famous one; it is beautiful, it has no sand to speak of, and in July and August it is extremely crowded.

Do I need a car on Gozo?

You will get far more out of the island with one. Buses connect the main towns well enough and all run through Victoria, but the cliffs at Dwejra and Ta’ Cenc, the western bays, Ggantija and several of the best walks are either poorly served or need a change and a wait. Roads are quiet, distances are tiny and parking is straightforward, which is the opposite of the situation on Malta. Hiring on Gozo is usually cheaper than bringing a car over on the ferry.

Can I still see the Azure Window?

No - and this is worth saying plainly because a great many guides and photographs still show it. The limestone arch at Dwejra collapsed into the sea during a storm in March 2017 and nothing of it remains above water. Dwejra is still very much worth the trip: the Inland Sea, the tunnel through the cliff that boats take out to the open water in calm weather, Fungus Rock and the cliff walk are all there, and the site is now a popular dive over the fallen arch itself. Do not book a tour on the strength of a photograph of the window.

What is a Gozo farmhouse, and is it a good idea?

It is the island’s characteristic rental: an old stone farmhouse, often two or three hundred years old, built around a courtyard with very thick walls, usually converted with a private pool and let by the week. For two couples, a family, or anyone staying more than a few nights they are generally better value and far more pleasant than a hotel room, and they are the reason many visitors come back. The practical points: they are almost all in the villages rather than on the sea, you will want a car, and the thick walls that keep them cool in August make them cold in January, so check the heating if you are coming in winter.
