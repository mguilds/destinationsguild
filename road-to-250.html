The Road to 250: America’s Founding Trail with Best Western

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

The Fourth of July 2026 has come and gone, but the two hundred and fiftieth anniversary of the United States was never a single day. The Revolution happened one winter, one battle and one hard choice at a time, and each of those is now turning 250 in its turn — beginning this Christmas with Washington’s crossing of the Delaware. This is the trail, in the order the story happened, from the Mayflower to the capital, with two or three Best Western hotels picked at every stop.

Prologue — Plymouth, 1620

Massachusetts · the Mayflower, a century and a half before the Revolution

Where the idea of self-government came ashore

Before there was a Declaration there was a compact. In November 1620, still aboard the Mayflower off Cape Cod, the passengers signed the Mayflower Compact, agreeing to govern themselves by laws of their own making; a few weeks later they settled at Plymouth. Walk the waterfront to Plymouth Rock and the Mayflower II, a full-size replica of the ship, then spend a morning at Plimoth Patuxet Museums, where the 1620s English village and the Wampanoag homesite are brought to life. It is the natural first page of the story.

Boston, Lexington & Concord, 1775

Massachusetts · where the argument became a war

One if by land, two if by sea

Boston’s Freedom Trail, a red line on the pavement about two and a half miles long, links sixteen sites from Boston Common to the Old North Church, where two lanterns were hung on the night of 18 April 1775. The next morning, militia and British regulars met on Lexington Green and at Concord’s North Bridge — “the shot heard round the world”. Minute Man National Historical Park keeps the Battle Road between them. South of the city, Quincy holds the birthplaces of John Adams and John Quincy Adams, two presidents from one farm.

Philadelphia, 1776

Pennsylvania · the Declaration, and later the Constitution

The room where it was signed

Independence Hall is where the Second Continental Congress adopted the Declaration of Independence on 4 July 1776, and where the Constitution was written eleven years later. The Liberty Bell hangs across the lawn, Carpenters’ Hall and Christ Church are a short walk away, and the Museum of the American Revolution, on Chestnut Street, tells the whole war in one building — including George Washington’s own field tent. Tickets for Independence Hall are timed; book them before you go.

Washington’s Crossing, Trenton & Princeton — Christmas 1776

Pennsylvania & New Jersey · the 250th falls on 25–26 December 2026

The night the Revolution was saved

By December 1776 the army was shrinking and the cause looked lost. On Christmas night Washington led his men across the ice-choked Delaware River at McConkey’s Ferry, marched through sleet and took the Hessian garrison at Trenton by surprise on the morning of the 26th. A week later, on 3 January 1777, he won again at Princeton. Washington Crossing Historic Park on the Pennsylvania bank traditionally re-enacts the crossing on Christmas Day; check this year’s programme before you travel. In Trenton, the Old Barracks Museum still stands, and Princeton Battlefield State Park is a quiet field worth the detour.

Brandywine & Valley Forge, 1777–78

Pennsylvania · a defeat, a winter, and the army that came out of it

The winter that made an army

On 11 September 1777 the British beat Washington at Brandywine and went on to take Philadelphia. That December the Continental Army marched into winter quarters at Valley Forge, where hunger, cold and disease killed hundreds — and where Baron von Steuben drilled the survivors into a professional force. Valley Forge National Historical Park keeps the huts, the parade ground and Washington’s headquarters; Brandywine Battlefield Park is at Chadds Ford, on the Brandywine Creek.

Williamsburg & Yorktown, 1781

Virginia · where the war was won

The world turned upside down

Williamsburg was the capital of colonial Virginia, and Colonial Williamsburg brings its streets, taverns and Capitol back to life. Thirteen miles away at Yorktown, American and French forces besieged Lord Cornwallis until he surrendered on 19 October 1781, the victory that effectively ended the war. The American Revolution Museum at Yorktown and the battlefield itself sit together on the York River, and Jamestown, where English Virginia began in 1607, is a short drive along the Colonial Parkway.

Washington, D.C. & Mount Vernon

The capital that came of it all

See the Declaration itself

The trail ends where the documents are kept: the original Declaration of Independence, Constitution and Bill of Rights are on display in the Rotunda of the National Archives, free to visit. The National Mall runs from the Capitol past the Washington Monument to the Lincoln Memorial. Fifteen miles south, above the Potomac, is Mount Vernon, George Washington’s home and resting place. There are no Best Western hotels inside the District itself, so we have chosen three close by.
