Tartu

Where to Stay in Tartu

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Tartu is Estonia’s second city and its university town, and it is smaller and gentler than that makes it sound — a flat, walkable centre of pastel classical buildings around a sloping market square, with a wooded hill of cathedral ruins immediately behind it and a slow river along one side. The university was founded here in 1632 and still sets the rhythm of the place: in term time the cafes are full and the bars are cheap, and in high summer a good deal of the town goes away. Around the centre sit two quarters of painted wooden houses, and out on the edge of town, on a former Soviet airfield, stands the Estonian National Museum, which is the best single reason to come.

Kesklinn: the centre around Town Hall Square

Most centralWalk to everythingWhere most hotels are

Tartu’s centre is small, flat and pink-and-cream, built around a sloping Town Hall Square that runs down to the river with the leaning house at one corner and the Kissing Students fountain in the middle of it. Nearly all of the city’s hotel stock is within a few streets of here, and so is nearly everything a visitor wants: the university’s main building, the museums, the cafes that fill with students in term time and empty in July. If you are coming for two nights, stay here and do not think about it any further.

Toomemägi: the cathedral hill

Green and quietThe university quarterFew beds

Immediately behind the centre the ground rises into a wooded park, and on top of it stand the red-brick ruins of the medieval cathedral, half of it rebuilt as a museum and the rest left open to the sky. The old anatomical theatre, the observatory and a scatter of university buildings are up here among the trees, linked by two small bridges that students have superstitions about. There is very little accommodation on the hill itself, which is rather the point of it: this is where you walk in the evening, five minutes above a town you can stay in.

Karlova: the wooden quarter

Painted timber housesLocal cafesFifteen minutes out

South of the centre, Karlova is a grid of wooden houses with carved porches and long gardens, built for workers a century ago and now the part of Tartu with the most character per street. There are galleries, bakeries and small bars along its main run, a neighbourhood festival in summer, and almost no hotels — what you find here is apartments and the occasional guesthouse. It is a flat fifteen minutes’ walk into the centre. Pick it if you would rather live somewhere for a few days than sleep in a lobby.

Ülejõe: across the Emajõgi

QuieterCheaperOver a bridge

The far bank of the Emajõgi is residential, ordinary and noticeably cheaper, and the river is narrow enough that crossing it costs you five minutes rather than a journey. The Estonian National Museum sits out this way, on the old Soviet airfield at Raadi, and it is one of the best museums in the Baltic states — a low glass building running up a former runway, with a permanent exhibition on Estonian life that is worth the trip on its own. Stay here for value rather than atmosphere, and expect to walk or take a bus into town each day.

Getting around Tartu

On foot

Tartu is the easiest Estonian city to walk: the centre is flat, compact and almost entirely free of hills apart from Toomemägi itself, and you can cross the whole of the old town in ten minutes. Most visitors never use any other form of transport.

Buses

The town bus network covers the outer districts and the National Museum, and the coach station in the centre is where the long-distance services to Tallinn, Riga and the rest of the country leave from. Tickets are bought on board or with a card.

The train from Tallinn

Trains run between Tallinn and Tartu several times a day and take a little over two hours on the faster services, arriving at a small station a short walk from the centre. It is a comfortable, inexpensive and scenic way to arrive, and for most visitors it is a better choice than driving.

Driving

A car is of no use inside Tartu and a great deal of use outside it — the national park at Lahemaa is a long way north, but Söömaa, the bog landscapes west of here and the border lakes to the east all want one. Driving is on the right, and dipped headlights are required day and night all year.

When to come, and what it costs

Tartu is a university town and its calendar follows the students. Term time, roughly September to May with a winter break, is when the cafes are full and the city feels like itself; in July and early August a good share of the population is elsewhere and the centre is noticeably quiet, which some visitors find restful and others find flat. Summer days are long and mild, with the river swimmable at the edges and festivals most weekends. Winter is cold, dark and cheap, and the snow suits the wooden quarters. The Guild would come in late May or in September, when the light is good and the town is awake.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Estonia itself is straightforward: in the European Union since 2004, in the Schengen area since the end of 2007 and on the euro since the beginning of 2011. An arrival from another Schengen country has no passport control at all, and an arrival from outside it is a single check at the airport you land in. The land borders with Latvia and the sea crossing from Finland are internal Schengen movements. The eastern border with Russia is an external EU frontier and the arrangements at its crossings have changed more than once in recent years, so if any part of your plan depends on one, check with the Estonian Police and Border Guard Board before you book rather than trusting anything written earlier, including this page.

One point of confusion worth clearing up: Estonian e-Residency is a digital identity for running a business online. It is not residency, it is not a visa and it gives you no right to enter or stay. If you are coming as a visitor, the Schengen rules above are the ones that apply to you.

Tartu — questions

Is Tartu worth a night, or is it a day trip from Tallinn?

It deserves a night. The train takes a little over two hours each way, so a day trip gives you perhaps five hours in the city, which is enough for Town Hall Square and the cathedral hill and nothing else. Staying over gets you the evening, which is when a university town is at its best, and a second morning for the Estonian National Museum or the wooden streets of Karlova. Two nights is the comfortable answer for most people.

What is the Estonian National Museum, and is it far?

It is the national museum of Estonian life and it is the single best thing to see in Tartu. It stands on the edge of town at Raadi, on a former Soviet military airfield, with the building itself running up the line of the old runway. It is a few kilometres from the centre, reachable by town bus, by taxi or by a long walk, and it wants half a day rather than an hour. Check the opening days before you go out there, because it is not a short hop back.

Which is better, Tartu or Tallinn?

They are not really competing. Tallinn has the walled medieval town, the sea, the ferries and the international flights, and it is where almost every visitor starts. Tartu has the university, the student life, the best museum in the country and a calmer, more Estonian feel, and it is where people who have already seen Tallinn tend to go next. If you have four days or more, do both; the train between them is easy. If you have two, stay in Tallinn.

Is anything open in July?

Most things, but the city empties of students and the atmosphere changes. Museums, restaurants and hotels run normally and there are festivals through the summer, so you will not find a closed town. What you will find is fewer people, quieter cafes and a slower pace, which is either the attraction or the disappointment depending on what you came for. Term time is the livelier choice.

Do I need Estonian or Russian?

Neither. English is widely spoken in Tartu, particularly by anyone connected with the university, which is a large share of the people a visitor meets. Estonian is a Finno-Ugric language related to Finnish and not to Russian or Latvian, and nobody expects a visitor to have any of it; learning aitiäh for thank you will still be noticed kindly.
