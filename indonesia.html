Indonesia Travel Guide

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated August 2026

Seventeen thousand islands, three time zones and a country that changes character every time you land. The temples and rice terraces of Bali, the vast stone mandala of Borobudur at first light, the smoking caldera of Mount Bromo, the quiet coasts of Lombok and the car-free Gili Islands, and reefs that hold more marine life than anywhere on earth. Warm, inexpensive and deeply courteous, this is the Guild’s chapter on Indonesia.

Why visit Indonesia

Few countries offer so many entirely different holidays under one flag — temples, volcanoes, reefs, rainforest and some of the warmest hospitality in Asia.

Temples & ceremony

Borobudur and Prambanan in Java, and a Bali where offerings are laid on doorsteps every morning and a temple festival is always somewhere nearby. Living culture, not museum culture.

Volcanoes & landscape

Sunrise over the Bromo caldera, the green stair-steps of the Jatiluwih and Tegallalang rice terraces, and Mount Rinjani rising over Lombok. Indonesia sits on the Ring of Fire and looks it.

Reefs & islands

The country sits in the Coral Triangle — Raja Ampat, Bunaken and Komodo for divers, and the gentle shallows of the Gili Islands and Menjangan for anyone with a snorkel.

Value & welcome

Your money goes remarkably far, service is gracious, and English is widely spoken in Bali and the tourist centres. It is an easy country to be a guest in.

How the country divides up

Bali

Bali is a Hindu island in the world’s largest Muslim country, and it feels like nowhere else in Indonesia. Ubud inland is the cultural and wellness heart, among rice terraces and river gorges; the south — Seminyak, Kuta, Jimbaran, Nusa Dua and clifftop Uluwatu — holds the beaches and the resorts. Nusa Dua and Sanur are the calmest and flattest, and the kindest to older travellers. It is also the most developed and traffic-bound part of the country, so choose one or two bases rather than trying to see it all.

Java — temples and volcanoes

Java holds the capital and the country’s greatest monuments. Jakarta is a sprawling megacity best used as a connection; Yogyakarta is the graceful cultural capital and the base for Borobudur, the largest Buddhist monument on earth, and the Hindu towers of Prambanan. Further east, Mount Bromo delivers Indonesia’s iconic sunrise over a smoking caldera — a pre-dawn start in genuinely cold air, and worth every minute.

Lombok & the Gili Islands

Lombok is greener and far less developed than Bali, with good southern beaches and Mount Rinjani above everything. The three Gili Islands offshore are tiny, car-free and ringed by coral — Gili Air the gentle middle ground, Gili Meno the sleepy one, Gili Trawangan the liveliest. Transport is by pony cart or on foot, and access is by fast boat: lovely in the calm months, uncomfortable in the rough ones.

Sumatra, Sulawesi and the east

This is Indonesia’s wilder side, and it asks for time. Sumatra offers Lake Toba, the immense cool caldera lake of a supervolcano, and Bukit Lawang, one of the few places on earth to see Sumatran orangutans in rainforest. Sulawesi pairs the extraordinary funeral traditions of Tana Toraja with the diving at Bunaken, while Komodo National Park and, further east, Raja Ampat hold the finest marine life in the world.

Top things to do in Indonesia

The experiences worth building a trip around.

Borobudur at sunrise

Watch dawn break over the ninth-century monument’s five hundred Buddhas and bell-shaped stupas, with volcanoes on the horizon. Access to the upper terraces is ticketed and guided now, so book well ahead.

Mount Bromo at first light

Ride to the Penanjakan viewpoint before dawn to see Bromo, Batok and distant Semeru rise from a sea of mist. Jeeps and horses cover the hardest ground, which makes it possible without a serious hike.

Bali’s temples & rice terraces

Lakeside Pura Ulun Danu Bratan, clifftop Uluwatu at sunset, and the UNESCO-listed Jatiluwih terraces, where a thousand-year-old irrigation system still works. Hire a car and driver for the day.

Orangutans at Bukit Lawang

Walk into Gunung Leuser National Park in northern Sumatra for one of the last chances anywhere to see Sumatran orangutans in their own forest. Half-day walks suit those who would rather not trek hard.

The Komodo dragons

Take a boat from Labuan Bajo to Komodo and Rinca to see the world’s largest lizards with a ranger, then snorkel with manta rays and stop at Pink Beach. Day trips and multi-day cruises both work.

Snorkel the eastern reefs

Whether at Raja Ampat, Bunaken, the Gili Islands or Nusa Penida, Indonesia sits in the Coral Triangle and the reef life is unmatched. The Gilis and Menjangan are the gentlest options for casual snorkellers.

Best time to visit & getting there

When to go

April to October is the dry season and the best window for Bali, Java, Lombok and Komodo, with June to September the sunniest — and the busiest and dearest. May, September and October are the sweet spot: dry weather without peak prices.

From about November to March the northwest monsoon brings heavy afternoon downpours rather than all-day drizzle. Mornings are often clear, the rice fields are gloriously green, and February is usually the quietest and cheapest month of the year.

One important exception: Raja Ampat runs the opposite way and is at its best from October to April. See our month-by-month guide to the best time to visit Indonesia for weather, crowds and prices right through the year.

Getting there

Bali Ngurah Rai (DPS) is the main leisure gateway and Jakarta Soekarno-Hatta (CGK) the main hub; both connect through Singapore, Hong Kong, Doha, Dubai and Tokyo. From the United States expect 22 to 28 hours with one or two stops, so allow a full day at each end and one more for the jet lag.

Inside the country, fly rather than drive. Yogyakarta (YIA), Surabaya (SUB) for Bromo, Lombok (LOP), Labuan Bajo (LBJ) for Komodo and Medan (KNO) for Sumatra are all short hops, and the roads and ferries are slow.

Most visitors buy a visa on arrival, valid 30 days and extendable once; buying the electronic version before you fly is quicker at the airport. Everyone must also complete the free All Indonesia Arrival Card online within three days of arrival, and Bali charges a one-off tourist levy per person. Confirm the current rules with the official Indonesian immigration service before you travel.

Indonesia travel FAQ

When is the best time to visit Indonesia?

The dry season runs roughly from April to October, and June to September are the sunniest and busiest months in Bali and Java. The northwest monsoon brings heavy afternoon downpours from about November to March — hot, green and much cheaper. Raja Ampat in the far east runs the opposite way and is best from October to April, so the two cannot easily be combined in one trip.

What currency is used in Indonesia?

The Indonesian rupiah (IDR, symbol Rp). Prices carry a great many zeros — what a menu writes as “50k” means Rp50,000. Cards are widely accepted in hotels, malls and better restaurants; carry cash for warungs, markets, temples and small towns. Use ATMs inside bank branches or malls, and when a terminal offers to charge you in dollars, decline and pay in rupiah.

Do I need a visa to visit Indonesia?

Most visitors, including US, UK, Canadian, Australian and EU citizens, are not visa-free. They buy a visa on arrival, valid for 30 days and extendable once for a further 30. It can be bought online in advance as an e-VOA, which is quicker at the airport, or paid for on arrival. Every traveller must also complete the free All Indonesia Arrival Card online within three days of arriving, and Bali charges a separate one-off tourist levy. A fee increase has been announced but not yet enacted, so budget generously and confirm the current rules with the official Indonesian immigration service before you travel.

How many days do you need in Indonesia?

Plan on 12 to 14 days at the very least, and 16 to 18 is far more comfortable. The country is over 3,000 miles across and internal flights, not map distances, govern the itinerary. A good first trip is two islands only: Yogyakarta for Borobudur and Prambanan, then Bali for Ubud and a quiet beach. Resist adding a third island.

Is Indonesia safe for travellers?

Broadly, yes — the country is welcoming and the realistic risks are mundane rather than dramatic. Traffic is the genuine hazard: do not rent a scooter, and use hired cars with drivers or the Grab and Gojek apps instead. Watch for petty theft in crowded places, swim only between the flags on Bali’s south and west coasts, drink bottled water, and take comprehensive travel insurance that includes medical evacuation. Volcanic eruptions are routine and well monitored; their usual effect on visitors is a flight delay rather than danger.
