Best Time to Visit Norway

Best Time to Visit Norway

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Norway does not have one best season; it has two good ones that have almost nothing in common. Summer is the country at full stretch — every ferry running, every road open, the sun refusing to set. Winter is a different holiday altogether, built around snow, the aurora and the strange blue light of the polar night. What follows is the year set out month by month, with daylight and what is actually running treated as seriously as the temperature.

The short answer

Three good answers, depending on what you came for.

For the fjords & hiking

Late June to mid-August. Everything open, high roads clear, long light. Busiest and dearest.

For value & calm

May and September. Still long days, fewer people, lower rates. Some high ground shut.

For the aurora

Late September to late March, in the north. Several nights, not one, and no promises.

Month by month

Daylight, weather and what is actually running. Norway is long, so the north and the south are described separately where they differ.

January

Deep winter. Polar night ending in the far north around mid-month. Good aurora odds, reliable snow, cheap after the first week. Fjord touring is not the plan.

February

The best of the winter north: light returning, snow at its most dependable, aurora still strong. Half-term weeks are busy and priced accordingly.

March

Our pick for a winter trip. Long enough days to see the landscape, dark enough nights for the lights, and the snow still good. Skiing is at its best.

April

An awkward month. Winter activities winding down, summer ones not open, high roads still shut, and Easter crowds wherever there is snow. Oslo and Bergen are fine; the fjord tour is not.

May

Lovely and underrated. Waterfalls at full snowmelt, orchards in blossom in Hardanger, long light, modest prices. Midnight sun begins in the north late in the month. Some passes still closed.

June

Arguably the best all-round month. Everything opening, the midnight sun at its height, green mountains, and the school holidays not yet started for most of Europe.

July

Peak summer. Warmest, busiest, dearest; Norwegian holidays overlap with everyone else’s. Book fjord accommodation early or stay flexible and travel by rail.

August

Summer with the pressure easing after the middle of the month. Water warm enough to swim in the south, berries on the plateau, and the first proper darkness returning north by the end.

September

The connoisseur’s month. Autumn colour, clear air, quiet fjords, falling prices, and aurora possible in the north in the second half. Bergen is wet.

October

Shoulder tipping into winter. Dark drawing in fast, high roads closing, rain in the west. Good aurora, good value, little else running.

November

The quietest month, and the least rewarding for a first visit. Polar night begins in the far north late in the month. Cheap, dark and wet.

December

Short days, Christmas markets, and a particular northern charm if you want it. Oslo gets about six hours of light; Tromsø gets none. Prices spike over the holidays.

The light, which matters more than the weather

Most countries you plan around rain and temperature. Norway you plan around daylight, because the swing between midsummer and midwinter is extreme and it changes what a day can hold.

The midnight sun runs north of the Arctic Circle from roughly late May to late July, longer the further north you stand. It is a genuinely strange experience — walking at one in the morning in full daylight — and it is also the reason to take a sleep mask, because Norwegian hotel curtains are not always equal to it.

The polar night is the other end of the same fact: in Tromsø, from about the last week of November to the middle of January, the sun does not clear the horizon. There is still several hours of blue twilight around midday, and snow and the sea throw a surprising amount of light about, but it is not daylight.

In the south the extremes are milder but still large. Oslo runs from about six hours of light at the solstice in December to around nineteen in June, with a summer dusk that never quite finishes.

Planning around the northern lights

The aurora needs three things at the same time: a dark sky, solar activity and no cloud. You can control the first by choosing the season and getting away from town lights, and nothing at all about the other two. Anybody guaranteeing you a sighting is selling a tour, not a forecast.

What a sensible plan looks like: go between late September and late March; base yourself in the north, Tromsø, Alta or the Lofoten islands; allow three or four nights rather than one; and book the trip for things you will enjoy in daylight too, so a cloudy run is a disappointment rather than a wasted journey.

Norway uses the Norwegian krone (NOK, written kr), not the euro. Norway is not a member of the European Union, and euros are not accepted in shops. It is also one of the most nearly cashless countries in the world: cards and phones are taken everywhere, including on buses, in mountain huts and at market stalls, and many places no longer handle notes at all. You do not need to carry krone in cash. Wine and spirits are sold only through the state shops, Vinmonopolet, which close early and are shut on Sundays; beer is in supermarkets but stops being sold at a set hour in the evening. Tipping is not expected and never chased — rounding up a restaurant bill is plenty. Decline any card machine that offers to charge you in dollars; pay in krone and let your own bank convert.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS — the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years — is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today — nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Norway is the case readers get wrong most often. It is not in the European Union, but it is in the Schengen area, so everything above applies here in full — the border checks, the Entry/Exit System, and ETIAS when it comes. Flying Oslo to Copenhagen or Stockholm is an internal Schengen hop with no passport control. What Norway’s position outside the EU does change is customs: there are duty limits on what you may bring in, and the allowance for alcohol and tobacco is modest and genuinely enforced.

When to visit Norway — questions

What is the single best month to visit Norway?

If you are made to pick one, September. The fjord boats and the Bergen railway are still running a full timetable, the summer crowds and the cruise ships have thinned, the air is often clearer than in July, room rates have come down, the autumn colour is starting on the plateau, and by the second half of the month the nights are dark enough in the north for the aurora. The trade-off is that some high mountain roads and lodges close through the month, so September suits a trip built on the railway, the coast and the fjords rather than on high hiking.

When is the midnight sun, exactly?

North of the Arctic Circle the sun stays above the horizon continuously from roughly late May to late July, and the further north you go the longer the period: at the North Cape it runs about mid-May to the end of July, in Tromso roughly from the third week of May to the third week of July. South of the circle you do not get true midnight sun, but you do get a long twilight that never becomes properly dark - in Oslo in late June it is light enough to read outdoors well past eleven.

When is the best chance of the northern lights?

The dark half of the year, and the north. The practical window is late September to late March, with the equinox periods either side of it often cited as the most active. You need darkness, solar activity and a clear sky on the same night, which is why no honest guide promises a sighting. Base yourself in Tromso, Alta or the Lofoten islands, allow at least three or four nights, and get away from street lighting.

How dark does it actually get in winter?

Darker than most visitors expect. Oslo in late December gets about six hours between sunrise and sunset, and low sun for most of that. North of the Arctic Circle there is the polar night, when the sun does not clear the horizon at all: in Tromso that runs from around the last week of November to the middle of January. It is not pitch black - there are several hours of blue twilight in the middle of the day, and snow throws light around - but it is not daylight either, and it is worth deciding in advance whether you find that magical or miserable.

Is it worth visiting Norway in winter?

Yes, for a specific trip. Winter is the season for the aurora, dog sledding, whale watching off the northern coast, and cross-country skiing, which is close to a national pastime and easy to try near Oslo. Prices outside the Christmas and February half-term peaks are often better than summer. What winter is not good for is the classic fjord tour: many ferry routes drop to a skeleton timetable, the high roads shut, and some mountain hotels close entirely.

Which month is cheapest?

Late October and early November, and most of January after the New Year, are the quietest and generally the cheapest weeks of the year - but they are also the weeks with the least to do: too dark for the fjords, too early or too unsettled for reliable snow. The better value question is the shoulder: May and September give you most of what July gives you at a visible discount. Within summer, late August is usually a little cheaper than mid-July.

How bad is the rain in Bergen?

Real, and not a joke the locals mind. Bergen is one of the wettest cities in Europe, with rain on well over half the days of the year, and the wettest months are the autumn ones, September through December. It does not spoil the city - the place is built for it, the rain is usually soft rather than violent, and Bryggen in the wet is genuinely handsome - but it does mean you should go with a proper waterproof and build in an indoor afternoon rather than hoping.

When do the fjord ferries and mountain roads run?

The main fjord boats on the popular routes run year-round or near enough, with reduced winter timetables, and the Bergen railway runs every day of the year. The things that close are the high ones: the mountain passes over the plateau and the roads to places like Geiranger and Trollstigen are typically shut by snow from some point in October until May or even into June, and reopening dates vary by year and are never promised in advance. If a high road matters to your plan, check it close to the date rather than booking around it.
