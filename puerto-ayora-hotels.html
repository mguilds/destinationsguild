The Galápagos — Puerto Ayora, Santa Cruz Island

Where to Stay in the Galápagos — Puerto Ayora

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Puerto Ayora, on the island of Santa Cruz, is the one real town in the Galápagos: a working harbour with a fish market, a few hundred metres of waterfront, dive shops, restaurants and the Charles Darwin Research Station at the end of the road. It is also the base for the cheaper half of Galápagos travel — land-based island hopping, where you sleep in town and take day boats out, instead of living aboard a cruise. The town is small and flat, so this is less about districts than about the waterfront, the hill behind it, and the highlands with the tortoises in them.

Avenida Charles Darwin — the waterfront

The one street that matters, running along the harbour from the pier to the research station. The boat pier where every day trip leaves from is on it, so are most of the restaurants, the dive shops, the tour agencies and the fish market where pelicans and a sea lion queue up beside the customers. Staying on or just off it means walking to everything, and it is flat the whole way. It is also the noisiest part of a very quiet town, which here means music until about eleven rather than anything serious.

Up the hill behind town — quieter and better value

Puerto Ayora rises gently away from the water, and three or four streets up the prices drop while the walk to the pier stays under ten minutes. These are the small family-run hotels and guesthouses, often with a roof terrace and a view over the bay, and they are where most of the value in the town sits. The only cost is the climb back up at the end of the day, at sea level and in the heat.

The eastern end — towards the research station

Past the end of the main strip the road continues towards the Charles Darwin Research Station, and the few places out this way are calmer, greener and about a ten-minute walk from the harbour. It is also the direction of the path to Tortuga Bay, a long paved walk out to one of the great beaches of the Pacific. A good choice for a quiet week; a slightly inconvenient one if you are catching early boats every morning.

The highlands — if you want tortoises outside the window

Inland and uphill from the town, the middle of Santa Cruz is farmland, cloud forest and the El Chato reserve, where giant tortoises live wild in the fields. A handful of lodges and farm stays sit up here, cooler and wetter than the coast, where tortoises genuinely do wander past. You are twenty to thirty minutes from the pier by taxi, which means an early day boat needs planning, so this works best as one or two nights rather than a base.

What to know before you book

Check the current island conditions before you travel. The UK Foreign Office records that flooding on Santa Cruz prompted an emergency declaration on 31 October 2026. That is recent enough to matter and we cannot tell you from here what the town looks like on the day you arrive, so write to your hotel and ask — they will know, and they will tell you.

Everything on an island costs what shipping costs. Food, drink, laundry and rooms are all dearer than on the mainland, because almost all of it arrives by boat from Guayaquil. That is not a hotel overcharging you; it is the freight.

Water and power are not the mainland’s. Tap water is not for drinking, most hotels supply filtered water, and the municipal supply is brackish in places — ask about drinking water and about hot water, which not every budget room has. Air conditioning is worth confirming in the warm season from December to May.

Book the first and last nights carefully, not the middle. Day boats are bought in town, often the evening before, and there is no advantage in fixing them from home. Beds, on the other hand, are limited in July, August and the last two weeks of December, and the arrival and departure days are the ones you cannot move.

Two of the best things here are free and need no boat. The walk to Tortuga Bay is a couple of flat paved kilometres from the edge of town, and the Charles Darwin Research Station is at the other end of the waterfront. Neither needs a tour, and both are worth a morning.

The park rules are not decoration. Stay on the marked trails, keep the regulation distance from the animals even when they do not move, never feed anything, and take a guide where one is required. The reason the wildlife still behaves this way is that the rules have been kept for sixty years.

Puerto Ayora — questions

Should I stay in Puerto Ayora or take a cruise?

It depends on what you want and what you can spend. A live-aboard cruise reaches the far western and northern islands that no day boat can get to, and if seeing the remote visitor sites is the point of the trip, nothing else does it. Land-based island hopping from Puerto Ayora costs a fraction of that, puts you in a town with restaurants, a fish market and a tortoise reserve nearby, lets you choose each day as it comes, and still gets you to Santa Fe, North Seymour, Bartolome, Isabela and Floreana on day boats. For most first-time visitors on a normal budget, the town is the sensible answer, and that is what this guide is about.

Which part of Puerto Ayora should I stay in?

Avenida Charles Darwin, the waterfront street, if you want to be in the middle of it - the harbour, the restaurants, the dive shops and the boat pier are all on it, and it is flat. The streets up the hill behind it are quieter and cheaper for the sake of a short climb, and the climb is at sea level so it is only a hill, not an altitude problem. Nearer the Charles Darwin Research Station at the eastern end is calmer again, and a ten-minute walk from the pier.

How do I get from the airport to Puerto Ayora?

It is a journey rather than a transfer, and knowing that in advance saves a bad first hour. Flights for Santa Cruz land at Baltra, which is a separate island: you clear the park formalities, take a bus to the Itabaca Channel, cross on a small ferry, then take a bus or taxi for about forty minutes down the length of Santa Cruz to the town. Allow an hour and a half to two hours in total, and the same in reverse - which matters on your departure day, because the morning flights leave early.

What does it cost to enter the Galapagos?

There are two separate charges and we are not going to print figures we cannot confirm on an official source. A transit control card must be bought at your departure airport in Quito or Guayaquil before you check in for the island flight, and a national park entrance fee is payable on arrival in the islands. Both are set by the Ecuadorian authorities and were last revised in 2024. Budget for both, pay only at the official desks, keep the receipts, and confirm the current amounts before you fly. Your luggage is also inspected for plant and animal material on the way in, which is a real biosecurity check rather than a formality.

Is island hopping by boat rough?

Often, yes, and it is worth being honest about. The inter-island speedboats are open, fast and take two hours or more in open Pacific water, and in the cool season from June to November the crossings can be genuinely uncomfortable. Sit at the back and in the middle, take something for seasickness before you board rather than after, and keep a dry bag for anything that must not get wet. The UK Foreign Office notes that there have been several serious accidents in the islands involving tour company boats and smaller vessels, and advises choosing reputable operators and checking for life vests before boarding. That advice is worth following to the letter.
