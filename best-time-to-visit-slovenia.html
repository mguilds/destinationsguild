Slovenia — when to go

Best Time to Visit Slovenia

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Slovenia packs three climates into an area smaller than Wales: a sub-Mediterranean coast, a continental middle and east, and the Julian Alps in the north-west where winter lasts months longer. That is why a single answer to “when should I go” is useless here and why this page is written region by region as well as month by month. The headline, if you want only that: June and September are the two best months for almost every kind of trip, February is the month for snow, and November is the one to avoid.

The short answer

Our pick: June, or September

Both give you warm days without August’s heat and crowds, swimmable lakes and sea, every mountain path and cave open, and prices below the peak. September edges it if you care about wine — the Štajerska vintage — or about having Bled to yourself in the morning. June edges it for long evenings and high-mountain snowmelt.

For the mountains: late June to September

The high paths in Triglav National Park hold snow well into the summer and the huts run a season roughly from late June to late September. The Vršič pass road is shut by snow in winter and clears in spring. If a serious walk is the point of the trip, come inside that window and check hut bookings and conditions before you commit.

For skiing: January to early March

February is the most reliable month. Slovenian resorts are low by Alpine standards, so snow cover varies and snowmaking matters; Kranjska Gora and Vogel above Lake Bohinj are the best known, and Pohorje above Maribor is the family option. Check conditions rather than the calendar, and expect school-holiday crowds in mid-February.

Avoid, if you can choose: November

The one month we would steer a first-time visitor away from. The autumn colour has gone, the snow has not arrived, the Ljubljana basin sits under fog, mountain huts and lake businesses are shut between seasons and the ski lifts are not yet turning. It is cheap, and the cities carry on, but it is Slovenia at its least rewarding.

Month by month

What each month is actually like across the country, written for a visitor rather than for a weather chart.

January

Cold, grey and often foggy in the Ljubljana basin, properly wintry in the mountains, and the cheapest month in the cities. This is the heart of the ski season: Kranjska Gora, Vogel and Pohorje are all working, and the lakes look their best under snow. Days are short, some lakeside and mountain businesses are closed, and the high passes are shut. A good month for thermal spas, which is what Slovenes themselves do with it.

February

The strongest winter month. Snow cover is at its most reliable, the days are lengthening and the light in the mountains is excellent. Expect crowds and higher rates at the resorts during the school holidays of several countries in the middle of the month. In the towns it is quiet and inexpensive. Ptuj’s Kurentovanje carnival usually falls now — it moves with Easter, so check the date for your year.

March

A month of transition and a gamble. Early March can still be full winter in the mountains with bright spring skiing; by the end the valleys are green, the cities have shaken off the fog and the first terraces are out. Lower ski areas lose their cover. Lake Bled is beautiful and almost empty. Rates stay low. A reasonable month for cities and spas, an unreliable one for anything that depends on snow.

April

Spring proper in the lowlands: blossom, green hills, warm afternoons and cold mornings, with the high mountains still deep in snow and their huts closed. Ljubljana and the coast are lovely and uncrowded. Easter is a real holiday here with closures on the Sunday and Monday, and the Day of Uprising Against Occupation falls on the twenty-seventh. The Vršič pass is usually still shut; check before planning a drive over it.

May

One of the best months and the least appreciated. The country is at its greenest, waterfalls and rivers are full with snowmelt, the lower walking is superb, and the caves and castles are open with no queues. Rain is likelier than in high summer. The first two days of May are a public holiday and a traditional short break, so the coast and the lakes fill for that weekend. Sea swimming is still cold.

June

Our pick, with September. Long light evenings, warm but not oppressive days, the lakes warming up and the sea swimmable from about the middle of the month, and the high paths opening as the snow retreats. Everything is running and nothing is yet crowded. Statehood Day on the twenty-fifth is a holiday, and Maribor’s Lent festival begins at the end of the month, which is the liveliest the second city gets.

July

Hot and busy. The Ljubljana basin and the east regularly pass thirty degrees Celsius, the coast is at its fullest with Italian and Austrian holidaymakers, and Bled’s shore path is shoulder to shoulder in the middle of the day. Against that: every mountain hut is open, the high-alpine season is at its best, the rivers are perfect for rafting and the festival calendar is at its fullest. Book rooms well ahead and insist on air conditioning in the lowlands.

August

The busiest and most expensive month, and the one with the most closed shutters in the cities — Ljubljana and Maribor empty of residents while the lakes and the coast overflow. The Assumption, on the fifteenth, is a major holiday across the region and the peak of the peak. The mountains are magnificent and crowded; thunderstorms build on hot afternoons, which matters if you are high up. Come in August only if school holidays decide it for you.

September

Our other pick, and for many travellers the best month of the year here. The heat eases, the crowds thin after the first week, the sea stays warm into October, the mountain huts are still open and the light turns golden. The Štajerska and Primorska vintages are under way, which makes the wine country genuinely interesting rather than merely pretty. Rates drop. Nothing much has closed yet.

October

Beautiful, cool and quiet. The beech forests on Pohorje and around the lakes turn, the walking at middle altitude is at its finest, and the cities are at their most liveable. High huts close through the month and the first snow falls on the tops; the Vršič pass can shut without much warning. Sea swimming ends. All Saints’ Day on the first of November, and Reformation Day on the thirty-first of October, close things and fill the cemeteries with candles.

November

The month to avoid if you have a choice. Short, damp, grey days, persistent fog in the Ljubljana basin, the colour gone and the snow not yet arrived, with mountain huts and a good many lakeside and coastal businesses closed between seasons and the lifts not yet turning. The cities work perfectly well and are at their cheapest, and the thermal spas are a sound idea. It is not a month for a first impression.

December

Two different Decembers. Ljubljana’s Christmas market and riverside lights run through the month and are among the prettiest in central Europe, with the city busier and dearer than at any other point in the winter. The ski resorts open as snow allows, usually around the middle of the month, and fill for the holidays. Christmas Day, and Independence and Unity Day on the twenty-sixth, close almost everything. Expect fog in the lowlands and short days throughout.

By region

The same year, seen from five different parts of a small country.

Ljubljana and the centre

A city, so it works all year. Best in May, June, September and early October for terrace weather, and in the first three weeks of December for the market. Hot and half-empty in August, foggy and cheap from November to February.

The lakes and the Julian Alps

Late June to September for walking, swimming and the high huts; January and February for snow. May is lovely low down while the tops are still shut. Bled is at its best early in the morning in any season, which is the argument for sleeping there.

The coast

Mid-June to early October for swimming, with June and September much the most pleasant. July and August are hot and crowded. From November to March Piran is a quiet, windy, inexpensive little town with many restaurants closed.

The Karst and the caves

All year. The show caves hold a steady temperature of around ten degrees Celsius whatever the weather outside, so they are a reliable wet-day or hot-day answer — take a jacket in August. Spring and autumn are the best above ground.

The east: Štajerska, Prekmurje and the spas

September and October for the vintage and the farm guesthouses, May and June for the hills. The thermal spas are a year-round business and are at their most appealing in the cold, foggy months when there is little else to do out here.

Holidays and closures

Slovenia keeps a long list of public holidays and they close shops, banks and many restaurants, so they are worth knowing before you book. The ones that most often catch visitors out are New Year on the first and second of January; Prešeren Day, the culture holiday, on the eighth of February; Easter Sunday and Monday; the Day of Uprising Against Occupation on the twenty-seventh of April, which together with Labour Day on the first and second of May makes a traditional week of travel; Statehood Day on the twenty-fifth of June; the Assumption on the fifteenth of August, which is the peak of the coastal season; Reformation Day on the thirty-first of October and All Saints’ Day on the first of November, which fall together and fill the cemeteries with candles; and Christmas Day with Independence and Unity Day on the twenty-sixth of December. Easter and the carnival at Ptuj move each year with the church calendar, so check the dates for the year you are travelling. Museums commonly close on Mondays, many high mountain huts run only from roughly late June to late September, and lakeside and beach businesses keep short seasons — if a particular cave, hut, lift or restaurant matters to your plans, confirm its opening on its own website rather than trusting a season you read somewhere, including here.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Slovenia itself is straightforward: in the European Union since 2004, in the Schengen area since the end of 2007 and on the euro since the beginning of that year. An arrival from another Schengen country has no passport control at all, and an arrival from outside it is a single check at the airport you land in. The land borders with Italy, Austria and Hungary are internal Schengen crossings and are often barely marked, as is the Croatian border since Croatia joined at the start of 2023 — although checks are sometimes reintroduced temporarily there and the summer queues towards the Croatian coast can be long. Carry your passport whenever you cross. Slovenian motorways require a vignette, now bought electronically, so check whether a hire car already has one.

When to visit Slovenia — questions

What is the single best month to visit Slovenia?

June or September, and we would not argue hard with anyone who picked either. Both give warm weather, swimmable water, open mountain paths, full opening hours and prices below the August peak. Choose September if you want the wine harvest, emptier mornings at Bled and still-warm sea; choose June if you want the longest evenings, the fullest rivers and waterfalls, and the alpine flowers. If you can only travel in high summer, go into the mountains rather than to the coast.

How many days does Slovenia need?

Five to seven nights covers the country properly, because it is small and nothing is far: two or three nights in Ljubljana with day trips to the Karst caves, two at Bled or Bohinj for the mountains, and two on the coast if the Adriatic appeals. Four nights is enough for Ljubljana and the lakes alone. Ten days lets you add the Soca valley, the east and the wine country without rushing. Many visitors never change hotel at all and drive out from Ljubljana each day, which works.

Is Slovenia a winter destination?

It is, but not a headline one. The skiing is real and well suited to families and learners rather than to people chasing big Alpine verticals — the mountains are lower here, so cover varies and snowmaking matters. What Slovenia does unusually well in winter is the combination of a modest ski day, a thermal spa and a city with a Christmas market, all within an hour or two of each other. Check snow conditions rather than the calendar before booking a ski trip.

When is it cheapest?

January, February and March in the cities and on the coast; November everywhere. The expensive weeks are the second half of July and the whole of August on the coast and at the lakes, the mid-February school holidays and the Christmas and New Year period at the resorts, and the first three weeks of December in Ljubljana. Shoulder season here is unusually good value because the weather is still reliable in May, June, September and early October.

Does the weather really differ that much across such a small country?

More than its size suggests, because it has three climates meeting inside it. The coast is sub-Mediterranean, mild and dry in winter and hot in summer. The centre and east are continental, with hot summers, cold foggy winters and real thunderstorms. The north-west is Alpine, with heavy snow, a short summer and weather that changes within an hour. On the same April day you can have blossom in Piran and a metre of snow on Vršič, which is worth packing for.
