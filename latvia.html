Latvia

The Latvia Travel Guide

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Latvia is the middle of the three Baltic states and the one with the great city in it. Riga stands where the Daugava meets the Gulf of Riga, and it is far larger and grander than a country of under two million people has any business owning — a medieval core of guild halls and brick gables, and then street after street of Art Nouveau apartment blocks built in a thirty-year building boom, one of the densest collections of that architecture anywhere in Europe. Beyond the capital the country divides into four old provinces: the pine-and-sand coast at Jurmala and the Vidzeme hills and river valleys to the north-east, the Kurzeme ports and beaches to the west, the lake country of Latgale in the south-east, and Zemgale’s farmland in between. More than half of Latvia is forest, the coast runs for about five hundred kilometres, and nothing in the country is more than a long afternoon’s drive from anything else.

Why visit Latvia

Riga is a serious city, not a stop

The historic centre of Riga has been a UNESCO World Heritage site since 1997, and the citation is not only about the medieval part. Riga grew enormously rich in the decades before the First World War, and what it spent the money on was apartment buildings: whole boulevards of them, in an Art Nouveau that runs from restrained to frankly peculiar. Walk Alberta iela and Elizabetes iela with your head up and you will see faces, beasts, peacocks and screaming masks on the facades. Nowhere else has quite this much of it in quite this small a space.

The beach is half an hour from the capital

Jurmala is a twenty-kilometre strip of white sand, dune and pine between the Gulf of Riga and the Lielupe river, with wooden villas and sanatoria scattered through the trees behind it. The commuter train from Riga takes about half an hour and runs often. It is a proper seaside resort rather than a day-trip beach, it has been one since the nineteenth century, and the water is shallow, calm and — this being the Baltic — bracing.

More than half the country is forest

Latvia is one of the most wooded countries in Europe, and the woods are not scenery kept at a distance: they come up to the edge of the towns, and the national habit of going into them for berries and mushrooms in late summer is universal and unselfconscious. Add about twelve thousand rivers and streams, several thousand lakes, raised bogs with boardwalks across them, and storks nesting on poles in the fields, and you have a country that is unusually easy to be outdoors in.

It is not crowded, and it is not dear

Riga takes a fraction of the visitors that Prague or Amsterdam take, and prices across the country sit at the lower end of the euro area. In practice that means a good room in a beautiful city for less than you would pay in western Europe, restaurants that are not braced against tourists, and the ordinary pleasure of walking through a famous old town in October and having a square largely to yourself.

Singing is the national history

Latvia has a nationwide Song and Dance Celebration that brings tens of thousands of singers and dancers to Riga about every five years, a tradition going back to 1873 and recognised by UNESCO as intangible cultural heritage. It is not folklore for visitors. When the Soviet Union came apart, the Baltic independence movement was called the Singing Revolution, and in August 1989 about two million people joined hands in a human chain from Tallinn through Riga to Vilnius. Latvians will tell you that they sang their country back, and they are not being poetic.

The twentieth century is visible everywhere

Latvia was independent between the wars, occupied by the Soviet Union, then Germany, then the Soviet Union again, and independent once more from 1991. You see it in the Stalin-era tower of the Academy of Sciences, in the abandoned naval forts on the beach at Liepaja, in the Soviet bobsleigh track at Sigulda still in use for World Cup races, and in the Corner House in Riga where the KGB worked and which is now open to walk through. It is recent, it is unhealed, and the country does not hide it.

Latvia at a glance

Capital

Riga, on both banks of the Daugava about fifteen kilometres from its mouth, with roughly a third of the country’s people in it and the largest population of any city in the Baltic states. The historic centre is a UNESCO World Heritage site. The airport sits about ten kilometres west of the middle of town and is the main hub for the region.

Money

The euro, which replaced the lats on 1 January 2014. Cards and contactless are taken almost everywhere a visitor goes, including on trains, trams and in the market halls. Prices sit at the lower end of the euro area, with Riga’s Old Town the one place that charges what a tourist will pay.

Language

Latvian only, as the official language — a Baltic language, sister to Lithuanian and relative of nothing else alive. Russian is widely spoken and is a live political question rather than a convenience, so a visitor should not reach for it first. English is common in Riga and in tourism generally, and thinner in small towns.

Borders

In the European Union since May 2004, in the Schengen area since December 2007 and in the euro since 2014, so arrival from elsewhere in Schengen involves no check at all. The eastern border with Russia and the south-eastern one with Belarus are external borders of the European Union, and not places to turn up casually.

Getting about

Driving is on the right. Riga has trams, trolleybuses and buses; the commuter railway reaches Jurmala, Sigulda and Cesis easily and is the best way to see the near country. Long-distance coaches are thorough and cheap and reach the small towns the trains do not. A car earns its keep in Kurzeme and Latgale.

Shape of it

About sixty-four and a half thousand square kilometres, most of it low, flat and wooded, with the highest point a hill of just over three hundred metres. Fewer than two million people live here and the number has been falling for a generation. It is a parliamentary republic whose president is elected by the Saeima rather than by the voters; Edgars Rinkevics has held the office since July 2023.

The regions

Four historical provinces and the capital, and you can cross the whole country in a long afternoon.

Riga and the Gulf coast

The capital on the Daugava, and the resort coast running west from it along the Gulf of Riga. This is where almost every visitor stays, and with the commuter railway reaching the beach in half an hour and the Gauja valley in an hour, it is a perfectly sensible base for most of a trip.

Vidzeme: the Gauja valley and the north-east

The largest province and the hilliest, with the Gauja cutting a wooded sandstone valley through the middle of it inside Gauja National Park. Castles, caves, a cable car across the gorge and the best autumn colour in the country. Sigulda is the base; Cesis, half an hour further on the same railway, has the finest small old town in Latvia.

Kurzeme: the west, the ports and the open sea

The western province, facing the open Baltic rather than the sheltered gulf, with long empty beaches, two working ports and the strangest military leftovers in the country. Liepaja is the one to stay in: a city of amber, rock music and abandoned forts, and a European Capital of Culture in 2027, so check what is on before you fix your dates.

Latgale and Zemgale: the lakes and the farmland

The south-east is Latgale, the lake district: several thousand lakes, the basilica at Aglona, a Catholic rather than Lutheran tradition, and Daugavpils, the second city, with a huge nineteenth-century fortress and an art centre devoted to Mark Rothko, who was born there. Zemgale, between Latgale and the sea, is flat grain country with the baroque palace at Rundale in the middle of it.

Things to do in Latvia

Walk the Art Nouveau streets with your head up

Start at the corner of Alberta iela and Elizabetes iela and simply look upwards. Riga’s pre-war building boom left whole blocks of Jugendstil apartment houses, many by Mikhail Eisenstein, loaded with masks, sphinxes, peacocks and plasterwork that has no business being on a block of flats. There is a museum inside one of them if you want the interiors too. An hour on foot, no ticket, and the single best thing in the city.

Climb St Peter’s and then eat in the market

The spire of St Peter’s Church has a lift to a gallery with the whole of Riga laid out under it — the red roofs, the river, the market halls and the Soviet tower across the water. Come down and walk ten minutes to the Central Market, where the pavilions include a former Zeppelin hangar, and eat smoked fish, rye bread, curd and whatever is being pickled that month.

Take the train to the beach

The Jurmala line runs out of Riga Central several times an hour and puts you on white sand in about half an hour. Get off at Majori for the busy middle and the pedestrian street, or a stop or two either side for emptier sand and the wooden villas in the pines. Go to the end of the line for Kemeri, where a boardwalk crosses a raised bog and the old sulphur spa stands half ruined in the trees.

Cross the Gauja gorge at Sigulda

A small cable car hangs across the valley between Sigulda and Krimulda, forty metres above the river, and it is the best three minutes in Vidzeme. On either side are the ruins of the Livonian Order’s castle, the red-brick tower at Turaida, and Gutmanis Cave, the largest in the Baltics, its sandstone walls carved with graffiti going back centuries. In early October the whole valley turns and the trains out from Riga fill with people going to look at it.

See what the Soviet navy left at Liepaja

North of the canal at Liepaja is Karosta, built as a Russian imperial naval port and then sealed off as a Soviet military district for decades. The Northern Forts sit broken on the beach where they were blown up and the sea has been working on them ever since; the garrison prison is open and is a genuinely bleak hour; St Nicholas Naval Cathedral still stands in the middle of it. Nothing else in Latvia explains the last century so quickly.

Stand on a raised bog

Latvia is full of raised bogs — deep peat domes with their own small lakes, stunted pines and a horizon that looks like nowhere else in Europe — and the good ones have boardwalks and a tower. Kemeri at the end of the Jurmala line is the easiest to reach, and the Great Kemeri Bog boardwalk is an hour’s loop. Go early, when the mist is still sitting on the pools.

When to go, and what it costs

Latvia has four real seasons and no month that is unusable, but they are not equal. Late May to early July is the prize: long light, lilac and then linden in flower, the beach warming up and the midsummer nights barely going dark at all. July and August are the warmest, busiest and best for swimming, and the only time Jurmala is genuinely full. September and early October are the country at its best looking — the Gauja valley turns, the light goes long and gold, and the crowds have gone. November to March is cold, often snowy and dark by mid-afternoon, which sounds like a reason to stay away and is not: Riga under snow with the Christmas market open in the Dome Square is a genuinely lovely thing, and it is the cheapest time to be here. The one date to know is Jani, midsummer on 23 and 24 June, when the whole country leaves town for the countryside; it is wonderful if you are invited to one and awkward if you expected the city to be working.

Fishing here

Fishing in Latvia means the Gauja and the Salaca for sea trout and salmon, the Daugava and the Lielupe for pike, perch and bream, the Latgale lakes in the south-east, and the Baltic coast itself, where ice fishing is a winter institution. An angling card is required, the salmon and sea-trout rivers carry extra restrictions and seasons, and some stretches are leased, so it is best arranged locally rather than puzzled out from abroad. Travelling in your later years

Riga is flat and the trams are frequent

Unlike most beautiful old European cities, Riga is not built on a hill. The Old Town, the boulevard ring and the Art Nouveau streets are all level walking, the distances between them are short, and trams and trolleybuses cover the rest of the centre at a few minutes’ interval. The cobbles in the Old Town are the only real obstacle, and they are uneven rather than steep.

The train does the work

Jurmala, Sigulda and Cesis are all on the railway out of Riga, the journeys are short, the carriages are level with the platform at the main stations and you do not need to drive, park or navigate. For anybody who would rather not take a hire car onto unfamiliar roads, this is a country where you genuinely do not have to.

Jurmala was built as a place to recuperate

The resort grew up in the nineteenth century around sea air, pine woods and sulphur water, and it has been in the business of looking after people ever since; several of its hotels still run spa and treatment programmes rather than simply having a pool. The sand is flat and firm, the water is shallow a long way out, and the pine paths behind the dunes are level and shaded.

Where the ground is difficult

Gutmanis Cave and the Gauja valley paths involve long wooden staircases down the gorge and back up; the Sigulda castle ruins are uneven; the Liepaja forts are broken concrete on sand and genuinely unsafe in the wrong shoes; and the bog boardwalks, while flat, are narrow and have no handrail. None of it rules the country out, and the cable car at Sigulda gives you the valley without the stairs, but it is worth choosing deliberately.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Latvia itself is straightforward: in the European Union since May 2004, in the Schengen area since December 2007 and in the euro since 2014. Arriving from anywhere else in Schengen there is no check at all, and Riga’s airport is the main hub for the three Baltic states, so most long-haul routes come through it. Coaches between Tallinn, Riga and Vilnius cross the internal borders without stopping.

One practical point. Latvia’s eastern border with Russia and its south-eastern border with Belarus are external borders of the European Union, and crossing them is not a casual matter for a visitor. If your map shows a tempting shortcut through either, ignore it.

Latvia — questions

How many days do I need in Latvia?

Three days is enough for Riga, and Riga is what most people come for. Five gets you the capital plus Jurmala and the Gauja valley, both of which are inside an hour of the city by train. A week lets you add the Kurzeme coast at Liepaja or Ventspils, or the lake country of Latgale in the south-east, without hurrying. Latvia also joins up neatly with its neighbours: Tallinn and Vilnius are each a long half-day away by coach, and a fortnight covering all three Baltic capitals is one of the better value trips in Europe.

Is Latvia expensive?

It is among the cheaper countries in the euro area, and noticeably cheaper than the Nordic capitals a short flight away. Riga is the dearest part of it, and within Riga the Old Town is dearer than anywhere else for both rooms and restaurant meals, often for no better a room. Move one street out of the tourist core, or across into the Quiet Centre, and the same money goes further. We do not print figures because they move, but the pattern is reliable: the Old Town carries a premium, the rest of the city does not, and the countryside is cheaper again.

What language should I speak?

Latvian is the only official language, and it is a Baltic language, related to Lithuanian and to nothing else living. Russian is widely spoken, especially in Riga, Daugavpils and among older people, but it is a sensitive subject rather than a neutral convenience, and a visitor is better off not assuming it. English is widely spoken in Riga and in anything to do with tourism, and well understood by younger people everywhere. Nobody expects a visitor to attempt Latvian, though labdien for good day and paldies for thank you are always well received.

Do I need a car?

Not for Riga, which is compact and has trams, trolleybuses and buses, and not for Jurmala or Sigulda, which are both on the commuter railway out of Riga and easy to reach without one. For the Kurzeme coast, the Latgale lakes or any serious wandering between small towns, a car makes a real difference, because the bus network is thorough but slow and the trains run on a few main lines only. Driving is on the right, the main roads are good, and country roads can turn to gravel without warning.

Where exactly is Latvia, and what is it next to?

Latvia is the middle of the three Baltic states, on the eastern shore of the Baltic Sea, with Estonia to the north, Lithuania to the south, Russia to the east and Belarus to the south-east. It has around five hundred kilometres of coast, most of it on the Gulf of Riga and the open Baltic, and the Daugava runs across it to the sea at Riga. It is a little over sixty-four thousand square kilometres, more than half of it forest, and home to fewer than two million people.

Is it a good trip for a first visit to Europe?

It is a good trip for a second or third one. Riga is beautiful, safe, easy to walk and far less crowded than the obvious European capitals, and the cost of being there is modest. What it does not have is the familiarity that makes a first trip easy: the language is unlike anything most visitors have met, the alphabet soup of Baltic history takes a little reading, and outside Riga and the resorts English thins out. Pair it with Estonia and Lithuania and it becomes an unusually rewarding fortnight.

What is the weather actually like?

Four real seasons, with a maritime edge on the coast and a continental one inland. Summers are mild and long-lit rather than hot, and midsummer nights barely go properly dark. Autumn is the country at its best looking and its wettest. Winter is cold, grey and genuinely snowy, with the sea freezing at the edges in a hard year and the light gone by mid-afternoon. Spring arrives late and quickly. Month by month detail is in our Best Time to Visit Latvia guide.
