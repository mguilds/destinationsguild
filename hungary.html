Hungary

Hungary Travel Guide

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Hungary is a country of about nine and a half million people in the Carpathian Basin, the broad bowl of plain and low hills where the Danube turns south in the middle of Europe. Two things shape a trip here. The first is Budapest, which is so much larger and grander than anywhere else in the country that most visitors never leave it — a mistake, but an understandable one. The second is water: more than a thousand thermal springs, a bathing culture that runs from Roman stones through Ottoman domes to great Edwardian palaces of hot water, and the shallow inland sea of Lake Balaton. Add some of the most interesting wine in Europe, a language related to almost nothing around it, and prices that still undercut Vienna an hour up the line, and you have a country that repays more days than people give it.

Why visit Hungary

A small country with one enormous capital, a bathing culture nobody else has, and wine that keeps surprising people.

Budapest is a great city, cheaply

Two old towns facing each other across the Danube, a parliament built like a cathedral, boulevards on the Paris model and the oldest underground railway on the European continent — all at prices well below Vienna an hour upstream.

The water is the point

More than a thousand thermal springs, and a public bathing habit that runs unbroken from Roman stones through Ottoman domes to great Edwardian halls. You can swim outdoors in hot water in February, which is not a thing most countries offer.

The wine is properly interesting

Tokaji, the sweet wine that European courts drank before Bordeaux was fashionable, and the dry whites alongside it; the reds of Villány and Szekszárd in the warm south; and the volcanic whites above Lake Balaton. Most of it never leaves the country.

It is small and easy

Nothing in this guide is more than about three and a half hours from Budapest by train, fares are low, and the country is flat enough that a day trip really is a day trip. You can see a great deal from one hotel room.

Hungary at a glance

Capital

Budapest, on the Danube in the north of the country — hilly Buda on the west bank, flat Pest on the east, joined into one city in 1873 and home to roughly a fifth of all Hungarians.

Money

The Hungarian forint (HUF, written Ft). Not the euro, and no date set for joining it. Cards and phones are taken nearly everywhere.

Language

Hungarian, which is not related to the languages of any neighbouring country. English is widely spoken in Budapest and among younger people elsewhere; German is often the better second language in the west.

Borders

In the European Union since 2004 and in the Schengen area since the end of 2007. Landlocked, with seven neighbours, of which Serbia and Ukraine are external Schengen frontiers.

Getting about

Trains radiate from Budapest and are cheap and comfortable on the main lines. Buses fill the gaps. A car is worth having only for the wine villages, the small Balaton towns and the plain.

Shape of it

Small, flat and warm in summer. The Danube cuts it north to south; Lake Balaton, the largest lake in central Europe, sits in the west; the Great Plain runs east to the Romanian border.

The regions

Five parts of the country hold everything most visitors come for, and all five are an easy train ride from the capital.

Budapest & the Danube Bend

The capital and the stretch of river just above it, where the Danube turns sharply south between wooded hills. Szentendre is a riverside town of painted houses half an hour out; Esztergom, further up, has the largest church in the country on a bluff over the water, with Slovakia on the far bank.

Lake Balaton

The shallow inland sea of western Hungary, seventy-odd kilometres long and warm enough to swim in from June to September. The south shore is flat, sandy and busy; the north shore has the volcanic hills, the vineyards and the Tihany peninsula; and Hévíz, just inland, is a thermal lake you can swim in all year round.

The North — Eger, Tokaj and the hills

Hungary’s only real hill country, running north-east towards Slovakia. Eger is a baroque town with a castle that held out against the Ottomans and a valley of wine cellars on its edge; Tokaj, further east where two rivers meet, gives its name to the sweet wine and to a whole UNESCO-listed landscape of terraced vineyards.

The Great Plain — Debrecen and the puszta

The east: enormous, flat, and the part of Hungary that looks least like anywhere else. Debrecen is the second city and the historic capital of Hungarian Protestantism; the Hortobágy grassland beyond it is a national park and a UNESCO site, with grey cattle, horse herds and a famous nine-arched bridge. Szeged, in the far south, was rebuilt in one piece after the flood of 1879.

Transdanubia — Pécs, Villány and the west

Everything west of the Danube below the lake. Pécs is the warmest city in the country, with Roman Christian tombs, an Ottoman mosque that became a church and the Villány red-wine villages half an hour south. Győr and Sopron, up near the Austrian border, are baroque market towns that most visitors pass without stopping.

Things to do in Hungary

A morning in the thermal baths

Széchenyi in Budapest’s City Park is the great yellow one, outdoors all year with chess players in the steam. Gellért is the Art Nouveau showpiece. Rudas and Király are small Ottoman bath houses with domed roofs and shafts of light, and feel like something much older than a swim.

The Danube at night

The Parliament lit from the water, the Chain Bridge, the castle on its hill. The whole river frontage is a UNESCO site. A cheap public ferry or an ordinary tram along the Pest embankment will show you as much as a tour boat.

Eger castle and the wine valley

The fortress that held off an Ottoman army in 1552, a story every Hungarian child knows, and below the town the Szépasszony-völgy — a horseshoe of small cellars cut into the hillside where the growers pour their own wine.

The Great Market Hall

Budapest’s cast-iron market of 1897, three storeys of paprika, sausage, pickles and lace. Go in the morning for the ground floor and the food rather than at lunchtime for the upstairs stalls.

Tokaj and the vineyards

The hills where sweet Tokaji was made centuries before the French classified anything, now a UNESCO landscape. The cellars are long, cold and furred with black mould, which is exactly as it should be.

Balaton in summer

Swim off the south shore, take the ferry across to Tihany and its abbey on the headland, and drink the volcanic white wine of Badacsony with your feet in the water. July and August are crowded and that is rather the point.

Best time to visit & getting there

Hungary has a continental climate, which in plain terms means hot summers, cold winters and two short, lovely shoulder seasons. Late spring, from the middle of May to the end of June, is our first choice: long days, warm afternoons without the August heat, and the cities not yet full. September is the other one, and arguably better — the weather holds, Lake Balaton is at its warmest just as the crowds leave, and the grape harvest runs through the month in every wine region in the country.

July and August are hot. Budapest regularly sits in the thirties Celsius, in the nineties Fahrenheit, and the city empties of Hungarians while Lake Balaton fills with them. If you come then, book the lake months ahead and plan your days around a bath or a shady terrace at two in the afternoon. Winter is cold, often grey and much the cheapest time to come, redeemed by Christmas markets in Budapest from the middle of November and by the particular pleasure of sitting outdoors in a steaming thermal pool while it snows. Our month-by-month guide to the best time to visit Hungary sets it out properly.

Getting there is straightforward. Budapest’s Ferenc Liszt airport is the country’s only significant international gateway and is well connected across Europe and the Gulf, with the low-cost airlines using it heavily; most North American travellers will connect through a European hub such as Frankfurt, Munich, Amsterdam, Istanbul or Vienna. Vienna airport is also a practical back door, with a direct coach and a fast train to Budapest in under three hours. Once you are in the country, the trains take over: Budapest to Eger, Debrecen, Pécs, Szeged or the Balaton shore costs very little and is comfortable on the main lines.

Hungary uses the Hungarian forint (HUF, written Ft), not the euro. Hungary has been in the European Union since 2004 but has kept its own currency and has set no date for changing it, so euro notes are not legal tender. A few hotels and tour desks quote in euros and some will take euro cash, almost always at a poor rate. Pay in forint. Cards and phones are taken nearly everywhere, including on buses, trams and at ticket machines, and you need very little cash — a small amount is handy for market stalls, thermal-bath lockers, church donation boxes and rural buses. Prices in shops include tax. In Budapest restaurants a service charge is often printed on the bill; check before adding a tip, and where there is none, ten per cent is plenty. Decline any card machine offering to charge you in dollars and let your own bank convert. Avoid the standalone exchange booths near the main tourist streets and at the airport, which are far worse than a bank machine or an ordinary exchange office in town.

Fishing here

Hungary is a coarse and predator fishing country rather than a fly-fishing one, and it is taken seriously. Lake Balaton is the centre of it — carp, bream, catfish and above all fogas, the pike-perch that appears on every menu around the lake. The Tisza in the east holds very large wels catfish and is famous for the brief June hatch of the long-tailed Tisza mayfly, which locals call the blooming of the river. The Danube and the gravel-pit lakes around it give pike, zander and carp within reach of Budapest. The paperwork is simple but real: you need a state angling licence plus a permit for the particular water, both sold locally or online, and the rules on size, season and keeping fish are enforced. We have no Hungarian fishing book on the shelf yet; the whole collection is in The Guild Fishing Library.

Travelling in your later years

An easy, inexpensive country to travel slowly, with a few things worth knowing before you book.

Budapest is two cities, and one of them is a hill

Pest is flat and walkable. Buda is not: the castle quarter and Gellért hill are proper climbs. There is a funicular up to the castle and a bus that does the same job for the price of a tram ticket, and taking either is not cheating.

The baths are kind to stiff joints

Warm water, handrails and shallow pools, and the medicinal baths are used by Hungarians of every age for exactly this. Changing areas can be a walk from the water, so take flip-flops and allow time.

Old centres are cobbled

Eger, Pécs and the Buda castle quarter are all laid with big uneven stones. The surfaces matter more than the gradients. Sensible shoes, and ask about lifts in converted townhouses before you book rather than after.

Care is good and inexpensive

Pharmacies are everywhere and used to advising. Private clinics and dentists are cheap and quick, which is why so many Europeans come here for dental work. Bring medicines in their original packaging with a copy of the prescription.

Summer heat is the real risk

July and August can be genuinely punishing and much of the older housing stock has no air conditioning. If heat is a worry, come in May, June or September, and check that your room is air-conditioned rather than assuming it.

Concessions are generous

Hungarian state railways carry residents over sixty-five free, and while that does not extend to foreign passports, museums, galleries and many baths give reduced rates to older visitors. It is rarely advertised in English, so ask at the window.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS — the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years — is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today — nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Hungary is a straightforward case. It has been in the European Union since 2004 and in the Schengen area since the end of 2007, so everything above applies here in full, and flying in from Vienna, Munich or Amsterdam involves no passport control at all. What Hungary has not joined is the euro, so the currency is the forint. Its borders with Austria, Slovakia, Slovenia, Croatia and Romania are all internal Schengen crossings now and you will usually drive or ride straight through them. The borders with Serbia and with Ukraine are external Schengen frontiers, where there are proper checks and the queues can be long. None of that affects an ordinary trip to the places in this guide.

Hungary travel FAQ

When is the best time to visit Hungary?

Late May to the end of June, and the first three weeks of September, are the two windows we would pick. The days are long, the squares and the Danube embankments have their tables out, the thermal baths are pleasant rather than punishing, and the queues are a fraction of what they are in August. July and August are hot - Budapest regularly sits in the thirties Celsius, well into the nineties Fahrenheit - and Lake Balaton is booked out by Hungarian families. Winter is cold and genuinely cheap, with Christmas markets in Budapest from the middle of November, and the outdoor thermal pools steaming in the snow are one of the best things the country does.

Is Hungary cheap for visitors?

It is one of the better-value countries in the European Union and noticeably cheaper than Austria, an hour away. Budapest has caught up with the rest of central Europe for hotel rooms in high season, but eating, drinking, museum tickets, trains and taxis are all still good value, and outside Budapest the difference is larger again. Eger, Pecs and Debrecen are inexpensive by any western European standard. The one thing that is not cheap is a bad exchange rate, so avoid the exchange booths on the main tourist streets and at the airport.

Does Hungary use the euro?

No. Hungary joined the European Union in 2004 and the Schengen area at the end of 2007, but it has kept the forint and has set no date for adopting the euro. Some hotels and tour desks quote prices in euros and a few take euro cash, almost always at a poor rate. Pay in forint. Cards and phones are accepted nearly everywhere, including on trams and at ticket machines, so you need very little cash. Decline any card machine that offers to charge you in dollars.

Do I need a car in Hungary?

For Budapest, no, and a car there is a liability. For the rest of the country it depends on what you want. The railway radiates from Budapest like spokes from a hub, which means trains from the capital to Eger, Debrecen, Pecs, Szeged and the Balaton shore are straightforward and cheap, but crossing between two provincial cities without passing through Budapest can be slow. If you want the wine villages of Villany or Badacsony, the Hortobagy puszta or the small Balaton towns, a car earns its keep. If you are doing Budapest and one or two cities, the trains are the easier answer.

Are the thermal baths worth the fuss?

Yes, and they are the thing most visitors remember. Budapest alone has around a dozen working public baths, and they are not all alike. Szechenyi in the City Park is the big one - vast, yellow, outdoor pools open all year, busiest and most photographed. Gellert is the ornate Art Nouveau one. Rudas and Kiraly are small Ottoman bath houses with domes and shafts of light, and feel more like a ritual than a swim. Take a swimming costume, a towel and flip-flops; bring a padlock or hire a locker; and check the day's opening hours and any single-sex sessions before you go, because they vary by bath.

How bad is the language barrier?

Hungarian is unrelated to the languages around it and you will not guess a single word, which unsettles people more than it should. In practice English is widely spoken in Budapest and in anything to do with visitors, and reasonably well in the other cities among younger people. In small towns and among older people it thins out, and German is often the better second language in the west of the country. Signs at stations and airports are bilingual, ticket machines have an English setting, and koszonom - thank you - said badly, is received warmly.

Is Budapest enough, or should I leave the city?

Budapest can fill four or five days without repeating itself and a great many people do only that. But the country beyond it is quieter, cheaper and quite different in character, and the trains make it easy. Eger is two hours away and gives you a baroque town, a castle and a valley of wine cellars. Pecs has Roman tombs, an Ottoman mosque turned church and the Villany reds nearby. Lake Balaton is a summer world of its own. Debrecen and the Great Plain are flatter, plainer and more Hungarian than anything in the capital. Two or three nights outside Budapest changes the shape of the trip.

How many days do you need?

Four days covers Budapest properly, baths included. Seven lets you add Eger or Pecs, or a few days on Lake Balaton in summer, with the train costing you only a morning each way. Ten gives you a real trip: Budapest, one wine region, one provincial city and the lake or the plain. Hungary is small - nothing in this guide is more than about three and a half hours from Budapest by train - so the usual mistake here is not over-reaching but treating the whole country as a two-night stop on a longer tour.
