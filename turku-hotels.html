Turku

Where to Stay in Turku

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Turku is the oldest city in Finland and was its capital until 1812, and it is still the country’s front door to the west: the Stockholm and Åland ferries leave from here, and the archipelago begins at the river mouth. The city is laid out along both banks of the Aura, which is the thing to understand before booking — the river is the spine, and the useful question is how far down it you want to be. The market square and the cathedral are at the upper end, the restaurants and the summer boat bars line the middle, and the castle, the maritime museum and the ferry terminals are at the bottom, a good half hour’s walk from the centre. Everything in between is flat, green and easy, and the little river ferry that crosses in the middle is free.

The market square and the centre

Most centralBuses and shopsFlattest walking

The Kauppatori and the covered market hall beside it are the middle of Turku, and the blocks around them hold most of the city’s hotels, its shops and the bus hub. You are five minutes from the river, ten from the cathedral and fifteen from the railway station, all on the level. It is the most convenient base and the least distinctive, in the way city centres are; the market hall, which is one of the best in the Nordic countries for lunch, more than makes up for it. Rooms here are mostly larger business hotels, so weekends are often cheaper than weekdays.

Along the Aura, the riverside

Restaurants and barsSummer boat terracesBest evenings

The banks of the Aura between the cathedral and the Martinsilta bridge are where Turku spends its evenings. Old ships moored along the quays become bars and restaurants from spring to autumn, the walking paths run both sides, and the museums — the medieval excavation at Aboa Vetus and the art museum up the hill — are a few minutes away. Staying on the river is the nicest way to be in Turku in summer. It is also, for the same reason, the noisiest on a warm Friday night, so ask which way your room faces.

Kakola hill

Views over the cityQuietA climb or a funicular

The hill on the south bank was the site of Finland’s largest prison for a century and a half, and since it closed the granite blocks have been converted into apartments, restaurants and a hotel, with a small funicular running up from the riverside. It is an unusual place to sleep, with the best views in Turku and almost no traffic, and it is ten minutes above the river bars without hearing them. The climb is real if the funicular is not running, and there is little in the way of shops up there.

The castle and the harbour end

Cheapest roomsBy the ferriesHalf an hour out

Down at the river mouth are Turku Castle, which is the oldest and largest in the country, the Forum Marinum maritime museum, and the terminals where the overnight ferries leave for Stockholm and the day boats for Åland. Rooms down here are cheaper than in the centre and this is the obvious place to sleep if you have an early sailing. The rest of the time you are a thirty-minute walk or a short bus ride from everything else, along a stretch of road that is working harbour rather than scenery.

Naantali and the archipelago road

Spa townMoomin World in summerTwenty minutes west

Naantali is a separate small town twenty minutes west of Turku, with a wooden old quarter, a yacht harbour, a large spa hotel and, on an island joined by a footbridge, Moomin World — which is open for the summer only and is built for small children. It is a pleasant and much quieter alternative base, and the start of the road out through the archipelago, where the inter-island ferries are part of the public road network and cost nothing to use. You will want a car, or the frequent bus from Turku.

Getting around

Turku is flat and compact and the centre is best walked. City buses, branded Föli, cover everything else on one ticket, including the run down to the ferry terminals and out to Naantali; there are no trams. The Föri, a little yellow cable ferry, crosses the river in the middle of town, takes about ninety seconds, carries bicycles and is free — it has been running since 1904 and is a local institution. Trains to Helsinki take about two hours; the railway station is a ten-minute walk from the market square, and the harbour has its own station for the ferries. Turku airport is small and ten minutes north. A car is unnecessary in the city and genuinely useful for the archipelago, where the road ferries are free and the islands are the point of coming.

When to come, and what it costs

Turku is a summer city, more so than most of Finland, because so much of what makes it enjoyable is on the water: the river terraces, the boat bars, the archipelago ferries, Moomin World and the sailing regattas all run from roughly late May to late August. July is the warmest and busiest month and the one when the city empties of its own residents. Spring and early autumn are quiet and cheap and still perfectly pleasant for the castle, the cathedral and the market hall. Winter is dark and damp rather than crisply cold this far south-west, the river rarely freezes hard, and much of the outdoor trade closes — with one great exception: the Declaration of Christmas Peace is read from the Brinkkala balcony on the old Great Square at noon on 24 December, has been for centuries, and is broadcast across the country. Midsummer at the end of June closes a good deal of the city for a long weekend.

Finland uses the euro, and it is one of the most cashless countries in Europe — cards and phones are taken everywhere, including on buses and at market stalls, and some places no longer take cash at all. Decline any card machine that offers to charge you in dollars — the rate it gives you is worse than your own bank’s.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Finland itself is straightforward: in the European Union since 1995, the Schengen area since 2001 and the euro since 1999, with the notes and coins arriving in 2002. An arrival from another Schengen country has no passport control at all, and an arrival from outside it is a single check at the airport you land in. The land borders with Sweden and Norway in the north are internal Schengen crossings and are often barely marked; the crossings on the Russian border have been closed to travellers since 2023, so check with the Finnish Border Guard before planning anything that depends on one.

Staying in Turku — questions

Turku or Helsinki — which should I choose?

They are two hours apart and do different jobs. Helsinki is the capital, with the museums, the best restaurants, the harbour and the ferries east; Turku is older, smaller, greener and built around a river, and it is the gateway to the archipelago and the Swedish side. If you only have time for one city, take Helsinki. If you have five or six days, the pair of them with a night or two in the islands between is a better week than either alone.

Is the archipelago easy to visit without a car?

Partly. The nearer islands are reached by bus and by the summer water buses from the city, and Åland is a day boat away from the harbour. But the Archipelago Trail, which is the route most people mean, runs island to island on small road ferries and is really a driving or cycling route — the ferries are free and frequent, and there is no bus along most of it. If you want the islands properly, hire a car for two days or take a bicycle on the boat.

Where do the Stockholm ferries leave from, and should I stay near them?

From the harbour terminals at the river mouth, about three kilometres from the market square, with their own railway station, frequent buses and plenty of parking. Stay down there only if you have an early morning sailing or a late arrival; otherwise the centre is a better base and the bus or taxi down takes ten minutes. The overnight crossing to Stockholm and the shorter day run to Mariehamn both leave from the same stretch of quay.

Is Moomin World open all year?

No. It is a summer park, open for a season that runs roughly from early June to late August, with a short extra opening at some winter holidays — check the dates for your week before building a trip around it. It is at Naantali, twenty minutes from Turku, and it is aimed squarely at children of about two to ten. Adults who have come for Tove Jansson’s drawings rather than for the characters want the Moomin Museum in Tampere instead.

How long does Turku need?

Two nights. One day covers the castle, the maritime museum, the cathedral and the market hall, with an evening on the river; the second is either the archipelago or Naantali. Add a third if you intend to drive the Archipelago Trail properly. In winter, when the boats and terraces are shut, a single night and a good lunch is a fair reckoning.
