Why visit the United Kingdom

Few countries pack this much history, culture, landscape and character into so compact a space — or make it so easy to travel end to end by train.

Layers of history

Stonehenge and Hadrian's Wall, Roman Bath, medieval cathedrals, Tudor palaces and hundreds of castles — thousands of years, all still standing.

Culture at every turn

The British Museum, the National Gallery and the Tate — most of them free — plus West End theatre, live music and a literary heritage without equal.

Landscapes to roam

The Lake District and the Scottish Highlands, Welsh mountains and the Yorkshire Dales, the white cliffs of Dover and the Cornish coast.

Pubs, tea and markets

A pint by a roaring fire, a proper Sunday roast, afternoon tea and buzzing food markets — the everyday pleasures that make a British trip.

How the country divides up

London & the South East

Almost every trip begins in London, and it should. Westminster and Buckingham Palace, the Tower and the British Museum, markets from Borough to Portobello and theatre every night — the capital layers two thousand years into a sprawl of distinct villages. Beyond it lie seaside Brighton, the cathedral city of Canterbury and royal Windsor, all an easy day trip by train.

The West Country & Wales

Honey-stone Bath keeps its Roman baths and Georgian crescents, and makes the gateway to the Cotswolds; harbourside Bristol is all street art and independent spirit. West, St Ives anchors Cornwall's surf coast, while across the border Cardiff opens up Wales — castles, male-voice choirs and the peaks of Snowdonia and the Brecon Beacons.

The North & the Midlands

Manchester is the reinvented powerhouse of the north — music, football and industry turned culture — while Liverpool keeps the Beatles and a great waterfront. York wraps its Minster in medieval walls, and the fells, tarns and Wordsworth country of the Lake District spread to the west. Shakespeare's Stratford and the Peak District sit in the green Midlands between.

Scotland & Northern Ireland

Edinburgh is one of Europe's most dramatic capitals — a castle on its rock, the Royal Mile and the world's biggest arts festival each August; Glasgow counters with grit, galleries and nightlife. North lie the lochs and glens of the Highlands around Inverness, and across the water Belfast leads to the basalt columns of the Giant's Causeway.

Top things to do in the United Kingdom

The experiences worth building a trip around.

Royal & historic London

The Tower of London and the Crown Jewels, Westminster Abbey, the Changing of the Guard at Buckingham Palace, and a spin on the London Eye.

Free national museums

The British Museum, the National Gallery, the V&A, the Natural History Museum and Tate Modern — world-class collections, most of them free to enter.

Edinburgh & its castle

Climb the Royal Mile to Edinburgh Castle, hike up Arthur's Seat for the view, and time a visit for the August festival if you can.

The Scottish Highlands

Glens, lochs and mountains, Loch Ness and Glencoe, the West Highland Line and the Isle of Skye — Britain's wildest, most cinematic scenery.

Stonehenge & Roman Bath

Stand before the prehistoric stone circle on Salisbury Plain, then soak up the ancient Roman Baths and Georgian streets of nearby Bath.

Walk the countryside

The Lake District fells, the Cotswold villages, the Welsh mountains and the coast path — footpaths and cosy pubs are a British institution.

Best time to visit & getting there

When to go

May–June and September are the sweet spots: mild, long days, gardens and countryside at their best, and lighter crowds than high summer.

July–August is the warmest and busiest season, ideal for the coast, the Highlands and festivals such as the Edinburgh Fringe — book ahead.

November–March is cold, wet and dark by mid-afternoon, but it brings Christmas markets, cosy pubs and low-season city prices. Pack a waterproof whenever you come.

Getting there

London Heathrow (LHR) and London Gatwick (LGW) are the main long-haul gateways, with direct flights from across the world.

Manchester (MAN), Edinburgh (EDI), Birmingham (BHX) and Glasgow (GLA) also take international flights, and fast trains link the major cities so smoothly that many visitors skip domestic flying altogether.

The currency is the pound sterling. Cards work almost everywhere and many places are cashless; carry a little cash for small independents. Remember that traffic drives on the left, and always check your current visa and passport-validity requirements before booking.

United Kingdom travel FAQ

When is the best time to visit the United Kingdom?

Late spring and early autumn — roughly May to June and September — are the sweet spots: mild, long days, gardens and countryside at their best, and fewer crowds than high summer. July and August are the warmest and busiest months, ideal for the coast, festivals and the Highlands. Winter is cold, wet and dark by mid-afternoon, but it brings Christmas markets, cosy pubs and low-season city prices. Pack a waterproof layer whenever you come — British weather changes fast.

What currency is used in the United Kingdom?

The pound sterling (GBP, symbol £). The UK does not use the euro. Cards, including contactless and phone payments, are accepted almost everywhere — shops, pubs, buses, taxis and even most market stalls — and many places are effectively cashless. Carry a little cash for small independents and rural spots. ATMs (cashpoints) are widespread; decline any machine that offers to charge you in your home currency, as the built-in rate is poor.

London or Edinburgh — which should I choose?

London is the grand all-rounder: world-class museums (many of them free), royal palaces, theatre, markets and endless neighbourhoods to explore across many days. Edinburgh is compact and dramatic — a medieval Old Town beneath its castle, Georgian elegance in the New Town, and the gateway to the Highlands. On a first trip many travellers do both, linked by a four-and-a-half-hour train up the East Coast Main Line.

How many days do you need in the United Kingdom?

For a first trip, seven to ten days is a good starting point: three or four days in London, then Edinburgh or a stretch of countryside such as the Cotswolds, the Lake District or the Scottish Highlands. With two weeks you can add Bath and Wales, York and the north, or a loop through the Highlands. Fast trains link the major cities, so many visitors skip domestic flying altogether.

Is the United Kingdom safe for travellers?

The UK is a very popular, well-developed destination and is generally very safe for travellers. The main thing to guard against is petty theft — pickpockets and phone-snatchers work crowded areas, transport hubs and busy nightlife districts in the big cities, so keep valuables secure. Remember that traffic drives on the left, so look right first when crossing, use licensed black cabs or official ride apps, and check your government's current travel advisory before you go.
