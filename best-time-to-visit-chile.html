When to Visit Chile

Best Time to Visit Chile — Month by Month

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Chile runs from the tropics almost to the Antarctic, so there is no single right month — there is a right month for the part of Chile you are going to. Patagonia has a short, windy, crowded summer and a long closed season. The Atacama is open all year. Santiago and the coast are at their best in spring and autumn, and the Lake District wants the summer. Below is the short answer by region, then every month set out plainly, and the national holidays that fill the country whatever the weather is doing.

The short answer

Remember first that Chile is in the southern hemisphere: December is high summer and July is deep winter. After that, the answer depends entirely on which part of a four-thousand-kilometre country you are going to.

Going to Patagonia?

December to February, with November and March as the quieter and often kinder shoulders. Outside roughly October to April a great deal of the far south closes, the days are short and the weather decides your itinerary for you.

Going to the Atacama?

Any month. The desert works all year. Nights are cold whenever you come, and the one caveat is the brief summer rain of January and February, which occasionally closes the road to the geysers.

Going to Santiago, the wine country and the coast?

September to November or March to May. Spring and autumn are warm, clear and far more pleasant than the dry heat of January. Autumn adds the grape harvest.

Going to the Lake District?

December to March. This is a genuinely rainy region and the summer is when you get the long evenings and the chance of a clear volcano. It is also when every Chilean family is there.

Want to ski?

June to September, in the Andes within an hour or two of Santiago — one of the few places where a northern-hemisphere traveller can ski in July.

Avoiding crowds?

Steer clear of January and February, the Chilean school holidays, and of the Fiestas Patrias around 18 September, when the whole country travels at once.

Month by month

January

High summer and the first half of the Chilean holidays. Patagonia is at its warmest and its windiest, and Torres del Paine is as busy as it ever gets — park hotels and refuges are often full months ahead. Santiago is hot and dry. The Lake District is at its best. In the Atacama this is the season of the invierno altiplánico, the altiplanic winter, when brief heavy storms can close high-altitude roads for a day.

February

Much like January, and the peak of the domestic holiday: the coast and the lakes are full of Chilean families and prices follow. Still an excellent month in the far south. Rapa Nui holds its Tapati festival, which is the island at its liveliest and its most booked-out.

March

One of the best months in the country. The holiday crowds go home in the first week, the weather in Patagonia often settles, and central Chile moves into the grape harvest with vineyard festivals through the valleys. Days are shortening in the south but still long enough to walk.

April

Autumn. Central Chile is warm, clear and quiet — arguably the nicest time to be in Santiago and on the coast. The far south is turning: colour in the beech forest, real cold arriving, and services beginning to close for the season. The Atacama is excellent.

May

The shoulder closes. Patagonia is largely shut to ordinary tourism and the Lake District is wet. Santiago cools and the winter smog begins to settle in the basin on still days. A good month for the desert and for city travel at low prices, and a poor one for the south.

June

Winter. Ski season opens in the Andes above Santiago, which is the main reason to come now. Short days, grey skies and poor air quality in the capital on the worst days. The Atacama is cold at night and brilliantly clear.

July

The depth of winter and the Chilean winter school holiday, so the ski fields are at their busiest and most expensive. Snow is usually at its most reliable. The south is dark and wet; northern Chile is pleasant by day and freezing after sunset.

August

Still winter, still skiing, and often the best snow of the season late in the month. Santiago begins to brighten towards the end. Patagonia remains out of season apart from specialist winter photography and wildlife trips.

September

Spring arrives in the centre, and with it the Fiestas Patrias around the 18th — the national holiday, when Chileans travel en masse, transport and hotels fill and much of the country takes several days off. Either plan around it or join it, but do not arrive unaware of it. The far south starts to reopen late in the month.

October

An underrated month. Spring in the centre with wildflowers and clear views of the cordillera, the Patagonian season opening with few people in it, and the Lake District green and quiet. Weather in the south is unsettled but the trade for having Torres del Paine nearly to yourself is a fair one.

November

Many travellers' pick for the whole country. Patagonia is open, the days are long, and the wind has not yet reached its summer worst. Central Chile is warm and not yet hot. Prices and crowds are both below the December peak.

December

Summer begins and the far south comes into its own, with daylight stretching towards eleven at night in Patagonia. The first half of the month is still relatively quiet; from about the 20th the Chilean holidays begin and the country fills. Book Christmas and New Year well ahead anywhere in the south.

By region

The Atacama — no bad month

Rain is so rare here as to be a local event, and the sun is strong in every season. What changes is the night: it is cold after dark all year and genuinely freezing in the southern winter, and the high-altitude excursions start before dawn. The only seasonal hazard is the summer altiplanic storms of January and February. For stargazing, pick your dates by the moon rather than the month.

Santiago and central Chile — spring and autumn

The summer is hot and bone dry and the city empties in February. The winter is mild by northern standards but brings the smog that the surrounding mountains trap over the basin, which matters for anyone with a chest condition. September to November and March to May are the sweet spots, and the ski fields an hour away make June to September worthwhile for a different reason.

Valparaíso and the coast — warm but never hot

The Humboldt Current keeps this coast cool and often foggy in the mornings, even in January, and the sea is cold all year. January and February bring Chilean holidaymakers to Viña del Mar in force; the hills of Valparaíso are better visited either side of that.

The Lake District — December to March

A green, rainy, temperate region where rain is possible in any month and likely in most. The summer gives the long evenings, the open roads and the clear volcano views. Winter is subdued, wet and dark, with a small ski operation on Osorno for the determined.

Patagonia — a real season

October or November to March or April, and the wind is the defining feature: fiercest from November to February, exactly when everything else is best. Expect four seasons in a day, book accommodation and refuges months ahead for January and February, and accept that the weather here will rearrange at least one of your plans.

Rapa Nui — year round, with one date

Easter Island is subtropical and visitable in any month, with the southern summer warmest and the winter wetter. February brings the Tapati festival, which is the most interesting time to be there and the hardest time to find a room.

Holidays and dates that fill the country

Three periods matter more than the weather when you are choosing dates. January and February are the Chilean summer school holidays: the coast, the lakes and Patagonia fill with domestic travellers and prices rise accordingly. The Fiestas Patrias around 18 September are the great national holiday, with several days of public celebration, packed transport and full hotels across the country. Christmas and New Year combine with the start of the summer holidays, which makes late December the single hardest time to book a room in the south. The winter school holiday in July does the same to the ski resorts. Religious and public holidays fall on other dates through the year and move from year to year, so check the current calendar when you fix your dates rather than relying on a list that would go stale.

When to visit Chile — questions

What is the single best month to visit Chile?

If you must pick one for the whole country, November. Patagonia is open and the days are long but the summer wind has not peaked, central Chile is warm without the January heat, the Lake District is green, and the Atacama is as good as ever. March runs it close, with the grape harvest as a bonus and the summer crowds gone home.

Is it worth going to Patagonia outside the summer?

In the shoulders, very much so: October, November, March and April give you the same landscape with fewer people, lower prices and, often, calmer air. In the southern winter it becomes a specialist trip — much is closed, days are short, and you would be going for winter photography or for the wildlife rather than for walking. Check what is actually open before booking anything between May and September.

Can I see the desert and Patagonia on one trip?

Yes, and a great many visitors do, but respect the distance: they are at opposite ends of the country and both reached by air through Santiago. Allow two internal flights, three nights minimum in each region, and about two weeks in total. Trying it in ten days means spending the best part of three of them travelling.

How bad is the wind in Patagonia really?

Bad enough to be a planning factor rather than a detail. In the summer months it regularly gusts hard enough to unbalance a walker on an exposed path, to close the catamaran across Lake Pehoe and to shut high routes for the day. It is not dangerous to a sensible visitor in the right clothing, but it is relentless, and a showerproof jacket is not enough — bring a genuine windproof shell.

Does it ever rain in the Atacama?

Very rarely, and in parts of it effectively never — which is why the observatories are there. The exception is the altiplanic winter of January and February, when storms over the high Andes can send brief heavy rain across the region and occasionally close the road up to the El Tatio geysers for a day or two. Ask your hotel in the morning rather than assuming the trip is on.
