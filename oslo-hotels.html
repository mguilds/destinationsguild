Oslo

Where to Stay in Oslo

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Oslo is an easy city to stay in and a confusing one to choose in, because it has grown a brand-new waterfront quarter in the last fifteen years while the old centre stayed put, and both now call themselves the middle of town. The short version: the centre between the station and the harbour is the convenient choice, Grünerløkka is the enjoyable one, and the west is the calm one. Below is what each area is actually like to walk out of in the morning.

Where to stay in Oslo, area by area

Four parts of the city, what each is actually like, and who each one suits.

The Guild does not name a hotel until an Explorer has confirmed it on the hotel’s own website, so this guide sets out the areas and sends you to the full, current list for each one rather than printing a shortlist we cannot stand behind yet. Named picks will be added to this page as they are checked.

Sentrum - the station, Karl Johans gate and the harbour

Most convenientStep-freeBest for a short stay

The obvious choice, and the right one for a first visit or a single night between flights. This is the strip from the central station along Karl Johans gate to the royal palace, and south to the water at Bjorvika and Aker Brygge. The airport train, every metro line, the trams and the fjord ferries all start here, the opera house and the new library are a few minutes on foot, and the harbour promenade is flat the whole way. It is also where the large international chains and the business hotels are, which is why weekend and July rates in this area are often the best value in the city. The honest drawback: the immediate streets behind the station are the scruffiest part of central Oslo, and the area is busy rather than charming.

Grunerlokka and Gronland - where Oslo eats

Restaurants and barsYoungerTram ride to centre

North-east of the centre across the Akerselva river, Grunerlokka is the old working-class quarter that became the city's eating and drinking district: coffee bars, bakeries, independent shops and a great many restaurants around Olaf Ryes plass and Thorvald Meyers gate. Gronland, between it and the station, is Oslo's most mixed neighbourhood and the best place in the country for cheap, good food. Both are a ten-minute tram ride or a twenty-five-minute walk from the station, and the river path between them and the centre is a lovely walk in its own right. Fewer hotels here than in the centre, and more apartments - worth knowing if you want a kitchen.

Majorstuen and Frogner - the quiet west

ResidentialGood value for spaceFamily-friendly

West of the palace, this is the handsome, settled part of Oslo: wide streets, nineteenth-century apartment blocks, embassies, the Vigeland sculpture park and the city's best food hall at Majorstuen. It is four or five metro stops from the centre, and the metro line that climbs to the ski jump and the forest starts here, which makes it the natural base if you want to walk or ski in Nordmarka. Rooms and apartments tend to be larger for the money than in the centre, and the streets are quiet at night. The trade-off is that you will use the metro every day rather than walking out of the door into the sights.

Bygdoy and the fjord side

MuseumsSummerCar or ferry

The peninsula south-west of the centre holds the museums most people come to Oslo for - the Fram polar ship, the Kon-Tiki rafts, the maritime museum and the Viking ship collection - along with beaches and woods. There is very little accommodation out here, and we would not suggest basing yourself on Bygdoy; it is included because readers ask. Stay in the centre and come out by the small summer ferry from the City Hall quay, which takes about ten minutes and is a pleasure in itself, or by bus in winter.

Getting around

One ticket covers the metro, trams, buses and the local fjord ferries, and it is sold by zone and by time rather than by journey, so a single ticket lets you change between all of them within the hour. Buy it in the transport app or from a machine; buying from the driver costs more where it is possible at all. The metro is the backbone and runs from early morning to past midnight, with night services at weekends.

The city is flat in the middle and hilly at the edges, and the harbour promenade from the opera house west to Aker Brygge and on to the museums is level, paved and continuous — the single best walk in Oslo and entirely step-free. Cycle hire is widespread in summer. Taxis are plentiful, metered and expensive, and a short ride across the centre will cost more than most visitors expect.

When to come, and what it costs

Oslo is a four-season city rather than a summer one. May to August is the best of it: the waterfront comes alive, the fjord islands open up for swimming, and the light lasts until almost midnight in June. September and October bring autumn colour in the forest and the start of the indoor season. December is dark — about six hours of light at the solstice — but the markets and the lights make a virtue of it. February and March are the skiing months, and the city is one of very few capitals where you can take the metro to a cross-country trail.

On price, Oslo runs against the Norwegian grain in one useful way. A large share of its hotels serve business travellers, so rates fall at weekends and through July when those travellers are elsewhere. If your trip has any flexibility, that is where the money is. Norway uses the Norwegian krone (NOK, written kr), not the euro. Norway is not a member of the European Union, and euros are not accepted in shops. It is also one of the most nearly cashless countries in the world: cards and phones are taken everywhere, including on buses, in mountain huts and at market stalls, and many places no longer handle notes at all. You do not need to carry krone in cash. Wine and spirits are sold only through the state shops, Vinmonopolet, which close early and are shut on Sundays; beer is in supermarkets but stops being sold at a set hour in the evening. Tipping is not expected and never chased — rounding up a restaurant bill is plenty. Decline any card machine that offers to charge you in dollars; pay in krone and let your own bank convert.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS — the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years — is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today — nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Norway is the case readers get wrong most often. It is not in the European Union, but it is in the Schengen area, so everything above applies here in full — the border checks, the Entry/Exit System, and ETIAS when it comes. Flying Oslo to Copenhagen or Stockholm is an internal Schengen hop with no passport control. What Norway’s position outside the EU does change is customs: there are duty limits on what you may bring in, and the allowance for alcohol and tobacco is modest and genuinely enforced.

Staying in Oslo — questions

Which part of Oslo should I stay in?

For a short first visit, the centre between the main station and the harbour. Almost everything a visitor wants is within a twenty-minute walk of it - the opera house, the fortress, the new Munch museum, the national museum and the waterfront - and the airport train and every metro and tram line meet there. If you would rather eat and drink where Oslo does and do not mind a tram ride, Grunerlokka is the answer. If you are here for several days, or travelling with family, the quieter west around Majorstuen and Frogner is calmer and better value for space.

Is Oslo walkable?

The central part is, genuinely and pleasantly so, and it is flatter than Bergen or Stockholm. The harbour promenade runs continuously for several miles past the opera house, the fortress and the new library, and it is level the whole way. Beyond the centre the city spreads out and climbs, and that is what the metro and trams are for - they are frequent, clean and easy, and one ticket covers all of them.

How do I get in from the airport?

Oslo Airport is about thirty miles north of the city and the train is the obvious way in. Two services run on the same line: a dedicated express that takes around twenty minutes to the central station, and the ordinary regional trains, which take a little longer, call at a couple of extra stops and cost noticeably less for the same journey. Both are step-free. Buy from the machines or the app rather than at a counter.

Is Oslo as expensive as the rest of Norway?

It is the most expensive city in the country for a restaurant meal and a drink, and about average for a hotel room - which surprises people, but Oslo has a lot of business hotels, and they discount hard at weekends and through July when the business travellers are gone. That is the single biggest saving available here: a Friday-to-Sunday or mid-summer stay in Oslo can cost a good deal less than the same room on a Tuesday in October.

Do I need a car in Oslo?

No, and it is an active nuisance. Parking is scarce and dear, much of the centre is closed or hostile to cars by design, and the public transport is genuinely good. If you are picking up a hire car to drive west into the fjords, collect it on the day you leave rather than keeping it parked.

How many days is enough for Oslo?

Two full days covers the city comfortably: one for the waterfront, the fortress and the centre, one for the museums out on the Bygdoy peninsula - the Fram, the Kon-Tiki and the Viking ship collection - reached by a small ferry in summer or a bus year-round. A third day is well spent on the fjord islands in summer or in the forest on the city's edge, which the metro reaches directly.
