Belize

The Belize Travel Guide

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Belize is a small, English-speaking country on the Caribbean coast of Central America, with the second-longest barrier reef in the world a few hundred metres off its cayes and serious jungle an hour inland. It was British Honduras until 1973 and independent since 1981, and it feels like neither a Spanish-speaking Latin country nor a Caribbean island but a quiet, slow, oddly British thing of its own. Fewer than half a million people live here. For a visitor the appeal is simple and unusual: you can stand on a Maya temple above the canopy in the morning and swim over a reef wall the next, in the same week, in your own language, using money that is pinned to the US dollar.

Why visit Belize

Everything is in English

Belize is the only country in Central America with English as its official language, a legacy of British Honduras, which it was until 1973. Road signs, menus, bus destinations, hospital paperwork and the conversation at the next table are all in English. Kriol is the language most Belizeans actually speak to each other, and Spanish, Garifuna, several Maya languages and Low German are all spoken somewhere in the country, but a visitor never needs any of them. For a first trip to the tropics with nervous travellers in the party, that one fact removes most of the friction.

The reef is on the doorstep

The Belize Barrier Reef runs almost the whole length of the coast and is a UNESCO World Heritage site. On Ambergris Caye and Caye Caulker it is not an excursion you build a day around — it is a few hundred metres offshore, close enough that the swimming water inside it is flat on days when the open sea is not. That proximity is the single thing that most distinguishes Belize from the bigger Caribbean islands.

Reef and rainforest in one week

The country is about the size of Wales or Massachusetts. You can snorkel in the morning on a caye, fly or drive inland the same afternoon, and be standing in front of a Maya temple in the Cayo District the next morning. Very few countries put salt water and serious jungle that close together, and the standard Belize trip — a few days inland, a few days on the water — exists because the geography allows it.

The Maya sites are not crowded

Caracol, Xunantunich, Lamanai, Altun Ha and Cahal Pech are all real, substantial sites, and on an ordinary weekday you may have long stretches of them to yourself. You can still climb a good deal of what you see, which is no longer true at many of the famous sites in Mexico and Guatemala.

American money works

The Belize dollar is pegged at two to one United States dollar and has been for decades. US dollars are accepted more or less everywhere, and change may come back in either currency, so it pays to be clear which dollar a price is quoted in before you agree to it. For American visitors the arithmetic is simply halving, which makes the whole country easy to read.

It is small enough to understand

Fewer than half a million people live here. There are four highways and one of them is mostly a drive through citrus and cacao. Within a few days most visitors have a working picture of the whole country, which is a rarer pleasure than it sounds and makes a second visit feel like going back rather than starting again.

Belize at a glance

Capital

Belmopan, inland and purpose-built after Hurricane Hattie wrecked the old capital in 1961. Belize City is still the largest town, the main port and the place the water taxis leave from, but it is not the seat of government and it is not where most visitors sleep.

Money

The Belize dollar, pegged at two to the US dollar. US cash is widely accepted. Cards are fine in hotels and larger restaurants; the cayes, the village shops and the buses want cash. ATMs exist in San Pedro, Caye Caulker, Placencia, San Ignacio, Belmopan and Belize City, and are sparse elsewhere.

Language

English is official. Belize Kriol is the lingua franca. Spanish is a first language for a large share of the population, particularly in the north and west. Garifuna is spoken along the southern coast, and Plautdietsch in the Mennonite communities.

Borders

Mexico to the north at Chetumal, Guatemala to the west at Benque Viejo and to the south at Punta Gorda by boat. Both land crossings are used routinely by travellers; both have their own fees and formalities, which change, so check the current position with the border authority rather than with a tour desk.

Getting about

Two domestic airlines hop between Belize City, San Pedro, Caye Caulker, Dangriga, Placencia and Punta Gorda in small aircraft — expensive by local standards and the thing that makes a short trip work. Water taxis run from Belize City to the northern cayes. Buses are cheap, frequent on the highways and slow. Hire cars are fine on the four main roads and want ground clearance anywhere else. Driving is on the right.

Shape of it

A low, wet coastal plain with the reef and some four hundred cayes off it, rising west and south into the Maya Mountains and the Cayo District. The Hummingbird Highway between Belmopan and Dangriga is the one drive worth taking for its own sake.

The regions

The northern cayes: Ambergris Caye and Caye Caulker

Two islands inside the reef, half an hour apart by boat, and between them they take the large majority of Belize’s visitors. Ambergris is much the bigger and busier: San Pedro is a proper town with streets, banks, a hospital, hundreds of places to eat and a long strip of resorts running north and south of it. Caye Caulker is a sand-lane village with no cars at all and a famous instruction — go slow — painted on a sign at the dock. Neither has a broad swimming beach in the Caribbean-poster sense: sea grass grows right up to the shore in much of the shallows, and most hotels put you in the water from a pier instead. Knowing that in advance is the difference between a happy arrival and a disappointed one.

Cayo and the west: San Ignacio and the Maya Mountains

Inland Belize, two hours west of the international airport on a good road. San Ignacio is a hill town on the Macal River with a genuine Saturday market, and it is the base for the great majority of the country’s land excursions: Xunantunich across the hand-cranked ferry, Cahal Pech on the ridge above town, Caracol deep in the Chiquibul forest, the pine ridge and its waterfalls, and the cave systems that run under all of it. The river lodges strung along the Macal and the Mopan are a distinct kind of Belize holiday in their own right.

The southern coast: Placencia, Hopkins and Dangriga

South of Dangriga the coast opens into the Stann Creek district and the long thin Placencia peninsula, which ends in a village where the road stops. This is where the mainland comes closest to having beaches in the ordinary sense, and it is the departure point for the southern cayes and the atolls. Hopkins, half an hour north, is a Garifuna village and the best place in the country to hear Garifuna drumming played by people who are not performing it for a tour bus.

The far south: Toledo and Punta Gorda

The wettest, greenest and least visited part of Belize, with Maya villages, cacao farms, waterfalls and almost no tourism infrastructure. It rewards people who have already seen the rest of the country and want the version with nobody in it. The boat to Guatemala leaves from Punta Gorda.

The north: Orange Walk and Corozal

Flat cane country running up to the Mexican border, with Lamanai reached by a long river boat through the jungle — for many visitors the single best half-day in Belize — and a quiet bay at Corozal that sees few foreign visitors and is popular with people who have retired here.

The atolls

Turneffe, Glover’s Reef and Lighthouse Reef sit outside the barrier reef in deep water. Lighthouse holds the Great Blue Hole, the collapsed cave mouth that appears on every poster of Belize. It is a long day by fast boat from the northern cayes and a genuinely serious dive rather than a snorkel; a good many people who make the trip enjoy the aerial view and the two shallower dives alongside it more than the hole itself, and it is worth knowing that before you book.

Things to do in Belize

Snorkel Hol Chan and Shark Ray Alley

The marine reserve off the south end of Ambergris Caye, and the shallow sandbar beside it where nurse sharks and southern stingrays gather. It is the most popular half-day in the country for good reason: the cut through the reef is full of fish, the water is shallow, and it works for competent swimmers who are not divers. Go with an operator licensed for the reserve, and go early if you want it quieter.

Climb Xunantunich

Reached by a hand-cranked cable ferry across the Mopan River near Benque, which is half the pleasure. El Castillo still stands high enough to put you above the forest canopy with Guatemala in view. It is the most accessible of the big sites from San Ignacio and can be done in a morning.

Take the river boat to Lamanai

An hour or more up the New River from Orange Walk, through narrow jungle channels, with the boatman pointing out birds, bats and crocodiles on the way. The site itself sits on a lagoon and includes the Mask Temple and the High Temple. The journey is as much the point as the ruins.

Go slow on Caye Caulker

Not an activity so much as a decision. Sand streets, bicycles, a swimming channel at the Split where the island was cut in two by a hurricane, and a day that organises itself around nothing. If the rest of the trip is busy, put this at the end of it.

Drive the Hummingbird Highway

Belmopan to Dangriga through the Maya Mountains: citrus groves, limestone hills, roadside stalls and the turning for St Herman’s Blue Hole national park, which is an inland swimming hole and nothing to do with the famous one offshore. Genuinely one of the finest short drives in Central America.

Hear Garifuna drumming at Hopkins

The Garifuna are descended from Carib, Arawak and West African people exiled to the Central American coast in 1797, and their language, music and dance are recognised by UNESCO. Hopkins is where to hear it. Garifuna Settlement Day falls on 19 November and the village is the place to be for it.

When to go, and what it costs

Belize has a dry season and a green season rather than four. The dry stretch runs roughly from late November or December into April or May, and that is high season: reliable sun, calm water, the best visibility on the reef, the fullest hotels and the highest prices, with Christmas, New Year and Easter dearer again. The green season from June through November is hot and humid with heavy afternoon rain rather than all-day drizzle, and the country is cheaper and emptier for it. The Atlantic hurricane season officially runs from the first of June to the end of November, with the greatest activity usually from August into October; most years pass without incident, but if you travel then, take the insurance and keep the plan loose.

Two other things move with the calendar. Sargassum seaweed has arrived on Caribbean shores in large quantities in recent years, generally worst from around March to August, and it affects the windward side of the cayes more than the lee; hotels rake it, but a bad week is a bad week. And whale sharks have historically been reported off Gladden Spit near Placencia around the full moons of the spring months — sightings have been far less dependable in recent years than the marketing suggests, so treat it as a possibility worth being there for rather than a booking you can count on.

The full month-by-month account, including what each part of the country is doing while the rest of it rains, is in Best Time to Visit Belize.

Fishing here

Belize is one of the homes of saltwater fly fishing. The flats inside the barrier reef and around the atolls hold bonefish, permit and tarpon in shallow, wadeable water, and the country is one of the few places where all three are realistically available in a single day — the grand slam that flats anglers travel for. Turneffe, Glover’s Reef, Ambergris Caye and the waters off Placencia and Punta Gorda are the names that come up most. The Guild’s saltwater writing lives in the flats guide, and every shelf in the wing is indexed in the Guild Fishing Library.

Travelling in your later years

The small planes are the kind thing

The hop from the international airport to San Pedro or Placencia takes a quarter of an hour and replaces a long drive and a boat. The aircraft are small and the steps are shallow; staff are used to helping. For anyone who tires on a road journey this is money very well spent, and it is the single adjustment that makes Belize comfortable at any age.

Piers, boats and the swim ladder

Much of the swimming on the cayes is from a pier rather than off a beach, which means a ladder and a step down into the water. Water taxis and snorkel boats are boarded from a dock and can be lively in a chop. Ask directly about the ladder and the boarding before you book, and prefer a sheltered dock on the lee side of an island.

Golf carts are the local car

On Ambergris Caye and in Placencia the golf cart is the normal vehicle. They are easy to get in and out of and slow, but they have no doors, the sand roads are rough, and the steps up are higher than they look. If you hire one, take the newer and larger model.

Heat, and the medical question

It is hot and humid for most of the year, and the middle of the day is genuinely draining. Plan the morning and the late afternoon and rest between. Belize City has the country’s main hospitals; the cayes and the smaller towns have clinics. Serious cases are normally flown out, which is the reason to buy travel insurance that explicitly covers medical evacuation before you come.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Most visitors from the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, the European Union and much of the Commonwealth may enter Belize as tourists without arranging a visa in advance, and are admitted for a limited stay that can usually be extended at an immigration office for a fee. A passport with sufficient remaining validity, onward or return travel, and an address for the first night are the ordinary requirements.

Beyond that we do not print the numbers, and nor should anyone else. Entry rules, the length of stay granted, the departure and border fees and the online arrival forms are exactly the details that change without notice and that a page like this one gets wrong months after it is written. Check the current position with the Belize Immigration and Nationality Department and with your own government’s travel advice shortly before you fly, and again if your plans include crossing into Guatemala or Mexico and returning.

Questions travellers ask about Belize

How many days do I need in Belize?

Seven nights is the trip that works, split between inland and the water: three or four nights in the Cayo District for the Maya sites and the caves, then three or four on Ambergris Caye or Caye Caulker, or down at Placencia. Five nights can be done but forces you to choose one half of the country. Ten lets you add the far south or an atoll without moving every other morning. Because the small planes cut the transfers to a quarter of an hour, Belize gives you more usable days than its road map suggests.

Is Belize expensive?

More than its neighbours and less than the big Caribbean islands. Guatemala and Mexico are cheaper for the same latitude, and visitors who arrive expecting Central American prices are regularly surprised. Imported food, the cayes and the dive operators are where the cost sits; buses, market food and the inland towns are modest. High season from December to April carries the top prices, and Christmas, New Year and Easter are higher again. We do not print figures because they move, but that shape is reliable.

Do I need to hire a car?

Not if you are staying on the cayes, where there are no cars worth speaking of. For the Cayo District a car is useful but not essential, because most lodges arrange transfers and every excursion is sold with transport included. If you do drive, the four main highways are well surfaced and straightforward, driving is on the right, and anything off them wants ground clearance. Many visitors fly between regions and hire nothing at all.

Which should I choose, Ambergris Caye or Caye Caulker?

Ambergris Caye if you want choice, restaurants, a hospital, a bank and a wide range of hotels, and do not mind that San Pedro is a busy little town. Caye Caulker if you want somewhere small, sandy and slow, and are content with fewer options and simpler rooms. The reef is reachable from both and the snorkelling is much the same. Many people spend a few nights on each, which costs half an hour on a water taxi.

Is the Great Blue Hole worth it?

Honestly, it depends what you want from it. It is a long day by fast boat from the northern cayes, usually three dives of which the hole itself is a deep one for certified divers, and what you see there is a wall of ancient stalactites in dim blue water rather than a reef full of fish. The two shallower dives on Lighthouse Reef that come with the trip are often the better diving. Snorkellers cannot meaningfully see it from the surface. The photograph everyone knows is taken from an aircraft, and a scenic flight is a legitimate way to experience it.

Will there be seaweed on the beach?

Possibly. Sargassum has reached Caribbean shores in large quantities in recent years and the worst of it tends to fall between about March and August, though it varies enormously from year to year and from one side of an island to the other. Hotels on the affected coasts rake their frontage daily. It is also worth knowing that much of the shoreline on the cayes is sea grass rather than clean sand even without it, and that most swimming happens off a pier.

Is Belize safe?

The tourist areas — the cayes, the Cayo District, Placencia and Hopkins — are generally straightforward, and ordinary care is what they ask of you. Belize City is the exception that every honest guide names: it has a serious violent-crime problem concentrated in particular neighbourhoods, and the sensible pattern is to move through it rather than linger, which is what most itineraries do anyway. Travel advisories change, so read your own government’s current advice rather than relying on any guide, including this one.

What language will I need?

None but English. Belize is the only country in Central America with English as its official language, and it is used in government, schools, signage and business. Most Belizeans speak Kriol to each other, and a great many speak Spanish, but every visitor interaction you will have works in English.
