Albania · when to go

Best Time to Visit Albania

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Albania is two countries as far as the calendar is concerned: a Mediterranean coast and an alpine interior, keeping different seasons within a couple of hours of each other. That makes the question of when to come less about the weather in general than about which half of the country you are coming for. This page sets out the year month by month, the dates that fill the rooms, the window when the northern mountains are open at all, and the pattern of what things cost — without inventing figures that would be out of date before you read them.

The short answer

Go in late May, June or September. Those are the weeks when everything in Albania works at once: the Ionian is warm enough to swim in, the Riviera is not yet full or has just emptied, Berat and Gjirokaster are comfortable to climb rather than punishing, and the mountain trails in the north are open and clear. July and August are hot and the southern coast is genuinely crowded. October is quiet and lovely inland with the sea still swimmable early on. From November to March the coast is mild and wet with much of the beach trade shut, the cities are perfectly good and very cheap, and the northern passes are under snow. If you only care about the beach, come between mid-June and mid-September; if you care about the towns and the mountains, come either side of that.

Albania month by month

January and February

The wettest and coolest part of the year on the coast, though mild by northern European standards — this is a Mediterranean winter of grey days, heavy rain and green hillsides rather than a frozen one. Tirana carries on as normal and is cheap. Berat and Gjirokaster are atmospheric and almost empty, with the risk that a cold snap makes the cobbles slippery. In the north it is winter proper: snow on the Accursed Mountains, the high passes shut, Theth largely closed up. A good time for the cities and museums and a poor one for the sea.

March

The turn. Rain eases through the month, the almond and then the Judas trees come out, and the hills are as green as they ever get. Summer Day on 14 March is an old Albanian spring festival and a public holiday, biggest in Elbasan, and worth catching if you are there. The sea is still too cold. Prices are at their lowest, the stone towns are at their best for walking, and the mountains are still shut.

April

A genuinely good month inland and an unreliable one on the coast. Wildflowers are everywhere, Butrint and Apollonia are green and empty, and the Vjosa and the southern rivers are running full. Expect some rain and some perfect days. Beach places begin to open towards the end of the month but the water is cold. Orthodox and Catholic Easter both fall in this part of the year and move from year to year, so check the date before you fix a trip around it.

May

The month that starts the good season, and the one we would pick for a first trip that is not mainly about swimming. Warm without being hot, long days, everything open, the Riviera road quiet, the stone towns pleasant, and the northern trails opening from the middle of the month as the snow goes off the passes. By the last week the sea is swimmable for most people. Prices are still low. If you want one month and can choose freely, this or September.

June

The best all-round month in the country. Hot but not yet fierce, the Ionian warm and clear, the Riviera busy without being overwhelmed, and the Theth-to-Valbona walk reliably open. Everything is running and nothing is yet at capacity. Towards the end of the month the coast begins to fill and prices start to climb, so the first three weeks are the sweet spot.

July

Hot, dry and busy. Inland temperatures in Berat, Gjirokaster and the Tirana plain get genuinely uncomfortable in the middle of the day, which is worth knowing before you plan to climb to a castle at noon. The coast is full: Ksamil, Himare and Saranda are at their peak and need booking well ahead. The mountains are at their best for walking. If you are coming for the beach and must come in summer, the first half of July is less crushed than what follows.

August

The hottest and by a distance the most crowded month, and the one we would steer you away from unless you have no choice. The Albanian diaspora comes home in August, so the coast fills from inside the country as well as from outside it; Ksamil in particular is at capacity, rooms are at their dearest and the Riviera road is slow. It is a fine month to be in the mountains and a hard one to be on the beach. If you come, book everything in advance and start your days early.

September

Our pick, level with June and better for the sea. The water is at its warmest after a summer of heating, the crowds thin out quickly after the first week, prices come off, the light turns long and gold, and the inland towns are comfortable again. The mountain trails are still open and the air is clearer than in August. If one month had to stand for the whole country, this is it.

October

Quiet and underrated. The first half can still give you swimming weather on the Ionian, and the whole month is excellent for Berat, Gjirokaster, Butrint and the Korce highlands, which come into their autumn colour. Beach businesses begin to shut from the middle of the month and the rain returns towards the end. The northern passes stay open in a normal year but the weather turns quickly up there, so leave margin.

November and December

The wet season sets in on the coast and the beach towns largely close, but Tirana is busy, inexpensive and lively, and the stone towns are quiet and cheap. 28 November is Independence and Flag Day — the declaration was made at Vlore in 1912 — with 29 November, Liberation Day, immediately after it: two public holidays together, flags everywhere and a genuinely national weekend, and the one date in winter worth planning a trip around. The mountains are shut in by snow from December.

The weather, and what it does to a trip

Albania is really two climates in one small country, and the mistake visitors make is to plan for the first and then go and stand in the second. The coast and the lowlands are straightforwardly Mediterranean: hot dry summers, mild wet winters, and most of the year’s rain falling between October and March. Inland and upward it turns continental and then alpine — about three quarters of the country is hill or mountain, rising to Mount Korab at 2,764 metres — so the north has cold snowy winters, a late spring and a short, reliable summer walking season.

Three practical consequences. First, the gap between the coast and the mountains in the same week can be enormous, so a trip that takes in both needs layers and should not assume beach weather in Theth. Second, summer heat inland is a real constraint rather than a detail: Berat and Gjirokaster are built to be climbed, and in July and August that is early-morning and late-afternoon work. Third, the northern passes and the high trails are a season rather than a destination — broadly late June to September, with the shoulders depending entirely on the year’s snow, which is why we will not print opening dates for them. Ask locally the week you travel.

We do not print temperature or rainfall figures on this page. Monthly averages for a country this mountainous are an average of places that have nothing in common with one another, and a traveller who plans around them gets a nasty surprise at altitude. The shape of the year above is what is actually useful; for the forecast on your dates, use a forecast.

Dates worth planning around

Summer Day — 14 March

Dita e Veres, an old Albanian spring festival older than any of the country’s religions, and a public holiday. It is biggest in Elbasan, where it is essentially the town’s own day, and marked with sweets made for the occasion. A fixed date every year.

Orthodox and Catholic Easter, and the Muslim calendar

Albania is a religiously mixed and famously relaxed country — the 2023 census put Islam at about half the population, Christianity at about a sixth and no religion at about a sixth — and the public holidays reflect all of it: both Easters, Eid al-Fitr and Eid al-Adha, and the Bektashi new year on 22 March. All but the last move from year to year, so check the dates for your travel year. None of them will stop a visitor doing anything, but banks and offices close.

Independence and Flag Day — 28 November

The big one. Independence from the Ottoman Empire was declared at Vlore on 28 November 1912, and the day is followed immediately by Liberation Day on the 29th, so the end of November is a two-day national weekend with flags on every balcony. Vlore and Tirana are where to be. Expect full hotels in both and book ahead.

Gjirokaster’s folklore festival

A national festival of Albanian folk music and dress held in the castle at Gjirokaster, and the best single event in the country if you can catch it. It runs every few years rather than annually and the schedule has not always been kept to, so do not build a trip around it without confirming from an Albanian source that it is on in your year — which is why we print no date for it.

The August diaspora

Not a festival, but the single most important date on this page for anyone booking a room. Albanians living in Italy, Greece, Germany, the United Kingdom and North America come home in August, which is why the coast is fuller than the tourist numbers alone would suggest. If you are travelling in August, book accommodation and the Corfu ferry well in advance.

What it costs, and when

Albania is one of the least expensive countries in Europe for a visitor, and the shape of the year matters more here than the headline. We do not print figures, because a figure a reader plans around ought to come from the booking page rather than from us, and prices on this coast have been moving fast for several years. The pattern, though, is stable and worth knowing.

The dearest weeks by a wide margin are late July and August on the southern coast, and Ksamil is the sharpest case: a small place with a fixed number of beds and more demand every year. June and September cost appreciably less for better conditions, which is the single most useful piece of planning advice on this page. Tirana barely has a season — it is a working capital, and its rooms cost much the same in February as in July, which makes a winter city break here good value. Berat, Gjirokaster and the interior are inexpensive all year and cheapest between November and March. In the north, guesthouses in Theth and Valbona are only open for the walking season at all, so there is no low season to find.

Two things that are not room rates but shape a budget. A hire car and fuel are a significant share of the cost of an Albanian trip, because the country has no useful passenger railway and the good parts are spread along roads — and in August the hire fleet is fully booked, so late booking costs more than early booking. And the Saranda–Corfu ferry, which many southern trips use, is seasonal, variable and the thing most likely to be sold out on your date.

Fishing here

The fishing year in Albania runs much as the travelling year does: the big border lakes — Ohrid at Pogradec, Skadar above Shkoder and the Prespa lakes — fish through the warmer months, the northern mountain rivers come into their own once the snowmelt has run off in late spring, and the Ionian and Adriatic are a summer and autumn business from a small boat. Lake Ohrid has endemic trout and protective rules of its own, the Vjosa has been a national park since 2023 with the restrictions that brings, and licences and seasons are handled locally and change, so arrange it with a local guide on the ground rather than from abroad. Getting in: what applies, and what does not

Albania is not in the European Union and not in the Schengen area, so it runs its own border. United States citizens may enter as tourists without a visa and may stay up to one year without a residence permit; your passport must be valid for at least three months from the day you arrive and have a blank page for the stamp. Most other Western nationalities are admitted visa-free for shorter periods, usually ninety days, so check your own before you travel. The United States currently rates Albania Level 2, exercise increased caution, in an advisory issued on 26 August 2026 on account of crime; read the current text at travel.state.gov before you go, because both the level and the wording change.

If your trip also crosses into Greece, Italy or anywhere else in the Schengen area — and many Albanian trips arrive by way of Corfu, Bari or Athens — the following applies to that crossing rather than to Albania:

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Albania’s seasons — questions

What is the single best month to visit Albania?

September, with June a very close second. September gives you the warmest sea of the year, a Riviera that empties out after the first week, prices below the summer peak, comfortable walking weather in Berat and Gjirokaster, and mountain trails in the north that are still open. June gives you almost the same conditions with a cooler sea and slightly longer days. Either one beats July and August on every count except the certainty of sunshine.

How bad is August really?

On the southern coast it is the real thing: Ksamil, Himare and Saranda are at capacity, rooms are at their dearest, the Riviera road is slow and the beaches are full. This is partly because the Albanian diaspora comes home in August, so the crowd is domestic as well as foreign. Inland it is simply hot — climbing to a castle in Gjirokaster at midday in August is hard work. The mountains in the north, by contrast, are at their best. If you must come in August, book everything early and plan your days around the middle of the day rather than through it.

When can I actually swim?

Most people will find the Ionian comfortable from about the last week of May through to early or mid-October, with the warmest water in late August and September rather than in July. The Adriatic beaches around Durres are shallower and warm up a little earlier but are less clear. Outside that window the sea is swimmable for the hardy and the beach businesses are shut, which matters more than the temperature.

When are the northern mountains open?

Broadly late June to September for the Theth-to-Valbona walk and the high trails, with the shoulders depending entirely on how much snow the winter left. Guesthouses in Theth and Valbona largely open for that season and close outside it, and the Lake Koman ferry runs to a seasonal timetable that changes. We deliberately print no opening dates: ask locally in the week you travel, or through your Shkoder hotel, rather than trusting a date from any website.

Is winter worth it at all?

For the cities, yes, and it is good value. Tirana is a working capital that barely has a low season in character even though its rooms are cheap, and Berat and Gjirokaster in winter are quiet, atmospheric and very inexpensive. What winter is not good for is the coast, where it is mild but wet and most beach businesses are closed, or the north, which is snowed in. Pack for rain rather than for cold.

Does the weather differ much across the country?

More than almost anywhere else its size in Europe. The coast is Mediterranean and the interior is mountainous — about three quarters of Albania is hill or mountain, up to Mount Korab at 2,764 metres — so the same week can be a warm beach day at Ksamil and cold rain on a pass in the north. Any trip that takes in both needs layers, and no monthly average will describe both halves of it, which is why this page gives you the shape of the year instead of figures.

Which public holidays will get in my way?

Very few, but two are worth knowing. 28 and 29 November — Independence and Flag Day, then Liberation Day — is a national two-day weekend when Vlore and Tirana fill up. Summer Day on 14 March is a fixed public holiday, biggest in Elbasan. The religious holidays — both Easters, the two Eids, and the Bektashi new year on 22 March — close banks and offices, and all but the last move from year to year, so check the dates for your travel year.
