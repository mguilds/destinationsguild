Stockholm

Where to Stay in Stockholm

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Stockholm is built across fourteen islands where a very large lake empties into the Baltic, and that geography is the whole problem of choosing a hotel here: neighbourhoods that look adjacent on a map can be a bridge and a hill apart, and almost every one of them has water on at least two sides. The short version: Norrmalm around the central station is the convenient choice, Gamla Stan is the atmospheric one, Södermalm is the enjoyable one and Östermalm is the calm one. Below is what each is actually like to walk out of in the morning.

Grand Hôtel Stockholm

Södra Blasieholmshamnen 8, Stockholm. The address is the point: the hotel stands on the quay at Blasieholmen looking straight across the water at the Royal Palace and the old town, which is the view most visitors come to Stockholm to photograph and very few get from their room. It is a few minutes’ walk from the National Museum, the opera and the bridge into Gamla Stan. Norrmalm — the central station, Drottninggatan and the waterfront

Most convenientStep-freeBest for a short stay

The business and shopping heart of the city on the mainland side, and the right choice for a first visit or a single night between flights. The airport train arrives here, every metro line crosses here, the buses and the long-distance trains leave from here, and the City Hall, the opera and the bridge into the old town are all a few flat minutes on foot. It is also where the large international chains are, which is why weekend and July rates in this area are often the best value in Stockholm. The honest drawback is that Norrmalm is a working district rather than a beautiful one: the blocks around Sergels torg are concrete and busy, and the streets immediately behind the station are the scruffiest part of the centre.

Gamla Stan and Skeppsholmen — the old town

Most atmosphericCobbles and stepsBusy by day

The medieval island between the lake and the sea, with the Royal Palace, the cathedral, the narrowest lane in the city and a great many ochre and rust-coloured houses leaning into one another. Staying here is a genuine pleasure in the evening, when the day visitors have gone and the lanes empty out, and it puts you ten minutes’ walk from most of what you came to see. Three things to weigh: the hotels are mostly small, old and priced for the address; the streets are cobbled, narrow and stepped, which matters if stairs or wheels are a consideration; and between about ten and five it is the most crowded square mile in Sweden. Quiet little Skeppsholmen, one bridge east, is the exception to all three.

Södermalm — where Stockholm eats

Restaurants and barsBest viewsHills

The big island south of the old town, and the part of the city most visitors end up liking best: independent shops, coffee bars, second-hand stores and a very large number of good restaurants, particularly around Södermalmstorg, Nytorget and the streets behind Hötorget’s southern twin, Medborgarplatsen. It also has the views — the cliff path at Monteliusvägen and the terrace at Fjällgatan look straight back across the water at the old town, and cost nothing. It is one or two metro stops from the centre, or a twenty-minute walk over two bridges. The catch is in the name: Södermalm sits on a ridge, and the northern edge rises steeply from the water by stairs and lifts.

Östermalm and Djurgården — the quiet, grand east

ResidentialMuseumsFamily-friendly

East of the centre, this is the handsome, settled Stockholm of wide boulevards, nineteenth-century apartment blocks, embassies and the city’s best food hall at Östermalms Saluhall. Immediately beyond it lies Djurgården, the former royal hunting park that now holds the Vasa museum, Skansen and the other collections most people come to Stockholm for — so an Östermalm base puts the museums within walking distance and the nightlife at arm’s length. Rooms tend to be larger for the money than in Norrmalm and the streets are quiet at night. The trade-off is that you will take a tram or a bus into the middle most days rather than walking out of the door into it.

Getting around

One ticket covers the metro, the buses, the trams, the local trains and the commuter boats, and it is sold by time rather than by journey, so a single fare lets you change between all of them within seventy-five minutes. Buy it in the transport app or with a contactless card at the barrier; there is no cash on board anywhere in the system. The metro — the tunnelbana — is the backbone, runs from early morning to past midnight with night services at weekends, and is worth riding for its own sake: a good many of the stations are carved out of bare rock and painted, and the blue line in particular is an art gallery you pass through on the way to dinner.

In summer the commuter boats are the pleasure. Scheduled services leave the central quays for the archipelago islands and for Djurgården all day, cost very little on an ordinary transport ticket for the short hops, and give you the city from the water for the price of a bus ride.

Walking is the right way to see the middle, with the caveat that Stockholm is a city of bridges and ridges rather than a flat one. The waterfront from the City Hall through the old town to Skeppsholmen is level and continuous; the climb up on to Södermalm is not, though public lifts at Katarinahissen and Mariahissen take the sting out of it. Taxis are plentiful, unregulated in price and genuinely expensive — agree the fare or use an app rather than hailing one at a rank.

When to come, and what it costs

High season is from the middle of June to the middle of August, when the archipelago boats run their full timetable, the outdoor restaurants are open and the city is at its best — and when room rates in the old town and Östermalm are at their highest. The oddity of Stockholm is July: the Swedes themselves leave for their summer houses, so the city is quieter than you would expect at the peak of the year and the business hotels in Norrmalm drop their rates while the atmospheric small ones do not.

Late August and September are the best value of the warm half of the year, with long light, warm water and thinner crowds. From late October the evenings shut down early and the city turns inward to its cafes and museums, which is no bad thing; December brings the Christmas markets and the St Lucia processions, and prices spike over the holidays themselves. January to March is cheapest, darkest and — with clip-on studs for your shoes, which cost very little in any pharmacy — perfectly manageable.

Two practical notes on cost. Hotel breakfast is usually included and usually substantial enough to carry you to the evening, which is worth more here than it sounds. And the single biggest saving available to a visitor is lunch: most city-centre restaurants serve a fixed-price dagens lunch on weekdays at a fraction of their evening prices, often with bread, salad and coffee thrown in.

Sweden uses the Swedish krona (SEK, written kr), not the euro, and is one of the most nearly cashless countries in the world — a great many cafés, buses and museums take cards only and will politely refuse notes, so bring a card you can tap and do not bother changing money. Wine, spirits and stronger beer are sold only through the state shops, Systembolaget, which keep short hours and are shut on Sundays; weaker beer is in the supermarkets. Tipping is not expected and never chased — rounding up a bill is plenty. Decline any card machine that offers to charge you in dollars; pay in kronor and let your own bank convert.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS — the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years — is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today — nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave. Sweden is in both the European Union and the Schengen area, so arriving from another Schengen country there is no passport control at all.

Staying in Stockholm — questions

Which part of Stockholm should I stay in?

For a first visit of two or three nights, Norrmalm around the central station: everything connects there, the walk into the old town is ten flat minutes and the chain hotels are the best value in the city at weekends and in July. For atmosphere, Gamla Stan, accepting cobbles, stairs and daytime crowds. For restaurants and the best views, Sodermalm. For quiet, space and the museums on the doorstep, Ostermalm. All four are within half an hour of each other on foot or one or two stops on the metro, so the choice is about what you want to walk out into rather than about access.

How do I get from Arlanda airport into the city?

The Arlanda Express train runs non-stop to the central station in under twenty minutes and is step-free end to end; it is the fastest way and the dearest. The commuter train on the same line takes a little longer and costs considerably less. Airport coaches run to the city terminal beside the central station and take about forty-five minutes depending on traffic. A taxi is a fixed-price arrangement with most firms rather than a meter - agree the price before you get in. Note that some budget flights use Skavsta or Vasteras, which are both a long way from Stockholm and served only by coach.

Is Stockholm walkable?

The middle of it very much so, with one qualification: it is a city of islands, bridges and ridges rather than a flat grid. Norrmalm, Gamla Stan and the waterfront are level and connected by bridges you can cross on foot in a few minutes. Getting up on to Sodermalm from the water means a climb, though the public lifts at Katarinahissen and Mariahissen do the work for you. Djurgarden and the museums are a pleasant walk or a short tram ride from Ostermalm. If stairs or uneven ground are a problem, base yourself in Norrmalm and use the metro, which is largely step-free at the central stations.

Do I need to book the archipelago in advance?

Not for a day trip. The scheduled boats to the nearer islands run frequently through the summer and you can simply turn up at the quay, buy a ticket and get on. What does need booking ahead is staying out there: the islands have very few beds, they fill up months in advance for July and for Midsummer, and the last boat back is earlier than visitors expect. If you only have a day, go to Vaxholm or Fjaderholmarna; if you have longer and want quiet, Grinda or Sandhamn.

Is Stockholm expensive?

It is not cheap, but it is a clear step below Oslo and roughly comparable with other large northern European capitals. Hotels are the main cost and move sharply with the season. What takes the sting out of it is that a great deal of the best of Stockholm is cheap or free: the archipelago boats on an ordinary transport ticket, the art in the metro stations, the island walks, the views from Monteliusvagen. Eat the weekday fixed-price lunch rather than a full dinner, take the included hotel breakfast seriously, and buy your wine - if at all - from Systembolaget rather than a restaurant list.

How many days do I need in Stockholm?

Three full days covers the old town, the Vasa and one other museum on Djurgarden, a walk on Sodermalm and an afternoon on the water. Four or five lets you add a proper day in the archipelago and a trip out to Drottningholm palace or up to Uppsala, forty minutes away by train. Fewer than two and you will see the old town and the Vasa and little else, which is a shame in a city whose pleasure is largely in wandering between its islands.
