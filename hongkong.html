Why visit Hong Kong

Few cities pack this much energy, food, scenery and sheer spectacle into so small a space — or make it feel this effortless to get around.

The greatest skyline

A wall of skyscrapers rising straight from the water, the neon-lit Symphony of Lights, and the sweeping view from Victoria Peak — one of the world's unforgettable cityscapes.

Food, from cart to Michelin

Dim sum trolleys and roast-goose canteens, dai pai dong street stalls, egg tarts and milk tea — and more Michelin stars than almost anywhere in Asia.

East meets West

Cantonese temples and colonial trading houses, double-decker trams and glass towers, Chinese New Year and afternoon tea — a culture all its own.

City and wild in one

Beyond the towers lie green country parks, hiking trails, quiet beaches and outlying islands — the Big Buddha on Lantau is a short cable-car ride from downtown.

How the territory divides up

Hong Kong Island

The historic and financial heart. Central stacks glass towers, luxury hotels and rooftop bars above the ferry piers, with the antique shops of Sheung Wan to the west and the Peak Tram climbing to the territory's most famous view. East along the tramline, Wan Chai and Causeway Bay trade finance for late-night bars, dai pai dong and non-stop shopping, with horse racing at Happy Valley. Our Hong Kong Island hotels guide covers where to stay.

Kowloon

Across the harbour, Tsim Sha Tsui owns the postcard skyline view, the Star Ferry pier, the museums and the Avenue of Stars, while chaotic, wonderful Mong Kok packs in some of the world's densest street markets. See our Kowloon hotels guide for the best bases.

The New Territories

North of Kowloon, the New Territories stretch to the mainland border — a mix of new towns, walled villages, wetlands famous for migrating birds, and country parks laced with hiking trails. Most visitors dip in for a temple, a market town or a day hike rather than staying overnight, but it is where you see Hong Kong beyond the towers.

The outlying islands

More than two hundred islands ring the territory. Lantau, the largest, holds the airport, Disneyland, the Big Buddha and the Ngong Ping cable car; car-free Cheung Chau and laid-back Lamma offer seafood, beaches and easy walks — all a short ferry from Central.

Top things to do in Hong Kong

The experiences worth building a trip around.

The Peak at dusk

Ride the historic Peak Tram up Victoria Peak and time it for sunset, when the skyline lights up and the whole harbour glitters below. Book tram tickets ahead in high season.

Cross on the Star Ferry

A few coins buys the best-value cruise in Asia — the ten-minute green-and-white ferry between Central and Tsim Sha Tsui, with the skyline on every side.

Yum cha & dim sum

Push a trolley's worth of har gow, siu mai and barbecue-pork buns at a bustling tea house, then chase egg tarts and milk tea through the markets.

The Big Buddha, Lantau

Glide over the hills on the Ngong Ping cable car to the giant seated Tian Tan Buddha and Po Lin Monastery — a serene half-day out of the city.

Kowloon's night markets

Temple Street after dark, the Ladies' Market and the goldfish and flower streets of Mong Kok — bargaining, street food and neon at full tilt.

Symphony of Lights

At 8pm the towers on both shores put on a laser-and-light show across Victoria Harbour — watch from the Tsim Sha Tsui promenade or a harbour cruise.

Best time to visit & getting there

When to go

October to early December is the sweet spot: warm, dry and clear, with comfortable temperatures and the year's cleanest skies — ideal for the Peak, the harbour and hiking alike.

Mid-December to February is cool and mild, good for shopping, festivals and Chinese New Year, though a jacket helps on the coldest days.

May to September is hot, humid and the height of the typhoon season, with heavy downpours and the odd storm; March and April are warm but often foggy and grey. See our full month-by-month guide.

Getting there

Hong Kong International Airport (HKG) on Lantau is one of the world's busiest hubs, with direct flights from across the globe and a fast Airport Express train into the city in about 24 minutes.

Once you arrive, the MTR metro, double-decker trams, buses, the Star Ferry and famously cheap taxis make getting around effortless — a stored-value Octopus card covers almost all of it.

The currency is the Hong Kong dollar. Cards and mobile payments work almost everywhere; carry some cash for markets and street stalls. Always check your current visa and passport-validity requirements before booking.

Hong Kong travel FAQ

When is the best time to visit Hong Kong?

October to early December is the sweet spot — warm, dry and pleasantly clear, with comfortable temperatures around 22–28°C and the year's cleanest skies. Winter (mid-December to February) is cool, mild and good for shopping and festivals. Spring (March–April) turns humid and foggy, and summer (May–September) is hot, sticky and the height of the typhoon season, when heavy rain and the occasional storm can disrupt travel.

What currency is used in Hong Kong?

The Hong Kong dollar (HKD, symbol HK$), which is pegged to the US dollar at around 7.8 to 1. Cards and mobile payments are accepted almost everywhere, but a stored-value Octopus card is the easy way to pay for the MTR, trams, ferries, buses and convenience stores. Carry some cash for market stalls, dai pai dong food stalls and small shops. ATMs are everywhere; decline any machine that offers to charge you in your home currency, which costs more.

Hong Kong Island or Kowloon — where should I stay?

Both sit on Victoria Harbour, minutes apart by MTR or the Star Ferry, so you can't go wrong. Hong Kong Island — Central, Admiralty, Wan Chai and Causeway Bay — is the polished side: finance towers, rooftop bars, the Peak Tram and the grandest luxury hotels. Kowloon — Tsim Sha Tsui and Mong Kok — is louder and more street-level, with the classic harbourfront skyline view, museums, night markets and generally better value. Many visitors split their stay between the two.

How many days do you need in Hong Kong?

Three to four days is enough for a first visit to see the headline sights — the Peak, the harbour and the Star Ferry, the temples and markets of Kowloon, and a dim sum lunch or two. Add a day or two for the outlying islands and the Big Buddha on Lantau, a beach or a hike, or a side trip to Macau, and five to six days makes a relaxed, well-rounded trip.

Is Hong Kong safe for travellers?

Hong Kong is one of the safest major cities in the world, with very low rates of violent crime and a fast, easy-to-use public transport system. The usual city sense applies — watch for pickpockets in crowded markets and on busy trains, and use licensed red taxis or the MTR. Traffic drives on the left, so look both ways. Check your government's current travel advisory and entry requirements before you go.
