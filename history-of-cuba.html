The History of Cuba

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

From the Taíno to the treasure fleets, the pirates and the Havana forts, sugar and slavery, two wars for independence, the Revolution and the Cold War — and where the island stands today. Every fortress, plaza and plantation house you will see in Cuba belongs to this story. Knowing it turns a visit into something much deeper.

The first Cubans

Before 1492

People were living in Cuba thousands of years before any European arrived. The oldest groups, often called the Guanahatabey and the Ciboney, were fishers and gatherers who lived along the coasts and in caves, and by 1492 they had been pushed largely to the far west of the island.

The great majority of Cubans that Columbus met were the Taíno, a farming and seafaring people whose ancestors had moved up the chain of Caribbean islands from South America. Estimates of their numbers in 1492 run from about 100,000 to several hundred thousand. They lived in villages of round thatched houses called bohíos, led by chiefs called caciques; they grew cassava, maize, sweet potatoes and tobacco, played a ball game in open plazas, and travelled between islands in large dugout canoes.

The world still uses their words. Hurricane, canoe, hammock, barbecue and savanna all came into English through Spanish from the Taíno language, and so, probably, did tobacco. The word bohío is still how rural Cubans describe a palm-thatched country house.

Columbus and the Spanish conquest

1492 – 1550

On 28 October 1492, two weeks after his first landfall in the Bahamas, Christopher Columbus came ashore on Cuba’s north-east coast, at a bay generally identified as Bariay in today’s Holguín province. He wrote in his log that he had never seen a more beautiful place. He was also certain he had reached Asia, and on his second voyage in 1494, after sailing far along the south coast, he had his crew swear a formal statement that Cuba was part of the mainland. He died in 1506 without accepting otherwise.

The conquest came later. In 1511 Diego Velázquez de Cuéllar crossed from Hispaniola with about three hundred men. Over the next five years he founded the seven original Spanish towns, several of which you can still walk through:

The Taíno did not give up quietly. Their best-remembered leader, Hatuey, was a cacique who had fled from Hispaniola to warn the people of Cuba what conquest meant. He led a guerrilla resistance in the eastern mountains until he was captured and burned alive near Yara in 1512. The friar Bartolomé de las Casas, who was in Cuba at the time, recorded that when Hatuey was offered baptism so that he might go to heaven, he asked whether Spaniards went there too — and on being told they did, said he would rather not go. Cubans remember him as the island’s first national hero; you will see his face on the label of Hatuey beer.

Under the encomienda system, conquerors were granted the labour of indigenous communities, nominally in return for protecting them and teaching them the Christian faith. In practice it was forced labour, mostly panning rivers for gold. Overwork, violence and above all European diseases such as smallpox, against which the islanders had no immunity, destroyed Taíno society within about two generations. Las Casas, who had himself held an encomienda in Cuba, gave it up and spent the rest of his life campaigning against the system. Taíno people did not vanish entirely — their descendants married into the colonial population, and DNA studies find their ancestry in many Cubans today — but their world was gone.

Treasure fleets, pirates and the forts of Havana

1550 – 1763

Cuba’s gold soon ran out. Its real wealth turned out to be its position. Havana’s deep, sheltered harbour sat right at the mouth of the Gulf of Mexico, where the Gulf Stream carries ships out into the Atlantic. From the 1560s, Spain’s annual treasure fleets — carrying the silver of Mexico and Peru home to Seville — gathered in Havana before the crossing. That made Havana the most important port in Spanish America, and the most tempting target in the Caribbean.

The first blow came in 1555, when the French corsair Jacques de Sores took the town, and when the ransom he demanded was not paid, burned it to the ground. Spain’s answer was one of the great building programmes of the age:

The forts worked: no enemy ever again took Havana by sailing into its harbour. But pirates, privateers and rival navies kept Cuba’s coasts and smaller towns under threat for more than a century. Some of the names connected with Cuba’s waters:

Sir Francis Drake

The Spanish called him El Draque, the Dragon. In 1586, fresh from sacking Santo Domingo and Cartagena, his fleet sailed past Havana — and, finding it too well defended, did not attack. The scare helped persuade Spain to build El Morro.

Piet Heyn

In 1628 this Dutch admiral did what every raider dreamed of: he trapped the Spanish silver fleet in Matanzas Bay, east of Havana, and captured almost all of it. It paid for years of the Dutch war against Spain.

Henry Morgan

The Welsh buccaneer who later became lieutenant governor of Jamaica. In 1668 he marched inland and plundered Puerto Príncipe — today’s Camagüey — a raid often given as one reason the town grew its famously confusing streets.

François l’Olonnais

Perhaps the cruellest of them all. According to the buccaneer-chronicler Exquemelin, when the governor of Havana sent a ship to hunt him down, he captured it and beheaded its crew, sparing one man to carry the news back.

Calico Jack, Anne Bonny & Mary Read

John Rackham’s crew — famous for two women who fought as hard as any man aboard — worked the waters between Cuba and Jamaica and sheltered on Cuba’s coast. They were captured off Jamaica in 1720.

The British Navy, 1762

Not pirates at all, but the most successful attack of the lot. See below — it changed Cuba’s history.

The British year

In 1762, during the Seven Years’ War, a British expedition of some two hundred ships landed east of Havana. After a siege of about six weeks, British engineers blew a breach in El Morro’s wall with a mine on 30 July, and the city surrendered on 13 August. Britain held Havana for eleven months and threw it open to trade — far more ships came in than under Spain’s strict monopoly. In 1763 Britain handed Havana back in exchange for Florida. Spain built La Cabaña so it could never happen again, and loosened its trade rules. Havana’s merchants and planters never forgot what open trade had done for them.

Sugar, slavery and fortune

1763 – 1886

Cuba’s great boom began in the 1790s. The slave revolution in neighbouring Saint-Domingue — soon to become Haiti — destroyed what had been the world’s richest sugar colony, and Cuban planters rushed to fill the gap. Sugar, along with coffee and tobacco, made Cuba one of the most valuable colonies on earth, and by the mid-nineteenth century it was the world’s largest sugar producer. Cuba also got railways early: its first line, opened in 1837, ran before Spain itself had one.

That wealth was built on slavery. Historians estimate that roughly three-quarters of a million to a million enslaved Africans were brought to Cuba over the centuries, most of them in the sugar boom of the nineteenth century, and a large share after Spain had promised Britain it would end the trade. Conditions on the sugar estates during the harvest were among the harshest anywhere in the Americas. The trade was finally stopped in 1867, and slavery itself was abolished only in 1886; Brazil, in 1888, was the only country in the Americas to end it later.

Africa shaped Cuba as much as Spain did. Cuban music, dance, food and religion — son and rumba, the drums of Santería, the cooking of the east — all carry the inheritance of the people who were brought there in chains. The grand houses of Trinidad, the ruins of the sugar mills in the nearby Valle de los Ingenios, and the old merchant palaces of Havana are beautiful, and travellers should know how they were paid for.

The long road to independence

1868 – 1902

When the rest of Spain’s American empire broke away in the 1810s and 1820s, Cuba stayed. Spain kept a large army there, and many Cuban planters feared that a war for independence might become a slave rebellion like Haiti’s.

The break came on 10 October 1868, when the planter Carlos Manuel de Céspedes freed the enslaved workers on his estate, La Demajagua, near Manzanillo, and called for independence — the Grito de Yara. The Ten Years’ War that followed cost tens of thousands of lives. Spain shot Céspedes’ son and offered to spare him if his father surrendered; Céspedes refused, saying all Cubans were his sons, and was himself killed in 1874. The war ended in 1878 in a truce, without independence. Spain’s gradual abolition of slavery followed.

José Martí

The figure who stands above all others in Cuba is José Martí — poet, journalist and organiser. Born in Havana in 1853, he was sentenced to hard labour at sixteen for his politics and deported to Spain. He spent most of his adult life in exile, many years of it in New York, where in 1892 he founded the Cuban Revolutionary Party. He insisted that a free Cuba must belong to all its people, black and white alike.

“Man is more than white, more than mulatto, more than black.”— José Martí, “My Race”, 1893

Martí landed in eastern Cuba in April 1895 to join the new war for independence, and was killed in one of its first skirmishes, at Dos Ríos on 19 May 1895. He was forty-two. Every Cuban town has a statue of him, and Havana’s airport bears his name.

The war went on without him. To starve the rebels of support, the Spanish general Valeriano Weyler forced country people into fortified towns — the reconcentración — where perhaps 150,000 or more died of hunger and disease.

The Maine and the Spanish–American War

On 15 February 1898 the American battleship Maine blew up in Havana harbour, killing more than 260 of her crew. American newspapers blamed Spain; later investigations suggest an accidental fire in a coal bunker may have set off her magazines, though the cause has never been settled. “Remember the Maine” carried the United States into war that April. The decisive fighting was around Santiago de Cuba in July — including Theodore Roosevelt’s Rough Riders at the San Juan Heights — and Spain’s Caribbean fleet was destroyed as it tried to escape from Santiago Bay. In the Treaty of Paris that December, Spain gave up Cuba.

Cubans had fought for thirty years for independence, and many resented being left out of the peace. After three years of U.S. military government, Cuba was required to write the Platt Amendment into its 1901 constitution, giving the United States the right to intervene in Cuban affairs and to lease naval stations. The Republic of Cuba was proclaimed on 20 May 1902, with Tomás Estrada Palma as its first president.

The republic

1902 – 1958

In 1903 the United States leased Guantánamo Bay for a naval station, at $2,000 a year in gold coin. A 1934 treaty raised the rent to about $4,085 and made the lease last until both sides agree to end it. Since 1959 Cuba has called the base an illegal occupation and has refused to cash the rent cheques. It is still there.

The early republic was rich, unequal and unstable. American companies came to own much of the sugar land, the railways and the utilities; U.S. troops landed again more than once; and the governments were often corrupt. The building of the Capitolio in the 1920s, modelled loosely on the U.S. Capitol, belongs to these years, as do the grand hotels, casinos and Art Deco towers of Havana.

The dictatorship of Gerardo Machado fell in 1933. Weeks later a young army sergeant-stenographer named Fulgencio Batista led the “Sergeants’ Revolt” and became the real power in Cuba for most of the next quarter-century. The Platt Amendment was repealed in 1934. Batista was elected president in 1940 under a new and progressive constitution, and stepped down in 1944.

In March 1952, as he was trailing in the polls before a new election, Batista seized power in a coup and cancelled the vote. His second rule was harsher and more corrupt. Havana became famous for its casinos and nightclubs, some run with the American Mafia — Meyer Lansky’s Riviera hotel opened in 1957 — while much of the countryside stayed poor.

The Revolution

1953 – 1962

On 26 July 1953, a young lawyer named Fidel Castro led about 160 men in an attack on the Moncada Barracks in Santiago de Cuba. It failed badly; many of the attackers were killed, several after capture. At his trial, Castro made the speech that closed with the words his movement took as its banner:

“Condemn me. It does not matter. History will absolve me.”— Fidel Castro, at his trial, October 1953

Released in an amnesty in 1955, he went to Mexico, where he met the Argentine doctor Ernesto “Che” Guevara. On 2 December 1956 they landed in eastern Cuba with 82 men aboard the overloaded yacht Granma. The army caught them within days; only a small band survived to reach the Sierra Maestra mountains. From there, with growing support in the countryside and in the cities, they fought a guerrilla war for two years. Washington stopped arms sales to Batista in 1958.

At the end of December 1958, Che Guevara’s column took the city of Santa Clara, derailing an armoured train sent to reinforce it. In the early hours of 1 January 1959, Batista fled to the Dominican Republic. Castro entered Havana on 8 January to enormous crowds.

The new government moved fast. Land reform broke up the big estates; American and Cuban-owned companies, banks, sugar mills, refineries and hotels were nationalised, mostly without compensation. Thousands of political opponents were tried and many executed, and a first great wave of Cubans — many of them professionals and the middle class — left for the United States, the beginning of the Cuban-American community in Miami. Washington cut the sugar quota, imposed a partial embargo in October 1960, broke relations in January 1961 and made the embargo total in February 1962. Castro declared the revolution socialist, and Cuba turned to the Soviet Union.

The Bay of Pigs

On 17 April 1961, about 1,400 Cuban exiles trained and armed by the CIA landed at the Bay of Pigs on the south coast. The invasion collapsed within three days. More than 1,100 were captured; at the end of 1962 they were returned to the United States in exchange for about $53 million in food and medicine. Today Playa Girón, where they came ashore, has a museum — and the nearby Zapata wetlands are one of Cuba’s best places for birds.

The Missile Crisis

In October 1962 American spy planes photographed Soviet nuclear missile sites being built in Cuba. For thirteen days President Kennedy and Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchev stood closer to nuclear war than the world has been before or since, while the U.S. Navy blockaded the island. Khrushchev agreed to withdraw the missiles; in return the United States promised not to invade Cuba and, secretly, to remove its own missiles from Turkey. Castro, who had not been consulted, was furious.

Cold War Cuba and after

1962 – 2025

For three decades Cuba lived on Soviet support, and in return became a champion of revolution abroad. Che Guevara left to fight in the Congo and then Bolivia, where he was captured and executed in October 1967. Cuban troops fought in Angola and Ethiopia in the 1970s and 1980s. At home the government built universal free schooling and health care, while banning opposition parties and a free press.

Relations with the United States stayed bitter, and tragedy came from both directions. In 1976 a bomb planted by anti-Castro exiles destroyed Cubana Flight 455 off Barbados, killing all 73 aboard. In 1980, after thousands of Cubans crowded into the Peruvian embassy, Castro opened the port of Mariel, and some 125,000 people left by boat for Florida in six months.

When the Soviet Union collapsed in 1991, Cuba lost most of its trade overnight. In the “Special Period” that followed, the economy shrank by about a third; there were long blackouts, bicycles replaced buses, and people went hungry. To survive, the government opened the island to foreign tourism and joint-venture hotels — the beginning of the resorts of Varadero and the northern cays — and legalised the U.S. dollar for a time. In 1994, during the balsero crisis, some 35,000 Cubans set out for Florida on rafts.

On 24 February 1996, Cuban fighter jets shot down two unarmed civilian planes of the Miami exile group Brothers to the Rescue over the Florida Straits, killing four men. Weeks later Congress passed the Helms–Burton Act, which wrote the embargo into law, so that only Congress can lift it.

Fidel Castro handed power to his brother Raúl because of illness in 2006, formally in 2008. Raúl allowed Cubans to buy mobile phones, sell homes and cars and run small private businesses. On 17 December 2014, Presidents Obama and Raúl Castro announced that the two countries would restore relations; embassies reopened in 2015, and in March 2016 Obama became the first sitting U.S. president to visit since Calvin Coolidge in 1928. Fidel Castro died that November. From 2017 the first Trump administration reversed much of the opening, and in January 2021 Cuba was returned to the U.S. list of state sponsors of terrorism.

Miguel Díaz-Canel succeeded Raúl Castro as president in 2018 — the first leader since 1959 not named Castro — and as head of the Communist Party in 2021. On 11 July 2021 the largest protests in decades broke out across the island over food, medicine and power cuts; hundreds were arrested and many given long sentences. From 2022 an exodus began larger than Mariel or the rafters combined, and in October 2024 the national power grid collapsed for days.

Cuba today — November 2026

Where the story stands

Cuba is going through its hardest year since the Special Period of the 1990s, with several crises arriving at once.

The power is going out

Cuba’s old power plants run on imported oil, and for years most of it came from Venezuela. In January 2026, after the United States removed Venezuela’s Nicolás Maduro from power, those shipments stopped, and a U.S. executive order threatened tariffs on any country that supplies Cuba with oil. Nationwide blackouts followed, including complete grid failures, and outages of many hours a day remain common outside the centre of Havana.

The economy is shrinking fast

The United Nations’ economic commission for Latin America expects Cuba’s economy to shrink by about ten percent in 2026. Official inflation passed 28 percent in September. Food, fuel and medicine are scarce. Cuba’s parliament is reviewing a package of 176 reforms that the government calls its largest overhaul since the 1990s.

The people are leaving

Cuba’s own statistics office puts the population at about 9.4 million at the end of 2025, down from more than 11 million a decade earlier. Some 245,000 people left in 2025 alone, deaths now nearly double births, and most of those leaving are young.

Tourism has collapsed

Cuba once welcomed close to five million visitors a year. Industry reports put arrivals in the first half of 2026 at well under half of the year before. Spain’s Meliá, Iberostar and Barceló ended their Cuban operations in June and July, leaving the island without its last international hotel brands, and many resorts have closed to save fuel.

Washington has turned up the pressure

A May 2026 executive order widened U.S. sanctions; in June the United States sanctioned President Díaz-Canel himself, and in November it named further companies, including two Chinese firms. On 30 September new Treasury rules narrowed the legal reasons Americans may visit: group “people-to-people” tours and trips for professional conferences are no longer allowed, while family visits, journalism and “support for the Cuban people” travel remain, under strict conditions.

Talk of talks

Secretary of State Marco Rubio has said the United States is open to talks on a political transition in Cuba, while calling the chances of a deal with the present leadership low. In late October President Trump said he would send Rubio to Havana “to seal the deal.” The Cuban government denies that any talks are taking place, and its foreign minister was in China this month seeking support.

What it means if you go

Cuba’s music, architecture, beaches and people are as remarkable as ever, and Cubans welcome visitors warmly. But a trip today means planning for power cuts, shortages and a cash-only economy. American travellers must fit one of the legal travel categories and must not stay at or pay any business on the State Department’s restricted lists. Our Cuba guide sets out the rules and the practicalities.

Cuba has stood at a turning point before — in 1898, in 1959, in 1991. Few people who watch the island closely expect the present situation to last. What comes next, and how soon, is the great open question.

Cuba history FAQ

Who lived in Cuba before Columbus?

Several peoples. The most numerous in 1492 were the Taíno, farmers and seafarers who had come up the island chain from South America and lived in chiefdoms led by caciques. Older groups, often called the Ciboney and Guanahatabey, lived in the far west. Within about fifty years of Spanish conquest the indigenous population had collapsed, mostly from European diseases, forced labour and violence, though Taíno ancestry and words such as hurricane, canoe, hammock and barbecue survive.

Why were there so many forts in Havana?

Because Havana was where Spain’s treasure fleets gathered before crossing the Atlantic, which made it the richest target in the Caribbean. After the French corsair Jacques de Sores burned the town in 1555, Spain built the Castillo de la Real Fuerza, El Morro and La Punta, and later, after the British captured Havana in 1762, the vast Fortaleza de San Carlos de la Cabaña. A cannon is still fired from La Cabaña at nine o’clock every night.

When did Cuba become independent?

Spain gave up Cuba at the end of the Spanish–American War in 1898. After a period of U.S. military government, the Republic of Cuba was proclaimed on 20 May 1902, which Cubans long celebrated as Independence Day. The Platt Amendment, written into Cuba’s 1901 constitution, gave the United States the right to intervene in Cuban affairs until it was repealed in 1934.

What happened in the Cuban Revolution?

Fidel Castro’s 26th of July Movement fought a guerrilla war against the dictatorship of Fulgencio Batista from the Sierra Maestra mountains between 1956 and 1958. Batista fled on 1 January 1959 and Castro entered Havana on 8 January. The new government nationalised land and businesses, including American-owned property, and within two years had aligned Cuba with the Soviet Union.

What is the situation in Cuba in 2026?

Very difficult. After Venezuelan oil stopped arriving in January 2026, Cuba suffered repeated nationwide blackouts and severe fuel shortages. The UN’s economic commission for Latin America expects the economy to shrink by about ten percent this year, the population has fallen to about 9.4 million through emigration, and tourism has collapsed. The United States widened sanctions through 2026 and narrowed the legal reasons Americans may visit. Washington says it is open to talks on a political transition; Havana denies that any talks are taking place.
