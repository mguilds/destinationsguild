Best Time to Visit Finland

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Finland has two seasons worth travelling for and two that mostly catch people out, and which is which depends entirely on whether you are going north or south. The south is a Baltic country: grey and dark in midwinter, glorious from June to August. Lapland runs on a different calendar again — snow from about November to April, the sun gone for weeks at midwinter and never setting at midsummer — so the best month for a husky trip is one of the worst for a lake cabin, and the other way round. What follows is month by month on the light, the temperature, the snow and the price, in both halves of the country, with the holidays that shut the place without warning marked as you go.

The short answer

Four ways to choose, depending on what you are coming for.

For snow, dogs and the lights

Late January to mid-March, in Lapland. Deep reliable snow, the aurora season still running, and the days long enough by February to do something in daylight as well.

For the lakes and the long light

Late June to mid-August. Warm, green, swimmable and barely dark at all. Avoid the Midsummer weekend itself unless you have somewhere to be, because the country closes.

For the midnight sun

June into early July, north of the Arctic Circle. The sun does not set for weeks. Bring a sleep mask and do not trust your own sense of the time.

For value and quiet

September, and early May in the south. Autumn colour in the north in September, white nights returning in May, prices down and almost nobody about.

Month by month

What each month is actually like, in both halves of the country, written for a visitor rather than for a weather chart.

January

The dark end of the year. The south gets about six hours of low grey light and hovers around freezing; Lapland is properly cold, often fifteen to twenty-five degrees below, with snow on everything and only a blue twilight at noon in the far north. It is the best month for cold-weather activities and among the best for the aurora, and the cheapest weeks of the winter are the ones straight after New Year.

February

The strongest all-round winter month. The snow is at its deepest, the days are lengthening quickly, the cold is drier and easier to dress for than people fear, and the aurora season is in full swing. Lapland is busy with school holidays from several countries, so book ahead; the south is quiet and sees little of the winter trade.

March

Our pick for a winter trip. Long bright days, full snow cover in the north, dazzling sun on white ground and temperatures that have stopped being severe. Everything in Lapland is still running and the lights are still out. In the south the thaw begins and the cities turn to slush and grit, which is the least attractive version of Finland there is.

April

A month of two halves and the hardest to advise on. Lapland holds its snow well into it and the spring skiing is excellent; the south is brown, wet and waiting. Easter is a public holiday with real closures, and the ski resorts are at their busiest for it. By the end of the month the ice is going off the lakes and nothing much is open at the water yet.

May

Spring arrives, suddenly, and the country visibly cheers up. Trees come into leaf in the south, the light stretches to late in the evening, terraces open and prices are low. The first of May — Vappu — is a proper public holiday and a student carnival. Water is still too cold for swimming and the cottage season has not quite begun, but cities are at their nicest.

June

The great month in the south. Long warm evenings that barely go dark, lakes beginning to warm, everything open and the countryside at its best. Midsummer, on the Friday and Saturday closest to the twenty-fourth, is the biggest holiday of the Finnish year: cities empty, a great many shops and restaurants shut, and transport thins out. North of the Arctic Circle the sun does not set at all.

July

The warmest and the busiest month, in Finnish terms — which still means around twenty to twenty-five degrees rather than real heat. Lakes are swimmable, the archipelago is at its best, cottages are full and the opera festival runs in the castle at Savonlinna. It is also peak mosquito season inland and in Lapland; take repellent seriously rather than dismissing it.

August

Warm at the start and noticeably autumnal by the end, and in our view the best of the three summer months. Water is at its warmest, the mosquitoes fade, the crayfish season starts and Finnish schools go back in the middle of the month, which takes the pressure off cottages and hotels. From the last week, the first faint chance of the aurora returns in the north.

September

The quiet reward month. Autumn colour — ruska — sweeps down Lapland through the first half, the hiking is superb in cool clear air, there are no insects left, and the aurora is properly back with enough darkness to see it. Prices fall everywhere. Cottages and some northern businesses begin to close for the season towards the end.

October

The gap month. The colour has gone, the snow has not arrived, Lapland is dark and brown and many seasonal operators shut between seasons. The cities carry on perfectly well and are cheap, and the aurora is active, but a trip planned around snow in October is a trip planned on hope. If you must come now, come for Helsinki rather than for Lapland.

November

Dark, and the one month we would tell a first-time visitor to avoid. The south has short grey wet days; Lapland may have snow from the middle of the month or may not, and the Christmas trade has not started. The exceptions are the ski resorts that open on stored snow, and the World Cup race weekend at Levi, which is usually in November — check the date before you plan around it.

December

Christmas in Lapland, and it is a season of its own: Santa Claus Village at Rovaniemi, reindeer, lights and full prices from about the middle of the month. Snow is usually reliable in the north by then, though not guaranteed. The far north is in polar night, with no sunrise at all for weeks and a few hours of blue dusk instead. Independence Day on the sixth, Christmas Eve and Christmas Day close almost everything.

Where to be, season by season

Deep winter, December to March

Be north. Lapland is the only part of the country where winter is an attraction rather than a condition to be endured: snow cover you can count on, the activities built around it, and the darkness the aurora needs. Helsinki in January is a fine city with a hard month on.

Spring, April and May

Be north in April for the last of the snow and the spring skiing, and south in May for the thaw, the light and the terraces. The weeks in between are the awkward ones, and the one time of year when it is genuinely hard to recommend anywhere.

Summer, June to August

Be on water. The Lakeland and the south-west archipelago are what the Finnish summer is for — a cottage, a boat and a lakeside sauna — with Helsinki, Tampere and Turku all at their most likeable. Lapland in summer is for walkers, fishermen and the midnight sun, and it is nearly empty.

Autumn, September and October

Be north in September, for the ruska colour and the first aurora of the season, and in a city in October, when the north is between seasons and much of it closed. September is the most underrated travelling month in Finland.

Public holidays and the things that close

Finland keeps a short list of public holidays, but it keeps them seriously: on the big ones shops, museums and restaurants genuinely close rather than trading reduced hours. The fixed dates are 1 January; 6 January, Epiphany; 1 May, Vappu; 6 December, Independence Day; and 24, 25 and 26 December. Good Friday, Easter Monday, Ascension Day and Whitsun move with Easter. Midsummer Eve and Midsummer Day fall on the Friday and Saturday closest to 24 June, and All Saints’ Day on the Saturday between 31 October and 6 November.

Two of them will change a trip rather than merely interrupt it. Midsummer is the one to plan around: Finns leave for their cottages, cities empty out, a great many businesses shut for the long weekend and public transport runs to a holiday timetable. It is a wonderful thing to be invited to and a bleak weekend to spend in an empty capital by accident. Christmas is the other: 24 December is the real day here, and from lunchtime that day until the twenty-sixth almost nothing is open except in the Lapland Christmas resorts, which run at full tilt and full price.

Two pieces of timing with nothing to do with the calendar. Finnish school holidays in February fill Lapland with families from several countries, so a half-term week there wants booking months ahead. And the glass-roofed cabins and ice-hotel rooms that people come north for are a small, fixed stock sold a long way in advance — if that is the point of your trip, book it before the flights.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Finland has been in the European Union since 1995, in the Schengen area since 2001 and on the euro since 1999, with the notes and coins arriving in 2002. An arrival from another Schengen country has no passport control at all; an arrival from outside it is a single check at Helsinki Airport. The land borders with Sweden and Norway in the north are internal Schengen crossings and often barely marked. The crossings on the Russian border have been closed to travellers since 2023 and there is no passenger train or scheduled bus to Russia, so check with the Finnish Border Guard before planning anything that depends on one.

Timing a Finland trip — questions

What is the single best month to visit Finland?

There is no one answer, and any guide that gives you one is picking a holiday for you. If you want the Finland of snow, huskies and northern lights, March, with February close behind. If you want the Finland of lakes, islands and endless evening light, August, with late June and July alongside it. If you want to spend the least and share it with the fewest people, September. The month to avoid, if this is your first visit, is November.

When can I actually see the northern lights?

The season runs from roughly late August to early April, because what you need is darkness, and in between those dates Lapland does not get properly dark. Within the season the equinox months, September and March, have a reputation for strong activity, and a clear cold night in midwinter is as good a chance as you will get. Three or four nights north of the Arctic Circle, away from town lights, is the minimum worth attempting. Cloud, not the aurora, is what usually spoils it.

Is there really snow at Christmas?

In Lapland, usually, and the Christmas trade is built on it — but usually is not always, and a warm December happens. In Helsinki and the south it is genuinely uncertain: some years are white, many are grey and wet with the sea unfrozen. If snow at Christmas is the point of the trip, go north of Rovaniemi rather than hoping in the south.

How cold does it get, and can I cope with it?

The south sits around freezing through the winter and the north commonly runs fifteen to twenty-five degrees below, with colder snaps. It is a dry cold and far more manageable than the numbers suggest, as long as you dress in layers, cover your head and hands and keep your feet dry. Outfitters lend full oversuits and boots for activity days, hotels and shops are heated hard, and nobody in Finland stays outside for longer than they need to.

What about the mosquitoes everyone warns about?

They are real, they are an inland and northern problem rather than a coastal one, and they are at their worst from roughly late June to early August — which is exactly the Finnish summer. They do not carry disease here, but they will spoil an evening by a lake. Repellent, long sleeves at dusk and a head net if you are walking in Lapland deal with it, and the first frosts of August end the matter.

Does anything in Finland close for the season?

A good deal does, and it is the commonest way to waste a trip here. Lakeland cottages, boat trips, archipelago ferries and summer cafés wind down from September. Lapland’s winter operators stop in April and many do not reopen until the snow returns, which leaves October and parts of May very thin in the north. Cities run all year. Check opening dates rather than assuming, especially outside the two main seasons.
