Puerto Natales & Torres del Paine

Where to Stay in Puerto Natales & Torres del Paine

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Torres del Paine is the reason most travellers come to Chilean Patagonia, and where you sleep decides how much of it you actually see. Puerto Natales, the small fjord town two hours from the park gate, has the restaurants, the outfitters and the affordable rooms. The lodges inside the park cost several times as much and give you back four hours of driving a day and the towers at first light. Below are the places we would actually book in both, each with an honest note and a Book Me link that opens that exact property on Trip.com.

In Puerto Natales

A small, weather-beaten town on a fjord, and the place almost everyone sleeps before and after Torres del Paine. The park gate is about two hours away by road. Staying in town is much cheaper than staying inside the park, puts you beside the outfitters, the gear-hire shops and the bus terminal, and means an early start on park days — which is the trade you are making.

Inside Torres del Paine

Sleeping inside the park costs a great deal more and saves two hours of driving at each end of every day — which, if you have come to walk, is the difference between seeing the towers at sunrise and not. These two are filed by Trip.com under Torres del Paine itself rather than under Puerto Natales. Note that park entry is charged separately and that the refuges and campsites on the trekking circuits are booked through their own operators, not through a hotel.

What to know before you book

The wind is the thing nobody warns you about

Patagonian wind in the summer months is not a breeze; it regularly gusts hard enough to knock a walker off a path and to close the ferry and the high passes. It is strongest from roughly November to February, which is also the best season for everything else. Bring a proper windproof shell, not a showerproof jacket, and expect a day’s plan to be rearranged by weather at least once.

Book far further ahead than feels reasonable

Torres del Paine in January and February is one of the most heavily booked places in South America. Hotels inside the park, and the refuges on the W and the circuit, go months in advance — and the refuges are booked through their own operators, which catches many visitors out. If you are coming in high summer, treat beds as the thing you arrange first and flights second.

Walking here is harder than the photographs suggest

The famous day walk to the base of the towers is roughly eight hours there and back over rough, exposed, steeply rising ground, and the last section is a boulder field. It is well within the reach of a fit walker and well outside the reach of a casual one. There is a great deal to see from the road, the viewpoints and the catamaran for those who would rather not, and no shame in it.

Getting here

Most visitors fly to Punta Arenas, about three hours away by road, and take a bus or a transfer up to Puerto Natales; there is also a small airport at Natales with seasonal flights. Many travellers cross to or from El Calafate in Argentina, which is a half-day on a bus and an international border — carry your passport and expect the agricultural checks on both sides.

Fishing here

Chilean Patagonia is trout country of the kind anglers plan a life around, with the rivers and lakes of the Magallanes region fishing through the southern summer and sea-run brown trout the prize further south still. Our guide is fly fishing in Chile, and the whole shelf is in The Guild Fishing Library.

Don’t see your perfect fit?

Puerto Natales has dozens of small hostels, B&Bs and apartment rentals that fill the gap between a bunk and a lodge, and they come and go with the season. The full list with live prices is one tap away.

Puerto Natales and Torres del Paine — questions

Should I sleep in the park or in Puerto Natales?

In the park if you have come to walk and can afford it: being inside saves about four hours of driving on every park day and puts you there for sunrise, which is when the towers earn their reputation. In Puerto Natales if you are on any sort of budget, if you want restaurants and shops in the evening, or if the park is one part of a longer trip. A common and sensible plan is two nights in town at each end with two or three inside the park in the middle.

How many days does Torres del Paine need?

Three full days is the honest minimum for a visitor who is not trekking: one for the towers viewpoint or a lower alternative, one for the Grey glacier and the lake, one for the Salto Grande, the lagoons and the wildlife on the eastern side. The W trek is four to five days and the full circuit is seven to nine. Anything less than three days and you will spend most of your time in a vehicle.

When is the best time to come?

December to February is the warmest and busiest, with long days and the strongest wind. November and March are the sweet spot for many travellers: fewer people, slightly less wind, and still long enough days to walk. April brings autumn colour and real cold, and much of the infrastructure closes through the southern winter, although winter photography trips do run.

Do I need a guide?

Not for the main day walks, which are well marked and busy in season, though the park requires walkers to register and has rules about which routes may be walked alone and at what time of day. A guide is worth it for the glacier trips, for wildlife, and for anyone nervous about navigating in weather that can change in twenty minutes. Check the park’s own current rules when you book — they are revised from season to season and we will not print a version that may be out of date by the time you read it.
