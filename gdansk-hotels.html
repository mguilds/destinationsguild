Gdansk

Where to Stay in Gdansk

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Gdansk is really three places at once, and that is the thing to grasp before booking. There is the Main Town, a tall gabled merchant quarter rebuilt after the war and as handsome as anything on the Baltic. There is the rest of the city, a working port of half a million people. And there is the Tricity — Gdansk, Sopot and Gdynia strung along the bay and joined by a commuter railway, so that a beach resort is twenty minutes from your hotel door. Which of the three you sleep in changes the holiday a good deal.

Where to stay in Gdansk, area by area

Four choices along the bay, from the gabled centre to the sand, and what each one gives you.

The Guild does not name a hotel until an Explorer has confirmed it on the hotel’s own website, so this guide sets out the areas and sends you to the full, current list for each one rather than printing a shortlist we cannot stand behind yet. Named picks will be added to this page as they are checked.

Główne Miasto — the Main Town and the Long Market

Most centralPrettiestBusy by day

The obvious choice and a very good one. This is the postcard Gdansk: the Long Market running from the Golden Gate to the Green Gate, lined with tall narrow merchant houses with decorated gables; Neptune’s fountain in the middle; the vast brick bulk of St Mary’s church; the amber shops along Mariacka with its stone terraces and gargoyles; and the medieval crane on the waterfront behind. Almost all of it was rebuilt after 1945, and the care taken shows. Staying here puts you in the middle of the restaurants and the river walk, with the Museum of the Second World War ten minutes away over a footbridge. It is busy with day visitors and cruise passengers in summer and quiet by ten at night. Rooms range from small boutique places in rebuilt townhouses to larger modern hotels at the edges.

Stare Miasto — the Old Town, quieter and a little cheaper

QuieterBetter valueTen minutes to the centre

Confusingly, Gdansk’s Old Town lies north of its Main Town and is the less visited of the two. It holds the Great Mill, the old town hall, St Catherine’s and St Bridget’s churches, and — most importantly — it runs up to the shipyard gates and the European Solidarity Centre, the museum of the strikes that began the end of communism in Europe and one of the best museums in the country. Staying here means a flat ten-minute walk to the Long Market, noticeably lower rates, and streets that belong to the people who live in them rather than to visitors. It is less uniformly pretty than the Main Town, with some gaps and some plain post-war blocks among the old, but it is a comfortable and sensible base.

Sopot — the beach resort, twenty minutes up the line

SeasideLively in summerCommuter train

Sopot is a proper nineteenth-century spa resort between Gdansk and Gdynia: a long sand beach, the longest wooden pier on the Baltic, a main street of bars and restaurants running down to it, and some handsome old villas in the pines behind. The commuter train from Gdansk takes about twenty minutes and runs every few minutes, which makes staying here entirely practical even if you came for the history — you simply commute into town. In July and August it is packed, loud at night along Monte Cassino street, and priced accordingly; out of season it is calm, bracing and good value. Choose it if the sea is part of the point, or if you are travelling with children.

Brzeźno, Jelitkowo and the city beaches

Sand and quietFamiliesTram to town

Gdansk has its own beaches north-west of the centre, at Brzeźno and, further along towards Sopot, Jelitkowo: broad stretches of pale sand backed by pine woods and a cycle path that runs the whole way to Sopot. There are a few larger resort hotels and a good many apartments out here, generally cheaper per square metre than the centre and much quieter. The tram into town takes twenty to twenty-five minutes. It suits a longer stay, a family holiday or anyone who would rather wake up near the water than in a city, and it is a poor choice for a two-night visit built around the museums.

Getting around

The thing that makes the Tricity easy is the SKM, the commuter railway that runs the length of the bay from Gdansk through Sopot to Gdynia every few minutes from early morning until late. Journeys are short — twenty minutes Gdansk to Sopot, about thirty-five to Gdynia — and cheap, and it means the three towns function as one place. Buy at the machines on the platform and validate before boarding; the SKM uses its own tickets rather than the city tram ones, which catches people out.

Within Gdansk there are trams and buses on a single time-based ticket, and the centre itself is small, flat and best walked. Lech Walesa airport is about twenty minutes from the centre by bus, train or taxi. Ferries cross to the Hel peninsula in summer, and the water trams along the river and over to Westerplatte are a pleasant way to see the port.

Underfoot, the Main Town is cobbled in places and the stone terraces along Mariacka have steps, but the main streets are flat and easy and the waterfront promenade is level the whole way.

When to come, and what it costs

Gdansk has a shorter season than the inland cities, because a good deal of what people come for is the sea. Late June to early September is the summer proper: the beaches busy, the pier crowded, the water warm enough to swim in by August, and every room in Sopot taken. May, June and September are our pick — long days, the Main Town without the cruise crowds, and rates well below the August peak. Winter is cold, windy and half-shut along the coast, though Gdansk itself keeps going and its Christmas market is one of the better ones.

Prices swing more sharply here than anywhere else in Poland: a Sopot room in August can cost three times what it does in October. If the sea does not matter to you, come in the shoulder months and stay in the Main Town. Poland uses the Polish zloty (PLN, written zł), not the euro. Poland is a member of the European Union but has kept its own currency and has set no date for changing it, so euro notes are not legal tender and the few places that take them give a poor rate. Pay in zloty. Cards and phones are accepted almost everywhere, including on trams, buses and at ticket machines, and you need very little cash — though a small amount is handy for market stalls, church donation boxes and the odd rural bus. Prices in shops include tax. Tipping is modest and not chased: rounding up, or ten per cent for good table service, is plenty. Decline any card machine that offers to charge you in dollars; pay in zloty and let your own bank convert. Beware the standalone currency-exchange booths at the airports, which are much worse than the ones in town or simply drawing cash from a bank machine.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS — the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years — is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today — nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Poland is a straightforward case. It has been in the European Union since 2004 and in the Schengen area since 2007, so everything above applies here in full, and flying in from Berlin, Vienna or Amsterdam involves no passport control at all. What Poland has not joined is the euro, so the currency is the zloty. Note too that Poland’s eastern border — with Belarus and with the Russian enclave at Kaliningrad — is an external Schengen frontier and is not a casual crossing; the crossings into Ukraine are open but busy. None of this affects an ordinary trip to the cities in this guide.

Staying in Gdansk — questions

Should I stay in Gdansk or Sopot?

Gdansk if you have come for the history and the architecture, Sopot if the beach is the point. The honest answer is that it matters less than you would think, because the commuter train between them takes twenty minutes and runs constantly, so either one gives you both. Stay in Gdansk's Main Town for a short visit built around the museums and the Long Market. Stay in Sopot in July and August, with children, or if waking up by the sea is what you want from the trip.

Is the Main Town original or rebuilt?

Rebuilt, and that is worth knowing rather than being disappointed by. Gdansk was about ninety per cent destroyed in 1945, and the Main Town was reconstructed over the following decades from old plans, paintings and photographs, with the facades returned to their seventeenth and eighteenth-century appearance. The brick churches are largely original in their structure. What you are looking at is a careful and deliberate recreation of a Hanseatic merchant city, and read that way it is more remarkable, not less.

How warm is the Baltic for swimming?

Cooler than most visitors expect, and late to warm up. The Baltic here is shallow and only mildly salty, and it takes the whole summer to reach a comfortable temperature, so the best swimming is from late July through to early September rather than in June, when it is still bracing. Even in August it is refreshing rather than warm. The beaches themselves are excellent at any time, broad and sandy with pine woods behind, and the water is clean.

What is the Tricity?

The name for the three towns that run into one another along the bay: Gdansk, the historic city and port; Sopot, the spa and beach resort in the middle; and Gdynia, a modern working port built from nothing in the nineteen-twenties. Together they hold about three quarters of a million people, and the SKM commuter railway links them end to end every few minutes. In practice it means your hotel is twenty minutes from a beach, a museum quarter and a fishing harbour, whichever of the three you sleep in.

Is Gdansk good for a winter visit?

The city is, the coast rather less so. Gdansk's main draws are indoors and open all year, and the Museum of the Second World War and the European Solidarity Centre are better in an empty November than a crowded August. The Christmas market in the Main Town is one of the best in Poland. What closes down is the seaside: Sopot and the beach hotels go quiet, many places shut, and the wind off the Baltic is genuinely cold. For a winter trip, stay in the Main Town.

How do I get to the Museum of the Second World War?

It is a ten-minute walk from the Long Market, north across the Motlawa by the footbridge, and impossible to miss once you are close. Allow three hours at least; it is very large, very detailed and tells the war from the Polish and central European side in a way most visitors have not seen before. Entry is inexpensive and tickets can be bought on arrival outside the busiest summer weeks, though booking ahead online saves a queue.
