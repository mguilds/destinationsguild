Quito — a Colonial City at 2,850 Metres

Where to Stay in Quito — the Best Areas

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Quito is long and narrow, strung for 40 kilometres along a valley at 2,850 metres with volcanoes on both sides. That shape decides everything about where to sleep. The colonial city is at the southern end, the modern city in the middle, the business towers to the north and the airport out in a valley to the east, and the ground between them is never level. Pick the wrong end and you will spend your trip in taxis; pick the wrong hill and you will spend it out of breath. Here is how the city divides.

La Floresta and La Mariscal — the first choice for most visitors

The modern city north of the colonial centre, and where most visitors are happiest. La Floresta is the better half of it: leafy, low-rise, full of small cafes, independent restaurants and a cinema, and quiet at night. La Mariscal, next to it, holds most of the mid-range hotels and the bars — convenient, and louder around the Plaza Foch end than some people want. Both are flat enough to walk, which matters more than it sounds at 2,850 metres, and both are fifteen to twenty-five minutes by taxi from the old town. This is where we would send a first-time visitor.

The Centro Histórico — inside the colonial city

Quito’s old town is the largest and best-preserved in the Americas and it is a World Heritage site — monasteries, gilded churches, a plaza with a presidential palace on it and streets that have not changed shape in four centuries. Sleeping inside it means walking out of your door into that, and a handful of converted colonial houses do it beautifully. The honest trade-offs: the streets are steep and cobbled, the altitude makes every slope count, the neighbourhood empties and goes quiet in the evening, and the Foreign Office names the old town specifically when it warns about mugging and pickpocketing. Wonderful by day, and a taxi after dark.

La Carolina and the north — business Quito

North of La Mariscal the city turns into glass towers, shopping centres and the big international chains around Parque La Carolina. It is safe, orderly, well served by taxis and completely without atmosphere, which is exactly right if you are here for work or want a known-quantity hotel with a gym and a reliable breakfast. It is also the closest of the city districts to the airport road.

Cumbayá and the Tumbaco valley — lower, warmer, greener

East of the city the land drops into a valley that sits several hundred metres lower than Quito, which means warmer days, softer nights and a noticeably easier time of it if the altitude is bothering you. Cumbayá has become a comfortable suburb of restaurants and boutique hotels, and it is on the airport side of town, so it suits an arrival or a departure. The catch is simple: you are out of the city, and everything — the old town, the museums, dinner anywhere else — is a drive.

Out at the airport — for one reason only

Mariscal Sucre is around 40 kilometres east of the city at Tababela, and the transfer is commonly 45 minutes to an hour or more. A hotel out there earns its place for exactly two situations: a flight that lands late at night, or one that leaves before dawn — and that second one includes the morning Galápagos flights. For any other night, stay in the city.

What to know before you book

Heating is not standard, and Quito nights are cold. The city runs mild days and nights close to single figures Celsius all year, and plenty of perfectly good hotels have no heating at all because the daytime climate never demands it. If you feel the cold, ask before you book whether the room has a heater or, in the colonial conversions, whether the room gets any sun.

Ask where the hotel actually is on the hill. “Five minutes from the Plaza Grande” can mean five minutes downhill and twenty-five back up. In the old town especially, ask for the street and look at it rather than trusting the distance.

The altitude is the one thing worth planning around. Arrive, have a quiet afternoon, and put the cable car, Cotopaxi and the Quilotoa crater on day three rather than day one. Our Ecuador guide has a section on travelling in your later years that goes into this properly.

The money is the US dollar and Ecuador mints its own coins alongside American ones. Carry small notes; fifties and hundreds are frequently refused, and taxis almost never have change for them.

Sunday morning is the best time in the old town. Streets close to traffic, families come out, and the city belongs to people on foot for a few hours.

Quito — questions

Which part of Quito should I stay in?

For a first visit, La Floresta or the quieter streets around La Mariscal. You are twenty minutes by taxi from the old town, you can walk to dinner, and the altitude feels easier on a flat street than on a cobbled hill. Stay in the Centro Historico itself if the colonial city is the reason you came and you do not mind steep cobbles and an early-quiet neighbourhood. Stay in the Cumbaya valley if you want warmth, air and a lower altitude, and accept that you will drive everywhere.

How far is the airport from Quito?

Further than almost anyone expects. Mariscal Sucre airport sits at Tababela, out in a valley roughly 40 kilometres east of the city, and the drive into the main hotel districts is typically 45 minutes to over an hour depending on traffic and which side of town you are heading for. Allow real time in both directions, pre-book your transfer through your hotel, and if you land late or leave before dawn, an airport hotel for that one night is money well spent rather than an indulgence.

Will the altitude affect me in Quito?

Quito sits at about 2,850 metres, which is high enough to notice. The usual first-day experience is breathlessness on stairs, a mild headache and poor sleep, and it passes. Take the first afternoon slowly, drink more water than you want, skip the alcohol on the first evening, and leave the TelefériQo cable car and any volcano until day three - the UK Foreign Office warns specifically about altitude sickness at Pichincha, which is where that cable car goes, and records deaths from hypothermia on the route to Rucu Pichincha. If you have a heart or lung condition, ask your own doctor before you book.

Is Quito's old town safe to walk?

By day it is the heart of the city and full of people, and that is how to see it. The UK Foreign Office says plainly that mugging and pickpocketing are common and to take care in Quito's old town and at transport hubs, so the sensible rules are the ordinary ones: nothing valuable on show, a phone that stays in a pocket, no walking there after dark, and a taxi booked through your hotel or a known app rather than hailed in the street. The Foreign Office also warns that using unregistered taxis significantly increases your risk. Quito is not in the area it advises against travelling to.

Can I walk between the old town and the modern city?

Not comfortably. They are around five kilometres apart along a valley, and the ground between them rises and falls the whole way. Taxis are inexpensive and are what residents use; the trolleybus lines run the same route and are heavily used, with the usual pickpocket warning that goes with any crowded transport in the city. Plan the old town as a day out from wherever you sleep rather than as a stroll.
