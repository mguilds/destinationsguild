Where to Stay in Funchal

Where to Stay in Funchal — Best Hotels & Neighbourhoods

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated August 2026

Funchal is built in a natural amphitheatre, and its streets climb from the harbour with real conviction. A map that says “ten minutes to town” is honest about the distance and silent about the gradient — it is the walk back that tells. That single fact should decide where you sleep. There are three genuinely level stretches in the city: the Lido seafront promenade in the São Martinho hotel zone, which runs flat and palm-lined for well over a mile; the Avenida Arriaga and the waterfront in the city centre; and the lanes of the Old Town down by the cable car. Everything else slopes. The compensation is that Madeira’s hotels are unusually well supplied with lifts — several run one straight down the cliff to the sea. The mountainside problem, and where the level walking is

Funchal rises steeply from the harbour, and the return journey is always the hard one. There are three flat places and it is worth knowing them by name. The Lido promenade, the Passeio Marítimo, runs level and palm-lined from Ponta Gorda and Gorgulho westward, well benched and lined with cafés and the seawater bathing complexes. The Avenida Arriaga and Avenida do Mar in the centre are flat. So are the Old Town waterfront lanes. Everything else, including most side streets in the hotel zone, slopes. Plan taxis home rather than out.

Getting in from the airport

Cristiano Ronaldo International sits on its famous stilted runway at Santa Cruz, some eighteen to twenty kilometres east of the city. Taxis wait outside arrivals and reach central Funchal in twenty to thirty minutes on the fast motorway; a pre-booked private transfer costs little more and spares you any negotiation after a long flight. The Aerobus, route 500, runs to Avenida do Mar in about twenty-four minutes and on to the Lido in about thirty-eight, for around seven euros paid on board. It has luggage space but a single step up.

There is no wrong month here

Madeira is subtropical and famously equable — it is sold as the island of eternal spring, and the claim largely holds. Funchal sits in a narrow band from the high sixties to around eighty all year and never has a genuinely cold month, which makes it the strongest destination in Portugal for a November-to-March trip. April to June and September to October give the most comfortable walking weather. Late spring brings the Flower Festival; New Year’s Eve brings one of the world’s great fireworks displays over the bay, and rooms for that week go many months ahead.

Cable car, levadas and what suits limited mobility

The cable car climbs five hundred and sixty metres from Avenida do Mar to Monte in about fifteen minutes, running daily from nine in the morning. The cabins are small and boarding is at a slow walking pace; if accessibility matters, telephone ahead, because it is not well documented. At the top, the Monte Palace garden is beautiful and steeply terraced. As for levada walks, the paths along the irrigation channels are often level — but the access tracks, tunnels and unguarded drops make most of them unsuitable for anyone unsteady. Ask operators specifically for the short, wide, fenced routes.

Luxury

Two very different grand hotels — one Victorian and famous, one modern and full of lifts.

Reid’s Palace, A Belmond Hotel

The grande dame of the Atlantic, opened in 1891 and still the island’s most storied address. Ten acres of subtropical garden tumble down a promontory to sea-bathing platforms, reached by lift rather than by steps — which here is the detail that matters. Afternoon tea on the terrace is an institution and the service is old-school without being starchy. It suits travellers who want ceremony and calm together. The caveat: the gardens are steeply terraced and the walk up from town is a genuine climb. Take a taxi.

An honest note. Madeira is volcanic and has almost no natural sandy beach. Funchal’s so-called beaches are pebble or imported sand, and what a hotel here calls “beachfront” almost always means a concrete or basalt bathing platform with ladders and handrails into the Atlantic, alongside saltwater pools. That is not a criticism — the engineered sea bathing on this island is superb, and often easier than a sandy beach. But judge these hotels on their platforms, pools and lifts, not on sand. If sand is essential to your holiday, the golden beach at Calheta, an hour west, uses imported sand.

Funchal Hotels — common questions

Does Madeira actually have beaches?

Not in the way Americans usually mean, and it is better to know that before you fly. Madeira is volcanic, and its coast is cliff and boulder rather than sand. Funchal’s Praia Formosa is pebble and Gorgulho is a small stony cove. What the island does superbly instead is engineered sea bathing: concrete and basalt platforms with ladders and handrails into clear Atlantic water, alongside large saltwater pools at the Lido and Ponta Gorda complexes and at hotels such as VidaMar and The Cliff Bay. If sand is essential, the golden beach at Calheta, about an hour west, uses imported sand.

Which area of Funchal should I choose?

It depends almost entirely on your legs. The Lido and São Martinho hotel zone gives you the flat seafront promenade, the sea pools, and hotels with lifts down to the water, at the cost of a ten-minute taxi to the Old Town. The city centre puts you among the market, the cathedral and the Avenida, all reasonably level, though the surrounding streets climb. The Old Town is best for restaurants and the cable car. Caniço and Garajau are quieter and cheaper but need transport.

Do I need to hire a car?

For Funchal itself, no — and many older visitors are relieved to hear it. The city is compact, taxis are plentiful and inexpensive, and hotels arrange excursions. Madeira’s roads are steep and narrow in the villages, full of tunnels and hairpins that reward a confident driver and punish a nervous one, and parking in Funchal is difficult. The sensible compromise is to base yourself in the city, take organised half-day coach tours to Porto Moniz, Câmara de Lobos and Pico do Arieiro, and hire a car only if you plan several days in the west.

How hilly is it really, and are lifts common?

Very hilly — and yes, unusually common, because the hotels are built into cliffs and have no choice. Several properties, including The Cliff Bay, Reid’s Palace and Sentido Galomar, run lifts right down to sea level. Within hotels, however, terraced grounds often mean short flights of steps or ramps between pool decks and gardens. When booking, ask two specific questions: is there a lift to the pool level, and how many steps lie between reception and my room? Both are fair questions and a good hotel answers them precisely.

When is the best time to visit for an older traveller?

Late April through June, and September into early November, give the kindest combination: temperatures pleasant for walking, sea warm enough for swimming, and beautiful light. High summer is agreeable rather than oppressive but busier. Winter is mild and green, which makes Madeira the one part of Portugal genuinely worth a November-to-March trip. December is festive and very heavily booked around New Year. The Flower Festival in late spring is a delight, though the parade involves long stretches of standing — reserved seating is sold and is well worth the outlay.

Is English widely spoken, and how do I manage practically?

Yes, and easily. English is spoken confidently in hotels, restaurants, taxis, pharmacies and tour offices across Funchal, and menus are routinely printed in English. Portugal uses the euro and cards work almost everywhere, though it is worth carrying coins for the Aerobus and small cafés. Tipping is modest — rounding up, or five to ten percent for good restaurant service. Pharmacies are excellent for minor complaints and Funchal has a well-regarded hospital. Bring any prescriptions in their original packaging with the generic names noted.
