Rotorua

Where to Stay in Rotorua

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Rotorua is a small town sitting on top of an active geothermal field, which gives it three things nowhere else in the country has: steam rising out of ordinary gardens, hot mineral water on tap in a good many hotels, and the strongest living Maori cultural presence of any town a visitor is likely to stay in. It also gives it the smell, which is hydrogen sulphide, which is entirely harmless and which you will stop noticing on the second day. The town is small enough to cross on foot; the choice is really about what you want outside your door.

Where to stay in Rotorua, area by area

Four parts of a small geothermal town, and who each one suits.

The lakefront and Government Gardens

ViewsHot poolsWalk to the centre

The northern edge of town where it meets Lake Rotorua, taking in the Government Gardens, the extraordinary Tudor-style Bath House that now holds the museum, and the lakefront promenade and jetties. This is the handsomest part of Rotorua and where a good many of the better hotels and lodges are, several with their own geothermal pools. It is a flat five to ten minute walk into the central grid, the lakefront walk and the Sulphur Point boardwalks start here, and the sunsets over the lake are the best thing in town. Rooms with a lake view carry a premium that is, for once, worth paying.

The central grid - Fenton Street and Tutanekai Street

Most convenientMotels and valueRestaurants

The town centre proper, and the motel capital of New Zealand: Fenton Street runs south from the lake for a couple of kilometres lined with motels, a great many of them with private thermal plunge pools, at prices well below what a hotel room costs in the cities. Tutanekai Street and the Eat Streat covered strip hold most of the restaurants. This is the practical base for a family or a self-drive trip - easy parking, kitchens, space, and everything within a flat walk or a two-minute drive. Fenton Street itself is a busy road rather than a pretty one, so ask for a room at the back.

Ohinemutu and the lakeside marae

Cultural heartVery few bedsWalk from town

The original lakeside Maori village on the western shore, fifteen minutes' walk from the centre, where steam comes up between the houses and the carved St Faith's church stands beside the marae. It is a living village rather than an attraction, and visitors are asked to walk quietly and respect that; there is almost nowhere to stay in it. We include it because it is the part of Rotorua most worth an hour of your time and the part most visitors miss, and because the lakefront hotels a few minutes away are the practical way to be close to it.

The Whakarewarewa side and the Redwoods

GeysersForest and bikingCar useful

South of the centre along Fenton and Tarawera Roads, where the geothermal valleys and the forest are: Te Puia and the Whakarewarewa living village with the Pohutu geyser, the Blue and Green Lakes, the Redwoods forest with its walking trails and mountain-bike network and its treetop walkway, and the Polynesian Spa and Wai-O-Tapu further out. Accommodation here is motels, holiday parks and a few lodges, and it suits anyone here for the biking or the geothermal parks. You will want a car or a bike, as it is spread out and the walk in from town is long.

Getting around

The town is compact and flat and the lakefront, the gardens, the museum and the restaurant streets are all within a twenty-minute walk of each other. A local bus network runs out to the suburbs and the main geothermal parks, and there are shuttles to the big attractions, but most visitors drive: the sights are spread along a few roads south and east of town and parking at them is free and easy. Rotorua's own airport is a short drive north-east with flights to the main cities, and the drive from Auckland is about three hours.

One genuinely local piece of advice: the geothermal attractions are not interchangeable and doing three in a day is a mistake. Te Puia and Whakarewarewa are about geysers and Maori culture together; Wai-O-Tapu, half an hour south, is about colour and scale; Hell's Gate, east towards the lakes, is about mud. Pick two over two days and give each of them a morning.

When to come, and what it costs

Rotorua works all year and is one of the few New Zealand towns that is arguably better in cool weather, because sitting in a hot mineral pool in the steam is more of a pleasure in July than in January. December to February is the busiest time, warm and humid, with the lakes in use and the school holidays filling the motels. March to May is our pick: warm days, fewer people, and the forest at its best. Winter is mild, often wet, and the cheapest time to come - and the steam is at its most photogenic on a cold morning.

On price, Rotorua is one of the better value places to stay in the country because of the sheer number of motels competing along Fenton Street, with the exception of the Christmas and January school holidays and any weekend with a large event. Getting in: what you need, and what you pay

Most visitors from visa-waiver countries need a New Zealand Electronic Travel Authority, the NZeTA, requested before you fly, and most also pay the International Visitor Conservation and Tourism Levy, which is normally collected at the same time. A separate New Zealand Traveller Declaration is completed shortly before you arrive and has replaced the old paper arrival card.

Both the NZeTA fee and the levy have changed more than once in recent years, so we do not print the figures here — read them off the official page at immigration.govt.nz when you book, and request the NZeTA through Immigration New Zealand’s own app or website rather than any agent offering to do it for you. Australian citizens need neither; Australian permanent residents who are not citizens do need the NZeTA. The airline checks it before you board, so do it when you book rather than at the airport.

Take the biosecurity questions on arrival seriously: declare all food, and clean the soil off boots, tents and fishing tackle before you pack them. Declaring something costs nothing; not declaring it can cost you an instant fine. The full picture is in our New Zealand travel guide.

Staying in Rotorua — questions

Does Rotorua really smell of sulphur?

Yes, and it is worth knowing before you arrive rather than after. The smell is hydrogen sulphide from the geothermal field beneath the town, it is strongest near the lakefront, Sulphur Point and the geothermal valleys, and it is more noticeable on still mornings. It is harmless at the levels found around the town, and almost everybody stops registering it within a day or two. If you are unusually sensitive to smells, a room on the southern side of town is further from the worst of it.

How many days do you need in Rotorua?

Two nights is the sensible minimum and three is comfortable. That gives you one day for a geothermal park and a Maori cultural evening, and a second for the Redwoods, the lakes and a long soak in a thermal pool, with time left for Hobbiton or Waitomo as a half-day drive. One night is enough to see one thing well and feel you rushed the rest, which is what most coach itineraries do.

Which hot pools should I choose?

It depends what you want from them. The Polynesian Spa on the lakefront is the established choice, with a range of pools of different temperatures and mineral content and a view over the lake; the Secret Spot and several newer places offer smaller private tubs; and a surprising number of the Fenton Street motels have their own private thermal plunge pool included with the room, which is the cheapest way of all to have one. Wherever you soak, take the signs about not putting your head under seriously.

Can I still visit White Island?

No. Whakaari, or White Island, the active volcano off the Bay of Plenty coast, has been closed to visitors since the eruption of December 2019, in which twenty-two people died. Tours do not land there and should not be expected to resume. Boat and flight trips that view the island from a distance operate from Whakatane, and if you want to walk in a crater instead, the Tongariro Alpine Crossing, Wai-O-Tapu and Hell's Gate are the alternatives inland.
