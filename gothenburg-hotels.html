Gothenburg

Where to Stay in Gothenburg

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Gothenburg is Sweden’s second city, its biggest port and, by a wide margin, its friendliest — a working harbour town laid out by Dutch engineers around a ring of canals, with the bare granite islands of the Bohuslän coast starting a tram ride from the centre. It is small enough that the choice of neighbourhood matters less here than in Stockholm, and the tram network makes most of the city fifteen minutes from most of the rest. The short version: Inom Vallgraven for convenience, the Avenue for the museums and the evening, Haga and Linné for the pleasure of the place, and Majorna for the harbour.

Inom Vallgraven — inside the moat

Most convenientFlatBest for a short stay

The original walled town between the canal and the river, and the obvious base: the central station and the long-distance coaches are on its northern edge, every tram line crosses it, and the harbour, the opera, the shopping streets and the canal boats are all within a flat ten minutes. The large hotels are here and they are priced for business travellers, which means weekends and July are often the best value in the city. It is level throughout, which is worth knowing if the steep streets elsewhere are a consideration. The honest drawback is that it empties in the evening: the restaurants and bars that stay busy after nine are mostly a few hundred metres south and west of it.

Avenyn and Vasastan — the boulevard and the museums

MuseumsEveningGrand streets

Kungsportsavenyn, universally just the Avenue, runs south-east from the canal to Götaplatsen and the art museum, and it is the city’s formal spine: pavement tables the length of it in summer, the concert hall and the theatre at the top, and the handsome nineteenth-century streets of Vasastan either side. It is a natural base if you want to walk to dinner and to the main museums, and the amusement park at Liseberg — which is a genuine institution rather than a tourist trap, and spectacular at Christmas — is a few minutes further on. Rooms here are generally larger and quieter than in the centre proper. It is a gentle uphill from the water, nothing more.

Haga and Linnéstaden — the enjoyable part

West of the Avenue, Haga is a few streets of restored wooden and brick houses that survived the city’s rebuilding, now given over to coffee houses, antique shops and the best fika in Sweden — the cinnamon buns here are the size of a dinner plate and are not a joke. Linnégatan, just beyond, is the city’s best run of restaurants, and the hill of Skansen Kronan above gives you the whole town from a bench. There are fewer hotels here and more apartments and small guesthouses, which is worth knowing if you want a kitchen. Haga Nygata itself is cobbled and the surrounding streets slope.

Majorna, Klippan and the harbour side

HarbourGood valueTram ride to centre

West again, following the river towards the sea, this is the old shipyard and dockworkers’ Gothenburg: low wooden terraces, flea markets, unpretentious restaurants and the best prices in the city for space. It is also the way out to the coast — the tram runs to Saltholmen, where the boats leave for the car-free southern archipelago, which is the single best half-day Gothenburg offers. You will be ten to fifteen minutes from the centre by tram rather than walking into it, and there is less to look at on the doorstep. For a stay of several days, with a tram pass, it is the value choice.

Getting around

The tram is the city, and it is one of the largest networks in northern Europe. Twelve lines cross at Brunnsparken beside the canal, they run from early until after midnight, and one ticket covers the trams, the buses and the harbour and archipelago ferries for ninety minutes with as many changes as you like. Buy it in the regional transport app or tap a contactless card on board; there is no cash anywhere in the system. For a stay of more than a day or two a multi-day pass pays for itself quickly, and it includes the boats.

The centre is flat and compact enough to walk end to end in twenty-five minutes, and the canal and riverside paths are level the whole way. South and west of the Avenue the ground starts to rise, and Haga’s main street is cobbled. Bicycles are everywhere and the city hire scheme is cheap, though the tram rails demand some respect on a bike.

Two journeys worth planning for. The airport coach from Landvetter takes about half an hour to the central station and runs frequently. And the tram to Saltholmen, at the end of the line, connects with the scheduled ferries to Styrsö, Brännö and the other southern islands, where there are no cars at all — an ordinary transport ticket covers it, and it is the thing to do with a spare afternoon.

When to come, and what it costs

Gothenburg’s season runs from May to September, and August is its best month: the sea is at its warmest, the archipelago boats are busy, the pavement tables on the Avenue are full and the shellfish is at its best. July is the Swedish holiday month, so the city is quieter than the calendar suggests and the business hotels in the centre drop their prices. The west coast is wetter and windier than Stockholm in every season — this is the Atlantic-facing side of the country — so bring a coat whatever the month, and do not plan a trip here around guaranteed sunshine.

Autumn is short and blustery, and from the end of October the afternoons finish by five. Winter in Gothenburg is mild by Swedish standards, grey more often than white, and the one time the city reliably sparkles is late November to Christmas, when Liseberg turns itself into a very large and very good Christmas market. Prices are lowest from January to March.

On cost, Gothenburg is noticeably cheaper than Stockholm for a room of the same standard, and its restaurants are better value for the quality — particularly the seafood, which is the thing to eat here. The weekday fixed-price lunch is the biggest single saving available: most city restaurants serve one, and it is a fraction of the same kitchen’s evening prices.

Sweden uses the Swedish krona (SEK, written kr), not the euro, and is one of the most nearly cashless countries in the world — a great many cafés, buses and museums take cards only and will politely refuse notes, so bring a card you can tap and do not bother changing money. Wine, spirits and stronger beer are sold only through the state shops, Systembolaget, which keep short hours and are shut on Sundays; weaker beer is in the supermarkets. Tipping is not expected and never chased — rounding up a bill is plenty. Decline any card machine that offers to charge you in dollars; pay in kronor and let your own bank convert.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS — the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years — is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today — nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave. Sweden is in both the European Union and the Schengen area, so arriving from another Schengen country there is no passport control at all.

Staying in Gothenburg — questions

Which part of Gothenburg should I stay in?

For one or two nights, Inom Vallgraven inside the old moat: the station is on its edge, every tram crosses it and it is flat throughout. For museums, the concert hall and dinner within walking distance, the Avenue and Vasastan. For the most enjoyable streets in the city, Haga and Linne, accepting cobbles and fewer hotels. For space, value and the boats out to the islands, Majorna and the harbour side. Gothenburg is small and the tram network is good, so none of these choices will strand you.

How do I get from Landvetter airport into Gothenburg?

The airport coach is the straightforward answer: it runs frequently, takes about half an hour to the central station and costs a fraction of a taxi. There is no train to Landvetter. A taxi takes a similar time and costs several times as much, and firms here set their own fares, so agree the price or use an app rather than hailing one. Some budget airlines have historically used other airfields well outside the city, so check which airport your ticket actually names before you plan the transfer.

Is Gothenburg worth visiting as well as Stockholm?

Yes, and the two are genuinely different rather than variations on a theme. Stockholm is the grander and more beautiful city; Gothenburg is the warmer and easier one, with better seafood, a more relaxed manner and the bare granite islands of the west coast on its doorstep. The fast train between them takes about three hours, which makes a trip taking in both entirely practical in a week. If you have to choose for a short visit, Stockholm; if you are coming back, Gothenburg is where the Swedes themselves go to enjoy themselves.

What is the best day trip from Gothenburg?

The southern archipelago. Take the tram to Saltholmen at the end of the line and the scheduled ferry to Styrso, Branno or Donso - islands with no cars at all, where the lanes are walked or cycled and the granite slopes into the sea. An ordinary ninety-minute transport ticket covers the whole journey, which makes it one of the cheapest good days out in Sweden. Further north, the fishing villages of Bohuslan - Marstrand, Smogen, Fjallbacka - are an hour or two away by bus or car and are the classic Swedish summer coast.

When is the best time to visit Gothenburg?

August, for the warmest sea, the busiest archipelago boats and the shellfish at its best. May, June and September are quieter and cheaper and still pleasant, with the caveat that Midsummer in late June closes a great deal for several days. This is the Atlantic-facing side of Sweden and it is wetter and windier than Stockholm year round, so a waterproof is not optional in any season. Late November to Christmas is the other good window, when Liseberg runs one of the better Christmas markets in northern Europe.
