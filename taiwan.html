Taiwan

Taiwan Travel Guide

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Taiwan is a single mountainous island about three hundred and ninety kilometres long, holding some twenty-three and a half million people, nearly all of them on the flat western third. The rest is mountain — two hundred and sixty peaks above three thousand metres, rainforest at the bottom and cloud forest at the top — and that range down the middle is the single most useful thing to understand before you book. It divides the island into a populous, fast, easy west coast served by a high speed railway, and a thin, slow, spectacular east coast served by one line and a coast road. It also divides the weather, which is why Taipei can be grey and drizzling in January while Kaohsiung, two hours away, is sunny and twenty-six degrees.

Why visit Taiwan

A small, safe, startlingly good-value island that most people fly over on the way to somewhere more famous.

The food is the reason people come back

Taiwan eats better, for less money, than almost anywhere on earth. Four hundred years of Hokkien, Hakka, indigenous, Japanese and mainland Chinese cooking landed on one small island and settled into a street-food culture that is not a tourist attraction but simply how people have dinner.

It is small, and the trains are fast

Three hundred and ninety kilometres end to end, with a high speed line down the populated side that does Taipei to Kaohsiung in well under two hours. You can have breakfast in a northern city and a swim in the tropics the same afternoon, which is not true of many countries.

Mountains, immediately

Two thirds of the island is mountain, and there are two hundred and sixty-odd peaks above three thousand metres. Yushan, at 3,952 metres, is the highest in Northeast Asia. From any west coast city the hills start within an hour of the station.

Easy, safe and genuinely welcoming

Low crime, excellent public transport, English on the signs in the cities, a convenience store on every corner that will let you pay a bill or forward a suitcase, and a reputation for kindness to lost strangers that is deserved. It is a gentle country to travel in alone or late in life.

Taiwan at a glance

Capital

Taipei, in a basin at the northern end of the island. Taoyuan International Airport, the main gateway, is forty minutes west of it by its own metro line.

Money

The New Taiwan dollar (NT$, TWD). Cards and phones work in cities and chains; carry cash for night markets, temples, small shops and buses outside the big cities.

Language

Mandarin Chinese is official, Taiwanese Hokkien and Hakka are widely spoken, and several indigenous languages are recognised. English appears on signs, stations and menus in the cities and thins out in the countryside.

The two coasts

West: flat, populous, the high speed rail, the cities. East: mountains straight into the Pacific, one slow railway, far fewer people. The range between them is the dividing line for weather as well as traffic.

Getting about

High speed rail down the west, conventional rail round the whole island, metro in Taipei, Taichung and Kaohsiung. One EasyCard or iPASS pays for nearly all of it.

Seasons

Hot and humid May to September, with typhoons July to September. Pleasant October to April in the south; damp and grey December to February in the north.

The regions

Five parts of the island. A first trip takes the west coast, which the fast train makes easy, and a second one takes the east.

Taipei & the north

The capital sits in a basin at the top of the island, ringed by green hills and old volcanoes, with hot springs in its northern suburbs and the Pacific an hour away in two directions. This is where almost every visitor lands and where the museums, the night markets and the metro are. It is also the wettest and greyest corner of Taiwan in winter.

Taichung & the centre

The middle of the west coast, sheltered from both monsoons and with the kindest weather on the island: warm, dry and bright when Taipei is under cloud. It is the gateway to the central mountains — Sun Moon Lake, Alishan and the high road over the ranges all start from here.

Tainan & the south-west

The oldest city in Taiwan, founded by the Dutch in 1624 and the island’s capital for two centuries, now a low-rise, temple-dense, slow-moving place that Taiwanese people travel to for the food. The Tropic of Cancer crosses the coast just north of here, and from this point south the climate is properly tropical.

Kaohsiung & the south

The big southern port, remade over twenty years from an industrial harbour into the most relaxed city in Taiwan: a waterfront arts quarter in the old warehouses, a light rail loop, palm trees and dependable winter sunshine. Kenting, the beach end of the island, is two hours further on.

The east coast

Over the Central Mountain Range and a different country: a thin strip of rice and sugar between high peaks and the Pacific, reached by a slow and beautiful railway rather than the high speed line. Hualien and Taitung are the two towns. The April 2024 earthquake hit this coast hard and Taroko Gorge is still only partly open — read the park’s own notices before you plan around it.

Things to do in Taiwan

Taipei 101 and the hill behind it

The tower is worth the lift for the view and the giant tuned mass damper that keeps it still in typhoons. The better photograph of it is from the Elephant Mountain steps opposite, twenty minutes up from the metro and free.

The National Palace Museum

The imperial collection carried out of Beijing in the 1930s and 40s: bronzes, jades, calligraphy and Song ceramics, one of the great museums of Chinese art anywhere. Only a fraction is shown at once, and it rotates.

A night market, properly

Shilin and Raohe in Taipei, Fengjia in Taichung, Liuhe in Kaohsiung, the Garden market in Tainan. Go hungry, go about seven, buy one small thing at a time and keep walking.

The high speed rail, and then a slow train

The fast line is a pleasure in its own right. The other half of the treat is the old railway down the east coast from Taipei through Hualien to Taitung, which runs between the mountains and the sea for hours.

Sun Moon Lake and Alishan

The two classic mountain trips, both out of Taichung or Chiayi. Sun Moon Lake is a cycling and boat day; Alishan is a narrow-gauge forest railway, high cedar woods and a famous sea of cloud at dawn.

Hot springs, north and east

Beitou, at the end of a Taipei metro branch, is a whole hot-spring suburb left over from the Japanese period, and the easiest soak in the country. Jiaoxi, an hour away in Yilan, is the other well-known one.

Best time to visit & getting there

Autumn is the answer if you only get one. October and November give warm, mostly dry, clear days the length of the island, the typhoon season on its way out and the summer humidity finally gone. Spring, late March into early May, is nearly as good and brings the tea hills and the mountain cherry into flower, but it ends abruptly in the middle of May when the meiyu or plum rains settle over the north for a month of grey and wet.

Summer, June to September, is hot, hard-breathing humid and the typhoon window, with the peak risk from July to September. It is also when rooms and flights are cheapest, when the high mountains are at their best and when the island is at its liveliest, so it is a real option rather than a season to avoid — just build in a spare day, keep domestic flights and ferries flexible, and expect at least one afternoon of spectacular rain.

Winter is the one visitors get wrong. From December to February the northeast monsoon parks itself over the top of the island, and Taipei, Keelung and the whole north coast go damp, overcast and twelve to eighteen degrees for weeks at a time. The mountains block it: Kaohsiung, Tainan and Kenting stay dry and sunny at twenty to twenty-eight. A winter trip planned from the south upwards is a very good holiday; the same trip planned from Taipei down is a disappointment. Our month-by-month guide to the best time to visit Taiwan sets it all out, including Lunar New Year, which is the one week to book around.

Getting there is straightforward. Taoyuan International Airport (TPE) takes nearly all long-haul traffic and is linked to Taipei Main Station by its own metro line in about forty minutes. Songshan (TSA), inside Taipei itself, handles regional flights, and Kaohsiung (KHH) is a useful second international door if you are starting in the south. From North America there are direct flights from the west coast and several eastern hubs; from Europe there are a few direct services and a very wide choice of one-stop routings through the Gulf or Asia. If you are travelling the length of the island, flying into Taipei and out of Kaohsiung saves you a day and is usually priced as an open-jaw without penalty.

Fishing here

The Guild has no Taiwan fishing book yet, and we would rather say so than pad this page. The honest picture: Taiwan’s rivers are short, steep and prone to flooding, which makes them hard water rather than famous water, and the island’s one celebrated native trout, the Formosan landlocked salmon of the Qijiawan river, is a protected endangered species that nobody fishes. What visitors do fish here is salt water — harbour and jetty fishing all round the coast, and charter boats out of the eastern ports for the big pelagic fish that follow the Kuroshio current past Hualien and Taitung. Rules differ by county, by river and by harbour, and much of the coastline and the mountain catchments are inside protected areas, so ask locally before you cast. The whole of our fishing shelf is in The Guild Fishing Library.

Travelling in your later years

One of the easiest countries in Asia to travel slowly and comfortably in, with heat rather than terrain as the thing to plan around.

A kind country for slow travel

Flat cities, short distances, excellent trains, air conditioning everywhere and people who will walk you to where you are going rather than point. There is very little rough ground between you and what you came to see.

The metro and the trains are step-free

Taipei, Taichung and Kaohsiung metros have lifts at every station, level boarding and clear English signs, and the high speed rail has wheelchair spaces and staff help booked in advance. Priority seating is taken seriously here and will be offered to you.

Heat and humidity are the real strain

From May to September the combination of heat and damp is harder work than the temperature suggests. Do the outdoor part of the day before ten in the morning, retreat to a museum, a tea house or a shopping arcade in the afternoon, and drink more water than you want.

Pavements, arcades and scooters

The covered arcades along older city streets are shaded and welcome, but they are also uneven, stepped and often parked full of scooters. Look both ways at every driveway, and do not assume a green man means the scooters have stopped.

Health care is good and cheap

Standards are high, hospitals in the cities are excellent and many doctors speak English. Visitors pay, but the sums are modest by American or European standards. Bring prescriptions in their original packaging with a copy of the prescription, and take travel insurance anyway.

Earthquakes, calmly

The buildings are engineered for them and the warnings arrive on your phone before the shaking does. Pick a hotel that is not the oldest building on the street, note where the stairs are, and then forget about it.

Getting in: what you need, and what you pay

For most of our readers, entry to Taiwan is as simple as it gets. Citizens of the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, the European Union and Schengen countries, Australia, New Zealand, Japan and South Korea, among a long list of others, are admitted visa-free for ninety days. There is no fee and no application. You need a passport valid for the whole of your stay, and the officer may ask to see an onward or return ticket and an address in Taiwan.

Other nationalities are admitted visa-free for thirty or fourteen days, and some need a visa obtained before travel. The list changes, and some of the shorter allowances are granted as temporary trial schemes that get extended a year at a time, so look your own passport up on the Ministry of Foreign Affairs’ Bureau of Consular Affairs site at boca.gov.tw rather than relying on anything written down, here or elsewhere, more than a few months ago.

Separately from the visa question, there is a free Taiwan Online Arrival Card to complete shortly before you land, which has replaced the paper card that used to be handed out on the plane. It takes two minutes on the official government website. As with every travel authorisation of this kind, use the official site: there are agents who will fill in the same free form for you and charge for it.

Two practical notes once you are through. Buy a stored-value card at the airport — an EasyCard or an iPASS — because it pays for the metros, the city buses, the local trains, the ferries and your coffee, and saves you queueing for tickets all trip. And if you want a local SIM or eSIM, the arrivals hall is the cheapest and easiest place to get one; coverage is excellent everywhere except deep in the mountains.

Taiwan travel FAQ

When is the best time to visit Taiwan?

October and November are the Guild's pick, with late March and April close behind. Autumn brings warm, mostly dry days, the typhoon season winding down and the humidity finally off the cities. Spring is pleasant too, though the plum rains arrive from about the middle of May and sit over the north for a month. Summer, June to September, is hot, very humid and the typhoon window; it is also the cheapest time and the best time for the high mountains. Winter is the one most visitors get wrong: from December to February Taipei and the whole north are grey, damp and about 12 to 18 degrees, while Kaohsiung, Tainan and the far south stay dry and sunny at 20 to 28. If you are coming in winter, plan the trip from the south upwards.

How many days do you need?

Ten days is comfortable and a week is workable, because the island is small and the trains are fast. A first trip that works: three or four nights in Taipei, a night or two in Tainan or Taichung on the way down, two or three in Kaohsiung, and a day trip into the mountains from whichever of them you prefer. Five days is enough for Taipei and one trip out of it. If you want the east coast as well, add four days, because the east is a separate journey rather than a detour.

Do I need a car?

No, and for most visitors a car is a nuisance. The high speed rail runs the length of the populated west coast, the conventional railway rings the whole island, and Taipei, Taichung and Kaohsiung all have metro systems. One stored-value card, an EasyCard or an iPASS, pays for the metros, the city buses, the local trains and your coffee in the convenience store. Where a car genuinely helps is the east coast and the mountain roads, and scooter hire is the local answer in small towns, though Taiwanese traffic is not the place to learn to ride one.

Is Taroko Gorge open?

Only partly, and you must check before you build a trip around it. The magnitude 7.4 earthquake that struck off Hualien in April 2024 brought down large sections of the gorge road and many of the trails, and Taroko National Park has been reopening in stages ever since rather than all at once. Some areas and roads have come back; others remain shut, and aftershocks and typhoons have closed parts again more than once. We are deliberately not printing a list of what is open, because any list we print will be out of date before you read it. Read the park's own announcements at the time you book, and have a second plan for that day.

Is Taiwan expensive?

It is one of the better-value places in East Asia and noticeably cheaper than Japan, South Korea or Singapore. Transport is the standout: the length of the island by high speed rail costs a fraction of the equivalent ride in Japan, and a metro journey across Taipei is small change. Eating is where the value is most obvious, because the night markets and the small neighbourhood shops are not the cheap option but the good one. Hotels in central Taipei are the main expense and rise sharply around Lunar New Year. Tipping is not customary; better hotels and smarter restaurants add a ten per cent service charge instead.

What do I need to enter Taiwan?

Citizens of the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, the European Union and Schengen countries, Australia, New Zealand, Japan and South Korea, among others, are admitted visa-free for ninety days with a passport valid for the length of the stay and an onward or return ticket. Some other nationalities get thirty or fourteen days, and some need a visa applied for in advance, so check your own passport on the Bureau of Consular Affairs site at boca.gov.tw rather than assuming. Separately, there is a free Taiwan Online Arrival Card to fill in before you land, which has replaced the paper arrival card; do it on the official government site and not through an agent charging a fee for the same form.

Is Taiwan safe, and what about earthquakes and typhoons?

Taiwan is one of the safest places you can travel. Street crime is rare, walking at night is normal and lost property has a habit of coming back. The two real hazards are natural. Earthquakes are frequent, most are small, and modern buildings are built for them; learn where the stairs are and otherwise do not lose sleep over it. Typhoons are the one that will actually change your plans, mostly between July and September: a typhoon day closes offices, trains and attractions, city by city, and the announcements come the evening before. Build one slack day into a summer trip and treat domestic flight and ferry bookings as provisional.

What should I eat?

Start with beef noodle soup, which Taiwan treats as a national dish and argues about endlessly, and then follow the queues. The night markets are the obvious way in, and the proper method is to buy one small thing from several stalls rather than a meal from one: oyster omelette, pepper buns, scallion pancake, braised pork over rice, stinky tofu if you are brave and sweet potato balls if you are not. Soup dumplings are a Taipei speciality. Bubble tea was invented here in the 1980s, in Taichung or Tainan depending on which tea house you believe, and nobody has settled it yet.
