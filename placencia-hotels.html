Placencia

Where to Stay in Placencia

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Placencia is the mainland’s answer to the cayes: a long thin finger of sand running south into the Caribbean from the Stann Creek coast, with the sea on one side, a lagoon on the other and a fishing village at the end where the road stops. It has the best beach in Belize by a comfortable margin, a genuine village rather than a resort strip, and a spread of hotels along sixteen miles of peninsula that run from simple rooms behind the Sidewalk to full resorts with their own docks. It is also the doorway to the southern cayes and to the jaguar reserve inland.

Where to stay in Placencia, area by area

Placencia is not a town so much as a sixteen-mile peninsula with a village at the end of it. One road runs the length of it, the sea is on your left going south and the lagoon on your right, and the only real question is how far up that road you are prepared to be.

Placencia village

At the southern tip, where the road stops. The village is small, friendly and walkable, with restaurants, bars, tour operators, the fishing dock and the famous Sidewalk — a narrow concrete footpath running behind the beachfront that was for years the main street and is still the pleasantest way to move about. Staying here means you walk to everything, you can change your mind about dinner, and you are where the boats leave from. It also means smaller, simpler hotels, closer together, with less room around them.

Maya Beach

At the northern end of the peninsula, perhaps forty minutes’ drive from the village. This is the quietest stretch: a thin line of small hotels and houses with a great deal of empty sand between them, a handful of good restaurants, and very little else. It is the right choice for a week of reading and swimming, and the wrong one if you expect to go into the village in the evening, because that is an hour of driving there and back in the dark.

Seine Bight and the middle

Between the two sits Seine Bight, a Garifuna village, and either side of it the larger resorts and the golf-course development. The middle of the peninsula is a compromise: more space than the village, less isolation than Maya Beach, and a ten- or twenty-minute drive to the restaurants at either end. Most of the bigger properties with pools and full facilities are along here.

The beach, honestly

This is the best mainland beach in Belize and the reason most people come. It is a genuine sand beach you can walk for miles and wade into, which distinguishes Placencia sharply from the cayes. The water is shallow and calm but not the clear turquoise of the outer reef — the peninsula sits well inside it, and the snorkelling that Belize is famous for is a boat ride out at Laughing Bird Caye or the southern cayes, not off the sand here.

Getting around Placencia

Getting here

The flight from Belize City takes a little under an hour with a stop or two and lands at the airstrip just north of the village; it is the simplest way and the one most visitors use. By road it is a long drive — the Hummingbird and Southern highways, then the peninsula road — of four hours or more from the international airport, though the Hummingbird stretch is one of the finest drives in the country if you have the day for it. Buses run to the village from Dangriga and Independence.

On the peninsula

One road, running the full length. Golf carts are the usual hire and are available in the village; a car is better if you are staying at the northern end or plan to drive inland. Shared taxis and hotel shuttles run up and down. Distances are real: Maya Beach to the village is not a walk.

In the village

Entirely walkable. The Sidewalk runs most of its length and is flat, though narrow and uneven in places, and the sand paths either side of it are soft.

Boats

Everything offshore leaves from the village docks: the snorkel and dive trips to Laughing Bird Caye and the southern cayes, the fishing boats, the whale-shark trips in season, and the boat south to Punta Gorda and on to Guatemala.

When to come, and what it costs

Placencia runs on the same calendar as the rest of Belize: dry and busy from December to April, green and cheaper from June to November. September and October are the wettest and the quietest, and this far south the rain is heavier than on the northern cayes; a number of businesses close for part of that stretch. Christmas, New Year and Easter are the dearest fortnights of the year.

The one thing peculiar to Placencia is the whale-shark season off Gladden Spit, traditionally around the full moons of the spring months. Sightings have been far less dependable in recent years than the marketing suggests, so treat it as a possibility rather than a reason to book. The fuller picture is in Best Time to Visit Belize.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Most visitors from the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, the European Union and much of the Commonwealth may enter Belize as tourists without arranging a visa in advance, and are admitted for a limited stay that can usually be extended at an immigration office for a fee. A passport with sufficient remaining validity, onward or return travel, and an address for the first night are the ordinary requirements.

Beyond that we do not print the numbers, and nor should anyone else. Entry rules, the length of stay granted, and the departure and border fees are exactly the details that change without notice. Check the current position with the Belize Immigration and Nationality Department and with your own government’s travel advice shortly before you fly.

Placencia — questions

Village or Maya Beach?

The village if you want to walk to dinner, meet people and be where the boats leave from, and are content with smaller hotels close together. Maya Beach if you want space, silence and a long empty stretch of sand, and are content to eat at the few places up there or to drive. The two ends of the peninsula are forty minutes apart, and underestimating that drive is the commonest mistake visitors make here.

Is Placencia better than the cayes?

It is different rather than better. Placencia has the real beach — sand you can walk for miles and wade into — which the cayes largely do not. The cayes have the reef on the doorstep, which Placencia does not; here the good snorkelling is a boat trip out to Laughing Bird Caye or the southern cayes. If beach matters most, Placencia. If the reef matters most, Ambergris Caye or Caye Caulker.

Do I need a car or a golf cart?

In the village, no — it is walkable. Anywhere north of the airstrip, yes. Golf carts are the usual hire and are fine for the peninsula road. Take a car instead if you want to drive inland to the Maya sites or the Cockscomb Basin, which is a very worthwhile day out and not a golf-cart journey.

How long is the drive from the airport?

Four hours or more from the international airport near Belize City, by the Hummingbird and Southern highways and then down the peninsula. The roads are surfaced and fine. Most visitors fly instead, which takes under an hour including the stops and costs considerably more. If you have the time and like driving, the Hummingbird Highway is worth doing once.

Can I see whale sharks?

Perhaps, and only in a narrow window. They have historically been reported off Gladden Spit around the full moons of the spring months, when snapper spawn there, and operators run trips then. Sightings have become much less reliable in recent years. Go if the dates fall right and treat a sighting as a bonus; do not build the trip around it.

Is there much to do besides the beach?

A good deal, and most of it is inland. The Cockscomb Basin Wildlife Sanctuary, the jaguar reserve, is about an hour and a half away and is one of the best walks in Belize. Garifuna Hopkins is half an hour north. Monkey River, mangrove and manatee trips, and the southern cayes all leave from the village docks. Placencia also has the best concentration of restaurants outside San Pedro.
