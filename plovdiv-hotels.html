Plovdiv

Where to Stay in Plovdiv

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Plovdiv has been lived in for something like eight thousand years, which makes it one of the oldest continuously inhabited cities in Europe, and the remarkable thing is how lightly it carries it. The city sits on and around a group of syenite hills above the Maritsa on the Thracian plain: a Thracian settlement became Roman Trimontium, which left a theatre still used for opera and a stadium whose curved end is now exposed under the main shopping street like a cross-section through the city. Above it all is the Old Town, a hill of enormous painted merchant houses from the 1840s and 1850s on lanes of round cobbles. Below, Kapana — the old craftsmen’s quarter, whose name means the trap — is now the best few streets of bars, workshops and small galleries in Bulgaria. It was European Capital of Culture in 2019 and spent the money well. It is also the hottest city in the country in July, which is worth planning a day around.

Where to stay in Plovdiv, area by area

The parts of Plovdiv a visitor actually chooses between — and the one real decision here is whether you sleep on the hill or under it.

The Old Town on Trimontium

Most atmosphericSteepBig cobbles

The hill itself: lanes of vast nineteenth-century merchant houses with overhanging upper floors painted in ochre and blue, several of them open as museums, the Roman theatre on the saddle, the Ethnographic Museum, and a handful of small guest houses and boutique hotels in restored houses. Staying here is the most atmospheric choice in the city and the least convenient: the lanes are steep, the cobbles are the original large round sort, cars mostly cannot reach the doors, and there is almost nothing in the way of shops. Wonderful for two nights if your knees are willing.

Kapana, the creative quarter

NightlifeWalkableCentral

A grid of narrow lanes between the Main Street and the mosque, originally the craftsmen and traders’ quarter, now full of small bars, coffee places, galleries, studios and some of the best eating in the city. It is flat, it is two minutes from the Main Street and ten minutes from the Roman theatre, and it is where the city’s evening happens. Noisier than the rest of central Plovdiv from Thursday to Saturday, though mild by the standards of most European nightlife districts. The best all-round choice for a first visit.

The Main Street and Tsar Simeon Garden

FlatShopsEasiest walking

Knyaz Alexander I, the long pedestrianised spine of the modern city, running from the Dzhumaya mosque and the exposed end of the Roman stadium down past the post office to the Tsar Simeon Garden with its singing fountains. Most of the larger and mid-range hotels are on or just off it. This is the level, practical choice: shops and cafes at the door, Kapana and the Old Town both within a ten-minute walk, taxis available, and no cobbles unless you go looking for them.

Sahat Tepe and the southern streets

QuietResidentialGood value

South and west of the garden, around the Clock Tower hill and the streets towards the stadium and the fairground: ordinary residential Plovdiv, with apartment blocks, local restaurants, a park on a hill with a view over the whole city, and prices below the centre. It is a fifteen to twenty-minute walk in, or a short taxi ride. A sensible choice for a longer stay, for families, or when the central hotels are full during the fair or a festival week.

The station and the fairground end

Best valueTransportPlain

Around the railway station south of the centre and the International Fair grounds across the river to the north. This is the cheapest end of the city and it is convenient if you are arriving by train from Sofia or Burgas, or here for the fair. It is a plain district with little to look at and a twenty-minute walk or a five-minute taxi to the Main Street. Fine for a night either side of a train; not where the city shows itself.

Getting around Plovdiv

On foot

Central Plovdiv is a walking city and you will need almost nothing else. The Main Street, Kapana, the mosque, the stadium and the Tsar Simeon Garden are all flat and within a few minutes of one another. The Old Town is the exception: it is a genuine hill with steep, large-cobbled lanes, and the climb from the Main Street up to the Roman theatre will take a quarter of an hour at a comfortable pace.

Buses and taxis

City buses cover the outer districts and the station; a visitor staying centrally rarely needs one. Taxis are plentiful and cheap, and the same rule applies as in Sofia: the per-kilometre rate is displayed on the rear side window, so glance at it, or book through an app. A taxi to the top of the Old Town is the standard move for anyone who would rather not walk up.

Getting to and from Sofia

About two hours by road on the motorway, which the buses use and which makes the coach the quickest and most frequent option. Trains take a little longer and are cheaper still. Plovdiv has its own small airport with a handful of seasonal routes, but almost every visitor arrives through Sofia.

Day trips

Bachkovo Monastery is half an hour south, the second-largest in the country and far quieter than Rila. Asen’s Fortress is a little beyond it, on a crag above the gorge. The Thracian Valley wine estates are spread east and west along the Maritsa. Kazanlak and the Valley of the Roses are about an hour and a half north over the hills, and the painted Thracian tomb there is worth the drive. A car opens all of this up; without one, organised days run from the city.

The heat, and how it shapes a day

Plovdiv is reliably the hottest city in Bulgaria, and in July and August the middle of the day on the Main Street is genuinely hard going. The local answer is the right one: out early, a long lunch somewhere shaded, the hill and the museums late in the afternoon, and the evening in Kapana. The Tsar Simeon Garden and the Old Town lanes hold shade; the pedestrianised Main Street does not.

When to come

May, June, September and October are the best months here by a wide margin. The city is warm rather than hot, the terraces are full, the Old Town is pleasant to climb and the wine country around it is at its best in September. July and August are very hot — regularly past thirty-five degrees — and while the city works perfectly well if you shape the day around the shade, it is not the fortnight to choose if you have one. Winter is cold and often grey, but December with the Christmas lights on the Main Street is a good short break, and the museums and the eating are unaffected.

Two dates worth knowing: the International Fair fills the city’s hotels at the end of September, and the summer season at the Roman theatre runs from about June to September, which is the only chance to use the building for what it was built for. Check the programme if that appeals — sitting in a second-century theatre for an opera is the best evening Plovdiv offers.

Getting in: what applies, and what does not

Bulgaria is in the European Union and, since 1 January 2025, a full member of the Schengen area — air and sea borders opened in March 2024 and the land borders followed on New Year’s Day 2025. Arriving from another Schengen country is now an internal journey with no routine passport check, which is a real change from older guidebooks. Bulgaria is also in the euro area: it adopted the euro on 1 January 2026 at a rate fixed at 1.95583 lev to the euro, and the lev stopped being legal tender on 1 February 2026, so prices here are in euro and there is no currency to change.

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen external border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave. Ninety days in any one hundred and eighty is the visa-free tourist allowance for American, Canadian, British and Australian visitors, and it is counted across the whole Schengen area rather than against Bulgaria alone.

Plovdiv — questions

Which area of Plovdiv should I stay in?

Kapana for a first visit: flat, central, the best eating and two minutes from the Main Street. The Main Street itself if you want level ground, shops at the door and a wider choice of mid-range hotels. The Old Town if atmosphere matters more than convenience and you can manage a steep cobbled hill. The southern streets or the station end for value.

Is Plovdiv worth staying overnight, or is it a day trip from Sofia?

Stay. It is two hours from Sofia and a great many people do it as a day trip, which gets you the Roman theatre and a walk up the hill and misses the thing that makes the city — the evening, when the Kapana lanes fill and the Old Town empties of coaches. Two nights is right for a first visit, and it also puts Bachkovo and the wine valley within reach.

How hard is the Old Town to walk?

Harder than it looks on a map. It is a real hill, the lanes are steep and surfaced with the original large round cobbles, and there are few handrails. Flat shoes with a proper sole make the biggest difference. If stairs or uneven ground are a problem, take a taxi to the top near the Ethnographic Museum and walk down, and note that the Roman theatre has a level approach from the upper side.

Is the Roman theatre still used?

Yes, and that is the best thing about it. It was built in the second century, seats a few thousand, looks south over the plain towards the Rhodopes, and holds a season of opera, theatre and concerts from roughly June to September. Tickets are inexpensive by any European standard. Outside the season it is open as a monument.

What else is there besides the Old Town?

The Roman stadium under the Main Street, with the curved end excavated and open; the Dzhumaya mosque from the fourteenth century; Kapana for the evening; the Tsar Simeon Garden and its singing fountains; the Regional Archaeological Museum; and the hills — Sahat Tepe with the clock tower and Bunardzhik for the view over the city. Half an hour out is Bachkovo Monastery, and the Thracian Valley wine estates are spread along the river.

Do I need cash in Plovdiv?

Very little. Bulgaria has used the euro since 1 January 2026 and cards are taken almost everywhere a visitor goes, including the museums and most of Kapana. Keep a few coins for a church candle or a market stall. Prices quoted in lev on older websites are from before the changeover.
