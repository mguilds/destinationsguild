Skagen

Where to Stay in Skagen

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Skagen is the town at the very top of Denmark, where the country narrows to a sandbar and two seas meet in plain view. It is a summer place and does not pretend otherwise: a fishing harbour, yellow houses, enormous skies and a light that brought a whole school of painters here in the 1870s. The practical choice is between the town, the beaches east of it and the smarter old settlement on the west side.

Skagen town and the harbour

Most convenientRestaurantsWhere the train stops

The town itself is a grid of low yellow-washed houses with red roofs and white trim, laid out between the railway station and a working fishing harbour that is still one of the busiest in Denmark. Staying here puts you within a walk of the restaurants on the quay, the Skagen museum and the bakeries, and it is the only part of the area where you can manage comfortably without a car.

Østerby and the eastern beaches

East of the harbour the houses thin out towards Sønderstrand and the long sandy beaches on the Kattegat side, which are the sheltered ones and the warmest water. This is holiday-house country more than hotel country, so expect rented summer houses and a handful of small places to stay. Lovely in June; very quiet in November.

Gammel Skagen — the old town on the west side

SunsetsSmarterNeeds a car or the bus

Gammel Skagen, or Højen, sits four kilometres west on the Skagerrak side, which is where people go for the sunset. It is the smarter end of the area — a few well-known hotels, a handful of restaurants and a steep little beach — and it is noticeably dearer in July. Without a car you will be relying on the local bus or a long walk into town for dinner.

Getting around

The town is small and flat and you can walk it in twenty minutes. In season a local bus runs out to Grenen and west to Gammel Skagen, and the sand tractor — a tractor-drawn trailer — carries people the last stretch across the sand to the point for anyone who would rather not walk it. Bicycles are hired all over town and are the usual way to get about.

Outside the summer months the bus service thins considerably and a car becomes much more useful, particularly for Råbjerg Mile and the buried church. The railway from Frederikshavn runs all year.

When to come, and what it costs

Skagen has a short, crowded season and a long, empty rest of the year, and it is worth choosing deliberately. July and the first half of August are the Danish holiday weeks: the harbour restaurants are full, the summer houses are let and rooms cost what they cost. June and the first half of September give you long light, open restaurants and space, and are our own pick.

From late October to March a good deal of the town closes, the wind comes off the Skagerrak without anything in its way, and the appeal is a wholly different one — empty beaches, weather and the museum. Fine if that is what you came for; a disappointment if you were expecting the postcard. Our month-by-month guide to Denmark sets out the year.

Denmark uses the Danish krone (DKK, written kr), not the euro. Denmark is a member of the European Union but voted to keep its own currency and has set no date for changing it, so euro notes are not legal tender — though the krone is held in a fixed band against the euro, which is why the rate barely moves from one year to the next. Pay in kroner. Denmark is one of the most nearly cashless countries in the world: cards and phones are taken everywhere, including on buses, at museum desks and at market stalls, and a good many places no longer handle notes at all, so you need very little cash and may need none. Prices in shops include the twenty-five per cent sales tax, which is among the highest in Europe and is already in the figure on the shelf. Tipping is not expected and never chased — service is in the bill, and rounding up is plenty. Decline any card machine that offers to charge you in dollars; pay in kroner and let your own bank convert.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS — the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years — is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today — nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Denmark has been in the European Union since 1973 and in the Schengen area since 2001, so everything above applies here in full and an arrival from anywhere else in Schengen involves no passport control at all. Denmark did not join the euro, so the currency is the krone. If you plan to cross to Malmö in Sweden, carry your passport even though the bridge is inside Schengen: Sweden has run identity checks on that crossing on and off for years.

Skagen hotels FAQ

Is Skagen worth the journey?

If you are already in north Jutland, yes. The sandbar at Grenen, where the Skagerrak and the Kattegat meet in a visible line of colliding water, is a genuine curiosity, and the light that drew the Skagen painters here in the 1870s and 80s is still the thing people remark on. If you are starting from Copenhagen it is the better part of a day each way, so give it two or three nights or leave it for another trip.

When should I go to Skagen?

June or September. July and early August are the Danish holiday weeks, when the town is full, the rooms are at their dearest and the harbour restaurants need booking. Late May, June and the first half of September give you the same light with room to move. From November to March a good deal of the town is shut and the wind comes straight off the sea — wonderful if that is what you want, bleak if it is not.

How do I get to Skagen without a car?

By train. The regional line runs from Aalborg to Frederikshavn and a small local train carries on to Skagen, about two hours in total, and the station is in the middle of town. Once there, the town and the harbour are walkable, a bus runs out to Grenen and to Gammel Skagen in season, and bicycles are hired everywhere — which is how most Danes do it.

What is there to do in Skagen?

Walk or ride the sand tractor out to Grenen and stand with a foot in each sea; the Skagens Museum, which holds the paintings made here; the buried church, whose tower stands alone in the dunes south of town; Råbjerg Mile, a migrating dune large enough to walk up; and the harbour itself at the hour the boats come in.
