Olympic National Park

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

A Visitor's Guide · Olympic Peninsula, Washington · Mountains, rain forest and 65 miles of wild coast

Three parks in one: snowy peaks above Port Angeles, the moss-hung rain forests of the Hoh and Quinault, and a Pacific shoreline of sea stacks and driftwood. Here is how to see it without wasting a day on the road.

Before you go

Olympic is not one place but several, strung around a loop of highway, and that shapes everything about planning a visit.

There is no road through the middle of Olympic National Park. The mountains and old-growth valleys at its heart are 95 percent designated wilderness, and the drivable parts sit around the edges, reached by spur roads off U.S. Highway 101 as it circles the peninsula. Hurricane Ridge is 17 miles uphill from Port Angeles. The Hoh Rain Forest is about two and a quarter hours from there, and Lake Quinault in the southwest corner is farther still. Think of the park as a ring of separate destinations, and give each one its own half-day or day.

The most practical plan for most travelers is two bases: Port Angeles for Hurricane Ridge, the Elwha and Lake Crescent, and Forks or the coast for the Hoh Rain Forest, Rialto Beach and Kalaloch. If you have a week, add a night near Lake Quinault on the way south. Trying to see the whole loop from a single hotel means long days behind the wheel on a two-lane road.

Weather rules this park. The western valleys are some of the wettest country in the lower 48, and winter storms close roads, campgrounds and trailheads on short notice. In late November 2026 the park had the Hoh, Mora and Staircase areas closed ahead of an incoming storm. Before every day's drive, check the park's alerts page or call the Road & Weather line at 360-565-3131.

Two more things to know before you go: the park does not accept cash at its entrance stations, so carry a card, and cell service is thin to nonexistent in the river valleys and along much of the coast. Download your maps and reservations before you leave town.

Best things to do in Olympic National Park

Six places that make the trip, spread around the loop.

Mountains

Hurricane Ridge

A 17-mile climb from Port Angeles to meadows and a wall of Olympic peaks. Short paved paths start at the parking area; bring your own food and water, since nothing is sold up top since the 2023 fire.

Rain forest

Hoh Rain Forest

Moss-draped maples and giant spruce along the Hall of Mosses and Spruce Nature trails. Summer parking fills early, so the park suggests arriving before 8 a.m. or after 5 p.m.

Lake

Lake Crescent

A deep, glacier-carved lake right on Highway 101, with the short walk to Marymere Falls, the paved Spruce Railroad Trail along the north shore, and the historic Lake Crescent Lodge.

Coast

Rialto and Ruby Beach

Sea stacks, driftwood logs and tide pools. Rialto is near La Push on the Mora road; Ruby Beach is a short, steep path off Highway 101 north of Kalaloch.

Hot springs

Sol Duc Valley

Mineral pools at Sol Duc Hot Springs Resort and a forest walk to Sol Duc Falls. The road and resort close for winter; in 2026 the resort ran March 20 to November 1.

Rain forest

Lake Quinault

The quieter southern rain forest, with short trails such as Maple Glade and Kestner Homestead and shore roads that make a loop around the lake in summer. Part of the South Shore Road is unpaved, and the park does not recommend these roads for RVs.

From the Guild

Guild tip: Build your trip around the weather, not the calendar. If the Ridge is socked in, drive to the coast or the rain forest, where fog and drizzle are part of the show, and save Hurricane Ridge for the first clear morning. The Hurricane Ridge webcam on the park website tells you in ten seconds whether the drive is worth it.

When to visit Olympic

Summer is the easy season. From July through September the weather is at its driest, Hurricane Ridge and the Sol Duc road are open, the lodges are all running, and ranger programs are on. It is also when campgrounds book out, the Hoh lot fills by mid-morning and Port Angeles hotels are at their highest rates. Book lodging and reserved campsites months ahead.

Spring and fall are the sweet spot for travelers who can live with some rain. May, early June and late September bring thinner crowds, and most facilities are open, though high-country snow lingers into early summer. In 2026, campground reservations were required from mid-May to late September at most reservable campgrounds, and from June 12 to September 6 at the Hoh.

Winter, roughly late November through March, is wet on the coast and in the valleys and snowy up high. Hurricane Ridge opens on winter weekends when the weather allows, for snowshoeing, cross-country skiing and tubing, and rangers lead snowshoe walks on weekend afternoons starting December 26, 2026. Kalaloch Lodge stays open all year, and a winter storm watched from its bluff is one of the park's real pleasures. Expect closures on short notice.

How to get to Olympic National Park

By car

U.S. Highway 101 circles the Olympic Peninsula and reaches nearly every part of the park. From Seattle, most people cross Puget Sound on a Washington State ferry (Seattle to Bainbridge Island, Edmonds to Kingston, or Coupeville to Port Townsend) and drive on toward Port Angeles by way of SR 104 and Highway 101. From Tacoma, SR 16 and SR 3 lead to the same route. Note that the Hood Canal Bridge on SR 104 closes from time to time for passing boats.

From the south, pick up Highway 101 at Olympia or at Aberdeen on the coast. Driving times are long: from the Port Angeles visitor center it is about 45 minutes to Hurricane Ridge, 2 hours 15 minutes to the Hoh Rain Forest or Kalaloch, and 2 hours 30 minutes to Quinault.

By air, ferry and bus

Seattle-Tacoma International is the main airport. Victoria International in British Columbia also serves the area, and the Black Ball Line's Coho ferry runs daily between Victoria and Port Angeles (bring a passport).

There is no park-wide shuttle. The Dungeness Line bus connects Sea-Tac with Port Angeles, and Clallam Transit runs local routes to Port Angeles, Forks, Lake Crescent and La Push. In recent summers Clallam Transit has also run a Hurricane Ridge shuttle from downtown Port Angeles; check its schedule before you count on it. For most visitors, a car is the only practical way to see the park.

Where to stay near Olympic National Park

There are only a handful of lodges inside the park, so most visitors sleep in Port Angeles on the north side and Forks on the west side and drive in each day.

Lodges inside the park

Four lodgings operate inside the park, each run by a private concessioner under contract with the National Park Service. Book through each lodge's own website, which the park lists on its lodging page, and book early: rooms for July and August go fast.

Lake Crescent Lodge sits on the south shore of Lake Crescent with lodge rooms, cabins and cottages; its 2026 season runs April 17, 2026 to January 1, 2027. Log Cabin Resort, on the lake's north shore, offers cabins, lodge rooms and an RV and campground in summer (May 15 to September 29 in 2026). Sol Duc Hot Springs Resort has cabins, suites and the hot spring pools, open March 20 to November 1 in 2026. Kalaloch Lodge, on the bluff above the Pacific, is the only one open year-round, with lodge rooms and cabins.

Just outside the park boundary on the south shore of Lake Quinault, in Olympic National Forest, stands Lake Quinault Lodge, a grand rustic lodge built in 1926 and listed on the National Register of Historic Places. It makes a fine base for the Quinault Rain Forest.

RV travel & camping at Olympic

Olympic is RV-friendly in places and RV-hostile in others. Know your length and your road before you go.

The park runs about a dozen campgrounds. Most of the larger ones (Fairholme on Lake Crescent, Heart O' the Hills below Hurricane Ridge, Hoh, Kalaloch, Mora, Sol Duc and Staircase) take RVs up to 21 feet, with a few sites up to 35 feet. In summer, Fairholme, Hoh, Kalaloch, Mora, Sol Duc and Staircase require reservations on recreation.gov, though most hold back some sites for first-come, first-served. Heart O' the Hills, Ozette and South Beach are first-come, first-served. NPS campgrounds generally run $15 to $24 a night.

None of the park-run campgrounds has hookups or showers. The only hookups inside the park are at Sol Duc Hot Springs RV Park (17 back-in sites, 26 to 36 feet, water and electric) and Log Cabin Resort RV & Campground on Lake Crescent (38 sites up to 35 feet, full hookups), both summer-only and booked through the resorts. Dump stations are at Fairholme, Kalaloch, Mora and the Sol Duc Campground; Kalaloch and Mora charge $10 a use.

Roads matter as much as campgrounds. RVs and trailers are prohibited on the Graves Creek Road, and the park does not recommend them on the Quinault shore roads, the North Fork Quinault, the Queets roads, Deer Park, Obstruction Point or Hurricane Hill roads. Hurricane Ridge Road itself is paved, but in winter every vehicle must carry chains. Private RV parks with full hookups are found in and around Port Angeles and Forks for travelers who want power and showers.

Fishing in Olympic

Fishing inside Olympic follows the park's own rules. A Washington recreational fishing licence is not required in park waters, except when fishing the Pacific Ocean from shore. Anyone fishing for salmon or steelhead does need a free Washington catch record card, and harvesting shellfish on the coast requires a Washington shellfish licence. Children under 15 need no licence.

Wild fish must be released unless the park's regulations say otherwise, and bull trout and Dolly Varden may not be targeted anywhere in the park. Most rivers and Lake Crescent require artificial lures with barbless single-point hooks. Lake Crescent is catch-and-release only, open June 1 to October 31 in 2026. The Elwha River is closed. Rules vary river by river, so read the current park fishing regulations before you wet a line.

Accessible travel at Olympic

What is available for visitors with limited mobility, and the passes that help.

The Olympic National Park Visitor Center in Port Angeles is accessible, with accessible parking and restrooms, a wheelchair to borrow, touch and braille exhibits, and a captioned orientation film. The Hoh Rain Forest Visitor Center is accessible and lends a wheelchair when staffed; it reopens December 4, 2026, Friday to Sunday, 9 a.m. to 4 p.m.

Good short paths for limited mobility include Madison Falls in the Elwha (0.1 mile, paved and gently sloping, to a 60-foot waterfall), the paved Big Meadow path at Hurricane Ridge (a quarter mile, up to 9 percent grade), the Hoh's Mini Rain Forest loop (0.1 mile, compacted gravel, under 5 percent), and Big Cedar near Kalaloch (0.3 mile, under 7 percent). The Hall of Mosses is hillier, with steps and rocky stretches. The park is candid that there is no accessible route to any of its coastal beaches, though the Beach 4 overlook at Kalaloch is reachable.

Fairholme, Heart O' the Hills, Hoh, Kalaloch, Mora and Sol Duc campgrounds have accessible restrooms and paved sites in at least one loop. The park's accessibility listing notes accessible public areas at Kalaloch, Lake Crescent and Sol Duc lodges, an accessible cabin at Kalaloch, and accessible pools and cabins at Sol Duc. Ask the lodge directly about the room you are booking.

Park passes that save you money. U.S. citizens and permanent residents with a permanent disability can get the free lifetime Access Pass. Visitors 62 and over can buy the Senior Pass ($80 for life, or $20 a year). Veterans and Gold Star families get a free Military Lifetime Pass, and current service members a free annual pass. Each covers entrance fees at every national park.

Traveling on dialysis? The nearest dialysis clinic is Northwest Kidney Centers' clinic in Port Angeles, the park's headquarters town on the north side of the peninsula. Book your treatment at a clinic there several weeks before you go; the large dialysis companies run travel desks that will arrange it with your home unit.

Olympic by cruise ship

Seattle is one of the West Coast's busiest cruise home ports, with ships sailing to Alaska from spring through early fall. Olympic makes an easy pre- or post-cruise add-on: take a Washington State ferry across Puget Sound, spend two or three nights between Port Angeles, Lake Crescent and the coast, and be back in Seattle for embarkation. Allow a buffer day before sailing, since ferry lines and Highway 101 traffic can run long in summer.

Olympic National Park FAQ

How much is the entrance fee for Olympic National Park?

$30 per private vehicle, $25 per motorcycle, or $15 per person on foot or bicycle, valid for seven consecutive days. An Olympic annual pass costs $55. The park does not accept cash.

Do I need a reservation to enter Olympic National Park?

No. There is no timed-entry system. You do need reservations for most campgrounds in summer, through recreation.gov, and for the in-park lodges.

Is Hurricane Ridge open?

In winter the road typically opens to uphill traffic 9 a.m. to 4 p.m. Friday through Sunday and some holidays, weather permitting, starting November 28, 2026. Chains are required for all vehicles. Call 360-565-3131 for the day's status.

Is the Hurricane Ridge lodge open?

No. The Hurricane Ridge Day Lodge burned in May 2023. Temporary trailers provide restrooms and a visitor contact station, but there is no food, indoor warming area or drinking water. The park is in early planning for a replacement.

Where should I stay to visit Olympic National Park?

Port Angeles for Hurricane Ridge and Lake Crescent, and Forks or Kalaloch for the Hoh Rain Forest and the coast. Inside the park, Lake Crescent Lodge, Log Cabin Resort, Sol Duc Hot Springs Resort and Kalaloch Lodge offer rooms and cabins.

How long does it take to drive around Olympic National Park?

Allow at least two to three days. From Port Angeles it is about 45 minutes to Hurricane Ridge, 2 hours 15 minutes to the Hoh or Kalaloch, and 2 hours 30 minutes to Quinault.

Do I need a fishing license in Olympic National Park?

Not for most park waters. A Washington licence is required only to fish the Pacific Ocean from shore, and a free catch record card is required for salmon and steelhead.

Can I take an RV into Olympic National Park?

Yes, on the main roads. Most campgrounds take RVs up to 21 feet with some sites to 35 feet; hookups exist only at Sol Duc RV Park and Log Cabin Resort. RVs are prohibited or not recommended on several gravel side roads.
