Where to Stay in Kathmandu

Where to Stay in Kathmandu — Thamel, Boudha & Patan

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Kathmandu is three or four cities wearing one name, and which of them you sleep in decides what your trip feels like. Thamel is the trekkers’ quarter — loud, cheap, useful, and ten minutes from everything you need to hire. Boudha, out to the northeast, is a Tibetan town around a great white stupa, and the calmest place in the valley to wake up. Patan, across the Bagmati, is a medieval city in its own right that most visitors see for an afternoon and wish they had slept in.

Choosing Your Area in Kathmandu

Where you sleep shapes the whole stay. Here is the honest lay of the land, quarter by quarter.

Thamel and the old city

A dense grid of lanes north of Durbar Square, stacked with guest houses, gear shops, bakeries and trekking agencies. It is the practical choice for a first or last night: you can buy a down jacket, book a bus, change money and eat at midnight within three streets. It is also noisy until late and the lanes are chaotic with scooters. Ask for a room at the back, and away from a rooftop bar.

Boudha, around the stupa

Six kilometres northeast, built in rings around one of the largest stupas in the world. Monasteries, Tibetan restaurants, incense and the slow clockwise shuffle of pilgrims at dawn and dusk. Hotels here are quieter, often with a terrace looking at the dome, and the atmosphere in the hour after sunset is the single most atmospheric thing in the valley. The trade-off is half an hour each way to the old city.

Patan (Lalitpur), across the river

The valley’s second royal city, now continuous with Kathmandu but still entirely its own: a Durbar Square many visitors prefer to Kathmandu’s, working metal and woodcarving workshops in the back lanes, and a handful of restored courtyard hotels. Quieter and greener than Thamel, with better food, and only a short taxi ride from the centre.

Know Before You Book

The handful of things that change how a Kathmandu trip goes.

Air, season and the winter months

Kathmandu sits in a bowl at about 1,400 metres, which keeps it mild but also traps the air. From roughly December to February a haze can settle over the valley and sit there for days, taking the mountains out of the view and bothering anyone with a chest. October and November are the clearest months of the year and the reason that is the high season. Nights are cold in winter and many older hotels heat rooms poorly, so ask what the heating actually is before you book a January room.

Getting in, and how you will leave

Tribhuvan International Airport (KTM) is the only international gateway most visitors use, and it is small for the traffic it carries — allow more time than the distance suggests at both ends. Nepal’s own airlines have been on the European Union’s air safety list since 2013 and were still on it through 2026 while the government worked to be removed; most travellers still fly domestically because the mountain roads take days, but it is a real consideration and worth your own reading before you book an internal hop.

Traffic, and where your hotel really is

Distances in the valley mean very little; the traffic decides. Boudha to Thamel can be fifteen minutes or fifty. Check a map before you book rather than trusting a hotel’s own description of itself as central, agree a taxi fare before you get in or use a ride app, and if you have an early flight, leave absurdly early. Pavements are uneven and often absent, so a torch is worth carrying after dark.

Kathmandu Hotel FAQ

The questions travellers ask most before booking a room in Kathmandu.

Where is the best place to stay in Kathmandu?

For a first visit with trekking to arrange, Thamel, because everything practical is within walking distance. For a calmer stay, Boudha, which has the best evenings in the valley. For food, quiet and medieval streets, Patan. A common and very good plan is a night in Thamel at each end of a trek and the middle nights in Boudha or Patan.

How many days do you need in Kathmandu?

Three full days covers the valley properly: one for Kathmandu Durbar Square and Swayambhunath, one for Boudha and Pashupatinath, and one for Patan and Bhaktapur. Most people give it two on the way in and regret it. If Bhaktapur is on your list, consider sleeping there for a night rather than making it a day trip — it is a different place after the buses leave.

Is Kathmandu high enough to cause altitude sickness?

No. At about 1,400 metres the valley is lower than Denver, and arriving travellers feel nothing worse than tiredness from the flight. Altitude becomes a real matter once you fly or drive up to trekking country — Lukla is around 2,840 metres and the trails climb steeply from there — which is why serious itineraries build in acclimatisation days rather than racing.

Do you need a visa for Nepal?

Most visitors, including United States passport holders, obtain a tourist visa on arrival at Tribhuvan International Airport and at the main land borders, and there is an official online form that can be completed in advance to shorten the queue. Fees, lengths of stay and the list of nationalities who must apply in advance all change, so confirm the current rules with Nepal’s own immigration authority before you travel, and use only the official government website.
