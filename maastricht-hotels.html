The areas, and the least Dutch city in the country

Maastricht is the oldest city in the Netherlands and the least Dutch-feeling — Roman, Burgundian, Catholic, and pressed up against Belgium and Germany rather than the flat green heart of Holland. The centre is barely a mile across, split by the Maas into the Binnenstad on the west bank and Wyck on the east, joined by a pedestrian bridge, and it is essentially flat — you can walk from the station to the great Vrijthof square in fifteen or twenty unhurried minutes without a hill. The catch is the surface, not the gradient: the old town is laid in cobbles and setts almost everywhere, hard on wheeled cases and slippery in rain, so cushioned, grippy shoes matter more here than fitness. The one real climb is the Sint-Pietersberg on the southern edge, home to the caves — treat that as an optional excursion by taxi or bus.

Vrijthof & the Markt (west bank)

The postcard Maastricht: the great squares, the basilicas, the shopping streets and most of the restaurants. Best if you want to step out into the sights — ask for a courtyard-facing room to escape the square’s evening cafe noise.

Onze Lieve Vrouweplein & the Jekerkwartier

The quiet, pretty half of the old town: a tree-shaded square, then a warren of student-quarter lanes and the old city wall. The calmest central option — but the small hotels here are historic houses where a lift is not guaranteed.

Wyck (east bank)

The station side: independent shops, good restaurants, and everything within five hundred metres of the platforms. The obvious choice if you arrive by train, have heavy luggage, or plan to day-trip to Liège and Aachen.

Boschstraat / Sphinxkwartier (west bank, north)

A regenerated former ceramics-factory district, now home to the two smartest hotels in town. Quieter and less touristed, still under ten minutes’ walk to the Vrijthof.

Luxury

An honest note first: the famous Kruisherenhotel, in the old Crutched Friars monastery and church, is closed for renovation until October 2026 — so if an older guide sends you there, it is not open. These two are the top of the market that is trading now, and both are lovely.

Hotel Maison Maastricht Centrum – Handwritten Collection

$$$$ LuxuryBoschstraat / SphinxkwartierOpened 2025Lift · wheelchair accessible

Maastricht’s newest five-star, reviving a nineteenth-century hotel address on Boschstraat with a wine bar, a patisserie and a cheese-and-wine cellar rather than a big formal restaurant. It is genuinely well set up for less mobile guests: the tourist board lists a lift, ground-floor rooms, accessible toilets, anti-allergenic rooms, air conditioning and a twenty-four-hour desk. It is about fifteen minutes on foot from Maastricht Centraal — flat, but over the bridge and some cobbles — and under ten minutes to the Vrijthof; valet parking is available.

Boutique

One hotel on the prettiest square with a Roman road in its cellar, and one family-run townhouse hotel that has been in the same hands since 1912.

Derlon Hotel Maastricht

$$$ BoutiqueOnze Lieve VrouwepleinRoman ruins in the cellarLift · roof terrace

A hotel on this site since 1870, sitting right on the tree-shaded Onze Lieve Vrouweplein beside the Basilica of Our Lady, with a private archaeological cellar containing a Roman road and sanctuary on the old Via Belgica — guests only, and breakfast is sometimes served down there. There are fifty rooms, a French restaurant and a roof terrace. For older travellers this is one of the easiest choices in town: a lift, a twenty-four-hour desk, entirely non-smoking, and the square itself quiet after the evening trade dies down — roughly a twelve-minute flat walk from the station across the bridge.

Family-friendly

Pet-friendly

Honest framing: nearly every historic-centre hotel in Maastricht refuses dogs. The dog-friendly options are on the edge of town, and both below publish a clear policy.

What to see, and how close two other countries are

Maastricht rewards a slow two or three days, and it puts two more countries within a short train ride.

The two great squares

The Vrijthof is the big ceremonial square, cafe terraces down one side and two churches on the other, including the centuries-old Sint-Servaasbasiliek over the grave of St Servatius. A few minutes south, the smaller, leafier Onze Lieve Vrouweplein is dominated by the fortress-like Romanesque Basilica of Our Lady — the most pleasant place in the city to sit with a coffee.

Boekhandel Dominicanen

A bookshop inside a thirteenth-century Dominican church that lost its sacred function two centuries ago, with a tall steel walk-up book stack in the nave and a cafe in the apse. Regularly called one of the most beautiful bookshops in the world, and free to wander — step-free at the entrance, though the upper book decks are stairs only.

The Sint-Pietersberg caves

Two man-made marl cave systems in the hill south of town, around thirty metres underground, with charcoal drawings, wartime inscriptions and the vault where Dutch national art treasures were hidden. Guided tour only, cold enough to bring a cardigan, and explicitly not suitable for people with walking difficulties — reach the meeting point by taxi or bus rather than walking up.

Belgium and Germany on the doorstep

Liège in Belgium is around half an hour to fifty minutes by direct train; Aachen in Germany, with Charlemagne’s cathedral, is about an hour by train or a frequent bus that drops you by the cathedral. Both are comfortable day trips from a city that has spent its whole history between borders.

Maastricht — common questions

How do I get to Maastricht from Amsterdam?

By a single direct train, no changes, of about two and a half hours — two hours twenty-three at its fastest — running roughly every half hour from Amsterdam Centraal via Utrecht, 's-Hertogenbosch and Eindhoven, and terminating at Maastricht. Because it is direct you can settle in and stay put, which is worth knowing if you are travelling with luggage. Advance fares on the NS site are cheaper than walk-up ones; check ns.nl for the exact times on your day.

Is Maastricht worth the trip so far south?

For many visitors it is the most memorable stop in the country precisely because it feels so unlike the rest of it — the oldest city in the Netherlands, Roman and Burgundian and Catholic, with two beautiful squares, a bookshop in a Gothic church, caves under the hill, and Belgium and Germany a short train ride away. It rewards a slow two or three days rather than a rushed day trip. If your trip is short and you have never seen the classic Holland of canals and windmills, do that first; if you have, Maastricht is a wonderful contrast.

When is Maastricht busy?

Two dates fill every bed in the city. Carnival, in February, when the whole place dresses up and normal sightseeing stops, and TEFAF, the world’s leading art and antiques fair in March, which sells out hotels across Maastricht and well into Belgium and Germany and multiplies the prices. Both are worth coming for if that is your aim, and worth avoiding otherwise. The wise windows are late April to early June and mid-September to late October: mild, long light, terraces open, and no fair inflating the rates.

Where should an older traveller stay in Maastricht?

In Wyck, on the station side, if ease matters most: the good hotels there are within a few hundred metres of the platforms, so you arrive and walk a short flat distance with your luggage before crossing the bridge for the sights. The west-bank hotels on and around the Vrijthof and Boschstraat put you in the middle of everything and the two smartest, Maison and Monastère, both have lifts. The quiet Jekerkwartier is loveliest but its small historic hotels do not all have lifts, so confirm before booking. The whole centre is flat — it is the cobbles, not hills, that tire you.

Can I visit the Sint-Pietersberg caves if I am not very mobile?

Honestly, probably not. The cave operator states outright that the guided tours are not suitable for people with walking difficulties: they run about an hour and ten minutes over uneven ground, they are cold, and the meeting point is up the Sint-Pietersberg hill on the southern edge of town. If you want to go, take a taxi or bus to the meeting point rather than walking up. If mobility is a real concern, enjoy the flat old town, the squares and the bookshop instead — there is more than enough there for two good days.
