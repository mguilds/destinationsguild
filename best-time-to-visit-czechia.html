When to go to Czechia

Best Time to Visit Czechia

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated August 2026

Czechia has a proper four-season climate and, unusually, three separate good times to visit rather than two. There is late spring, when the country is green and open and not yet crowded. There is early autumn, when the light turns and the Moravian wine harvest begins. And there is December, which is cold and dark and lit end to end with Christmas markets. What you are really choosing between is warmth, crowds and price — and in this country they move together more predictably than in most.

The short answer

If your dates are flexible, these are the months to aim at.

Go in May or September

These are the two best months in the Czech calendar and it is not a close contest. Warm days, long light, everything open, and crowds well below the July and August peak. May has blossom and the Prague Spring music festival; September has warmth, golden light and the beginning of the Moravian wine harvest.

Or October, for colour and quiet

Cooler, often bright, with real autumn colour along the river valleys and in the parks — and noticeably lower prices and shorter queues than September. The evenings turn cold by the end of the month. For unhurried travellers who dislike crowds, this may be the best choice of all.

Or December, for the markets

The Christmas markets in Prague, Brno and Olomouc run from the start of Advent into early January and are among the finest in Europe. It is cold and dark and the weekends are busy, but a weekday evening among the stalls is worth planning a trip around.

Avoid mid-July to late August — in two places

The problem is not the country; it is Prague’s Old Town and Český Krumlov, both of which are genuinely uncomfortable at midday in high summer. Moravia, the spa towns and the countryside stay perfectly civilised all summer. If August is your only window, base yourself in Brno or Olomouc.

Czechia month by month

Weather, crowds, prices and what is actually happening, one month at a time.

January — Coldest and quietest

Deep winter. Days are short, temperatures sit around freezing and often below, and there can be snow. Prague is at its emptiest and cheapest, and the castle in snow is a genuinely beautiful sight with almost nobody in it. Bring proper cold-weather clothing and expect early darkness.

February — Cold, cheap, clearing

Much like January but with the light beginning to return. Still properly cold. Hotel rates are at their lowest of the year and museums, concert halls and beer halls are all at their most agreeable when it is grim outside. A good month for a city break if you do not mind the cold.

March — The turn

Unpredictable — it can be raw and grey or it can be the first soft week of the year. Prices are still low and crowds still thin. Gardens and castle grounds outside the cities are often closed or only just reopening, so this is a month for towns rather than countryside.

April — Spring arrives

The country begins to look like itself again. Blossom in the parks, castles reopening for the season, and comfortable walking temperatures by the end of the month. Easter brings markets and a particular set of Czech traditions. Rain is likely; pack for it.

May — The best single month

Warm without being hot, long light evenings, everything open, gardens at their peak, and the crowds not yet at full strength. If you can choose freely, choose May. The Prague Spring music festival fills the concert halls, and the beer gardens open in earnest.

June — Excellent, and filling up

Long days, reliable warmth and the whole country open for business. The crowds are heavier than May but still manageable in the first half of the month. Moravia is lovely now. Book accommodation further ahead than you would in spring.

July — Hot and busy

Peak season. Prague’s Old Town and Český Krumlov are genuinely crowded between mid-morning and evening, and the heat in the city can be heavy. Countryside, the spa towns and Moravia stay far more civilised. Go early in the day and rest in the afternoon.

August — The busiest, and the emptiest

The height of the tourist season and, oddly, the month when Prague’s own residents leave. Some smaller restaurants and shops close for holidays. Hot, crowded and expensive in the honeypots. If you must come now, base yourself in Moravia and treat Prague as a day or two.

September — The second sweet spot

Arguably as good as May and warmer. The crowds thin markedly after the first week, the light turns golden, and the wine harvest begins in Moravia — the burčák season, when young partly-fermented wine appears everywhere, is a real and delightful thing.

October — Autumn colour, low crowds

Cooler but often bright and clear, with superb colour in the parks and along the river valleys. Prices fall, queues shorten, and the city looks its best in low sun. By the end of the month it is properly cold in the evenings. An excellent time for an unhurried trip.

November — Grey, then lit

The bleakest few weeks of the Czech year — damp, grey and dark by four in the afternoon — right up until the Christmas markets open in the last days of the month and everything changes. Cheap, quiet, and rewarding if you like a city to yourself.

December — Christmas markets

The third season. Prague’s Old Town Square, Brno and Olomouc fill with wooden stalls, mulled wine and roasting almonds from around the start of Advent into early January. It is cold and it is busy at weekends, but it is one of the great winter experiences in Europe. Go midweek.

Crowds, and how to dodge them

Czech crowding is concentrated in two places and a handful of hours. Knowing that is worth more than choosing the perfect month.

It is two places, not a country

Prague’s historic core and Český Krumlov absorb the overwhelming majority of the country’s visitors. Brno, Olomouc, the spa towns, south Bohemia and the whole of Moravia are quiet even in August. Building a trip that spends two or three days in Prague and the rest elsewhere solves the crowding problem almost entirely.

It is also a matter of hours

The coach parties and the day-trippers work a predictable shift: roughly ten in the morning to six in the evening. The Charles Bridge at seven in the morning is a different structure from the Charles Bridge at noon. Get up early, rest in the afternoon, and go out again after seven. This single habit transforms a summer trip.

Stay the night in Český Krumlov

Most visitors see the town on a day trip from Prague or on the way to Austria, which means it is packed from late morning to late afternoon and nearly empty either side. Staying overnight gives you the evening and the early morning in a town that is genuinely magical when it is quiet.

Book ahead in the peak, not in the trough

From June to early September, and over the Christmas market weekends, book accommodation and any concert tickets well in advance. From November to March you can travel almost spontaneously and pay considerably less for better rooms.

The four seasons, plainly

Spring (April–May)

Cool and changeable in April, reliably pleasant by May. Parks and castle gardens reopen and look their best. Rain is common in April; pack a proper coat. May is the finest month of the Czech year.

Summer (June–August)

Warm to hot, with occasional genuinely hot spells and afternoon thunderstorms. Long daylight — light until well past nine in June. Beer gardens open, outdoor concerts everywhere, and the honeypot towns at their most crowded. Confirm air conditioning before booking.

Autumn (September–October)

The connoisseur’s season. September is warm and golden with the wine harvest under way in Moravia; October is cooler, colourful and quiet. Prices ease steadily through both months.

Winter (November–March)

Cold, often around or below freezing, with snow possible and darkness by mid-afternoon. November is the bleakest stretch; December is transformed by the markets; January and February are cold, cheap and beautifully empty. Rural castles and gardens are largely closed.

Timing your Czech trip — questions

What is the single best month to visit Czechia?

May. Warm but not hot, long evenings, every castle and garden open, and the summer crowds not yet arrived. September runs it very close and is a touch warmer. If your dates are fixed and neither is possible, early October and early June are the next best.

When are Prague and Český Krumlov least crowded?

November, January, February and early March are the quietest months by a wide margin. Within any month, the hour matters as much as the season: Prague’s Old Town and the Charles Bridge are calm before about nine in the morning and again after seven in the evening, even in August. Český Krumlov empties completely once the day-trip coaches leave in the late afternoon, which is the strongest argument for staying the night there.

How cold does it actually get in winter?

Cold enough to matter. Prague in January and February typically hovers around freezing, dipping below it at night, with occasional colder spells and some snow. It is a dry cold rather than a damp one, which most visitors find more bearable than the number suggests, but you need a proper coat, gloves and shoes with grip — the cobbles are slick when wet or icy.

Are the Christmas markets worth a special trip?

Yes, if you dress for it. They run from the start of Advent through to early January in Prague, Brno and Olomouc, among many others. Olomouc’s is the loveliest and least besieged of the three; Prague’s Old Town Square is the most photogenic and the most crowded, especially at weekends. Go on a weekday evening.

Is summer too hot in Prague?

It can be uncomfortable. July and August regularly bring genuinely hot spells, and a great deal of older Czech accommodation has no air conditioning. If you are travelling in high summer, confirm air conditioning explicitly before booking rather than assuming it, and plan your sightseeing for the morning.

When is the wine and beer season?

Beer is year-round, but the beer gardens open properly from May to September. Moravian wine has a distinct season: the grape harvest in September brings burčák, a cloudy young wine sold for only a few weeks, and the wine festivals through September and early October are among the most enjoyable events in the Czech calendar.

Does anything close out of season?

Yes, and it catches people out. Many castles, châteaux and their gardens outside the cities close entirely from around November to March, or open only at weekends in the shoulder months. City museums, galleries, concert halls and churches stay open year-round. If a particular castle is the reason for your trip, check its own opening calendar before fixing dates.
