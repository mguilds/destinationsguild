Where to Stay in Bogotá

Where to Stay in Bogotá — Best Hotels & Areas

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated August 2026

Bogotá is a capital of eight million people spread along a high green plateau under the Andes, and it is a city where the choice of district decides almost everything about the trip. It has the finest museums in the country, some of the best restaurants in South America, and a reputation for danger that is thirty years out of date in the places visitors actually go — and still worth respecting in the places they do not.

Four districts matter. Zona G, in northern Chapinero, is where we send nearly everyone: a few leafy, well-lit blocks of restaurants with the best hotels in the city among them, walkable in the evening. Zona Rosa, also called the Zona T, is the shopping and nightlife quarter just north of it, livelier and a little louder. Usaquén, further north again, is a former colonial village with a famous Sunday flea market, quieter and further from the museums. And La Candelaria, the colonial old town, is where the Gold Museum, the Botero collection and the cathedral are — magnificent by day, and empty enough after dark that we would not book a bed there.

Before you book anything: the altitude. Bogotá sits at 8,660 feet (2,640 m) — higher than Aspen, and higher than almost anywhere most American visitors have slept. For the great majority it means nothing worse than breathlessness on stairs, a light headache and a poor first night’s sleep, and it passes within a day or two. The precautions are free: take the first day slowly, drink far more water than feels necessary, go easy on alcohol the first evening, and leave the Monserrate cable car — another 1,600 feet up — for day two or three. If you have heart or lung disease or take blood-pressure medication, this is a conversation to have with your own doctor before you book, not with us. And if your trip includes the coast, doing Cartagena first and Bogotá second is easier on the body than the other way round.

Luxury

Bogotá’s best hotels are small and are almost all within a few streets of one another in Zona G — which means you can walk to dinner, and in this city that is worth a great deal.

Four Seasons Hotel Casa Medina Bogotá

A 1946 house of hand-carved stone and timber, declared a national monument and now the loveliest hotel in the city — sixty-odd rooms round a glass-roofed courtyard, thick walls, real fireplaces in the suites and a quiet that is rare in Bogotá. It sits in the middle of Zona G, so the best restaurants in the country are a three-minute walk away, and Four Seasons service handles altitude, luggage and early flights without being asked twice. The rooms in the original building are the ones to have.

What You Need to Know Before You Book

Four things that decide how Bogotá goes.

Which district, honestly

Zona G for most peopleCandelaria by day

Zona G (northern Chapinero): our default recommendation. A handful of quiet, leafy blocks holding the best restaurants in Colombia and the best hotels in the city, safe and pleasant to walk in the evening, and a short ride from everything else. If you take one piece of advice from this page, stay here.

Zona Rosa / Zona T: ten minutes north. Shops, bars, restaurants and more noise. Excellent if you want somewhere to go after dinner; less peaceful if you do not.

Usaquén: a colonial village swallowed by the city, with a good Sunday flea market and a village square full of restaurants. Charming, calm, and a long way from the museums.

La Candelaria: the historic centre and the reason to come in daylight — the Gold Museum, the Botero collection, the Plaza de Bolívar, the cable car up Monserrate. It empties in the evening and we would not book a first-time visitor a bed there, with the single exception of the Hotel de la Ópera.

Traffic, taxis and the airport

Allow 90 minutes in rush hourUse an app

Bogotá’s traffic is genuinely bad. Rush hour runs roughly seven to nine in the morning and five to eight in the evening, and a journey that takes twenty-five minutes at noon can take an hour and a quarter at six. Allow ninety minutes to El Dorado airport if you are travelling at those times, and rather more on a Friday.

Use a ride app or a hotel car rather than hailing a taxi in the street; at the airport, take an authorised taxi from the official rank inside the terminal or a pre-booked car, and ignore anyone who approaches you in the arrivals hall. The fare from the airport to Zona G is usually 30,000 to 45,000 pesos. Do not rent a car: the traffic, the one-way system and the pico y placa licence-plate restrictions make it a poor idea for a visitor.

The weather, and what to pack

66°F day, 47°F nightAll year

Bogotá has one temperature and it does not change: around 66°F by day and 47°F at night, in January exactly as in July, because the city sits on the equator at high altitude. What varies is the rain — drier in January to March and July to August, wetter in April to June and September to November, usually as a heavy shower late in the day.

Pack layers, a light waterproof and shoes with grip; the pavements are uneven and the afternoon rain is real. Almost no hotel here has air conditioning and none needs it — check that the heating works instead, particularly in older colonial buildings. And bring lip balm: the air at this altitude is very dry.

Safety, in plain terms

North is comfortableOrdinary precautions

In Zona G, the Zona Rosa, Chapinero Alto and Usaquén, Bogotá is a comfortable city to walk in by day and in the evening, and these are among the best-policed districts in Colombia. The risks that actually reach visitors are opportunistic rather than dramatic: phone snatching in the street, pickpocketing on the crowded TransMilenio buses, trouble with taxis hailed on the street, and drink-spiking in bars.

So: keep the phone in a pocket rather than a hand, use a ride app, treat La Candelaria and the city centre as daytime places, never leave a drink unattended, and be wary of invitations from people met online or in bars. The U.S. advisory rates Colombia as a whole Level 3 and names five Level 4 areas — none of them is anywhere near Bogotá. Our Colombia guide sets that out in full.

Frequently Asked Questions

The questions Guild readers ask most about staying in Bogotá.

Which part of Bogotá should I stay in?

Zona G is the answer for most visitors: a compact, leafy, well-lit quarter of restaurants in northern Chapinero, safe to walk in the evening, with the best hotels in the city inside a few blocks. Zona Rosa (the Zona T), a little further north, is the shopping and nightlife equivalent. Usaquén, further north again, is a former colonial village with a Sunday flea market and a village feel, quieter and further from the museums. La Candelaria, the colonial old town, holds the Gold Museum and the Botero collection and is wonderful by day, but it empties after dark and we would not book a hotel there for a first visit.

How bad is the altitude in Bogotá?

Bogotá sits at about 8,660 feet (2,640 m), higher than Aspen. Most visitors notice it only as breathlessness on stairs, a light headache and a broken first night’s sleep, and it passes in a day or two. The precautions cost nothing: take the first day slowly, drink far more water than you think you need, skip the second drink on the first evening, eat lightly, and let someone else carry the bag. Anyone with heart or lung disease, or on blood-pressure medication, should raise it with their own doctor before booking. Arriving from the coast rather than straight from sea level helps, and the cable car up Monserrate takes you a further 1,600 feet — leave that for day two or three.

Is Bogotá safe for visitors?

In the northern districts we recommend — Zona G, Zona Rosa, Chapinero Alto and Usaquén — Bogotá is comfortable to walk by day and in the evening, and these are among the best-policed parts of the city. The risks that reach visitors are opportunistic: phone snatching, pickpocketing on the TransMilenio, taxi trouble if you hail on the street, and drink-spiking in bars. Use a ride app rather than hailing, keep the phone out of sight, and treat La Candelaria as a daytime destination. Colombians have a phrase for the general principle: no dar papaya, don’t hand anyone the opportunity.

Are there adults-only hotels in Bogotá?

Not in any meaningful sense, and we would rather say so than invent one. Adults-only is a resort format, and Bogotá is a working capital of eight million people — we could not verify a single genuine adults-only hotel here through our booking partner. What does exist is plenty of small, quiet, grown-up boutique hotels where children are simply rare: the Click Clack and Casa Medina both feel that way in practice. For a true adults-only property in Colombia, look to Cartagena or the coast.

How do I get from El Dorado airport to my hotel?

Take an authorised taxi from the official rank inside the terminal, a pre-booked hotel car, or a ride app — not a driver who approaches you in the hall. The northern hotel districts are about forty minutes away in ordinary traffic and considerably more in rush hour, which in Bogotá means roughly seven to nine in the morning and five to eight in the evening. Budget an hour and a half to get back to the airport at those times. The fare to Zona G is usually in the region of 30,000 to 45,000 pesos.

What is the weather like in Bogotá, and what should I pack?

Cool and changeable in every month: highs around 66°F by day, lows around 47°F at night, with no seasonal variation to speak of. Bring layers, a light waterproof and comfortable shoes with grip, because the afternoon showers are heavy and the pavements are uneven. January to March and July to August are the drier stretches. Very few Bogotá hotels have air conditioning and none of them need it — check instead that the heating works.
