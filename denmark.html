Denmark

Denmark Travel Guide

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Denmark is a small, flat, well-run country of about six million people, made of one peninsula reaching up from Germany and some four hundred islands scattered east of it — and that geography does most of the work. Nowhere is far from the sea, nowhere is far from anywhere else, bridges and trains have replaced most of the ferries, and the highest ground in the country would not count as a hill in Norway. What you get in exchange for the lack of drama is the easiest travelling in Scandinavia: short distances, flat towns, bicycles instead of cable cars, and a capital you can cross on foot.

Why visit Denmark

A small country that has quietly made itself the most comfortable place in the north to be a traveller.

It is the easiest country in the north to travel in

Short distances, level ground, punctual trains, and a language problem that does not exist — English is spoken fluently almost everywhere. A great deal of Denmark can be done without ever renting a car.

Copenhagen earns its reputation

A capital of harbours and squares that is genuinely walkable end to end, with a serious design tradition, one of the best restaurant cities in Europe, and a waterfront people actually swim in through the summer.

The bicycle is not a gimmick

Denmark built its towns around cycling before it was fashionable, with separated lanes, their own traffic lights and bridges built for bikes. Hiring one for a day is the cheapest good decision you will make here.

Hygge is a real thing, not a slogan

Strip away the gift-shop version and what is left is a national habit of making indoor winter comfortable — candles, long meals, small rooms, nothing hurried. It is why a dark January evening in Denmark is pleasanter than the latitude suggests.

Denmark at a glance

Capital

Copenhagen, on the island of Zealand, facing Sweden across a three-mile strait.

Money

The Danish krone (DKK, kr). Not the euro, although the rate is held close to it. Cards and phones everywhere; cash is nearly obsolete.

Language

Danish. English is spoken fluently and almost universally, including by people in their seventies and eighties.

Borders

In the European Union and in the Schengen area, but not in the euro. One land border, with Germany; a bridge and a tunnel to Sweden.

Getting about

Trains the length of the country, a good bus network, and bridges where there used to be ferries. Copenhagen to Aarhus takes about three hours.

Shape of it

Small and flat. The highest point is about 170 metres, and no part of the country is more than roughly thirty miles from salt water.

The regions

Five Denmarks, and a week comfortably takes two or three of them.

Copenhagen & Zealand

The capital and the big eastern island around it. Royal palaces, the harbour baths, and — within an hour by train — Kronborg at Helsingør, the Viking ships at Roskilde and the modern art at Humlebaek.

Things to do in Denmark

Tivoli Gardens

The pleasure garden in the middle of Copenhagen, open since 1843 and still the best thing of its kind in Europe. Gardens, old wooden rides, concerts and lights, and a season that now runs through summer, Halloween and Christmas.

The harbour by water

A harbour bus down the Copenhagen waterfront costs the price of a bus ticket and shows you the opera house, the new library and the Little Mermaid from the water. In summer, the harbour baths are free and clean enough to swim in.

Louisiana, at Humlebaek

Modern art in a low house in a sculpture park above the sound, forty minutes north of Copenhagen by train. Many people rate it the finest small museum in Europe, and the setting is half the reason.

Kronborg and the Viking ships

Shakespeare’s Elsinore is a real Renaissance castle on the water at Helsingør; Roskilde has five excavated Viking ships in a hall built for them. Either is an easy day out of the capital.

Aarhus in a day

Den Gamle By rebuilds a Danish town street by street across three centuries, and ARoS keeps a circular rainbow-glass walkway on its roof. Both are ten minutes from the station.

Skagen and the two seas

Stand on the sandbar at Grenen with a sea on each side, then look at the paintings the light here produced a century ago. Go in June or September rather than July.

Best time to visit & getting there

Denmark has one obvious season and two quiet ones, and the gap between them is smaller than the latitude suggests — the sea keeps the winters mild and the summers moderate. Summer, roughly mid-June to mid-August, is when everything is open and the light is extraordinary: better than seventeen hours of daylight at midsummer in Copenhagen, and dusk that never quite finishes. It is also when Danish families take their own holidays, so the coast is busy and the cities are oddly quiet.

The shoulders, May and September, are our own pick. The days are still long, the gardens and the beaches are open, rates step down, and the famous places are calm. Late May in particular is a lovely time here — everything green, nothing crowded, and the light already running to ten at night.

Winter is dark rather than cold. Copenhagen is at the latitude of Edinburgh and keeps roughly seven hours of daylight at midwinter, with temperatures that hover either side of freezing and rain more often than snow. It is grey, and the Danes have organised their indoor life around that fact for centuries, which is why the Christmas markets, the candlelight and the long meals work as well as they do. Our month-by-month guide to the best time to visit Denmark goes through it season by season.

Getting there is simple. Copenhagen is the biggest airport in Scandinavia and takes nonstop flights from a dozen North American cities as well as everywhere in Europe; Billund in central Jutland is the second airport and useful if Legoland or west Jutland is the point. From Copenhagen airport the train and the metro both reach the city centre in about a quarter of an hour, and trains run on from the same station to Odense, Aarhus and Aalborg without changing in the city.

Denmark uses the Danish krone (DKK, written kr), not the euro. Denmark is a member of the European Union but voted to keep its own currency and has set no date for changing it, so euro notes are not legal tender — though the krone is held in a fixed band against the euro, which is why the rate barely moves from one year to the next. Pay in kroner. Denmark is one of the most nearly cashless countries in the world: cards and phones are taken everywhere, including on buses, at museum desks and at market stalls, and a good many places no longer handle notes at all, so you need very little cash and may need none. Prices in shops include the twenty-five per cent sales tax, which is among the highest in Europe and is already in the figure on the shelf. Tipping is not expected and never chased — service is in the bill, and rounding up is plenty. Decline any card machine that offers to charge you in dollars; pay in kroner and let your own bank convert.

Fishing here

Denmark is a coastal fishing country above all else. With several thousand miles of shoreline and nowhere far from it, the national obsession is sea trout from the beach — waded, with a fly or a spinner, right through the cold months when the inland rivers are shut. The rivers themselves are modest by Nordic standards, with the Skjern and the Varde in west Jutland the best known for salmon, and the lakes are mostly pike, perch and zander. Anyone between eighteen and sixty-five needs the state fishing licence, which is bought online by the day, the week or the year and covers the coast as well as fresh water; privately stocked put-and-take lakes sell their own ticket instead. Travelling in your later years

Flat ground, short journeys and universal English — Denmark is the gentlest country in Scandinavia to take at your own pace.

The ground is kind

Denmark is flat, the pavements are level and wide, and lifts, ramps and accessible lavatories are the rule in stations, museums and any hotel built in the last forty years. The Copenhagen metro is step-free from street to platform at every station.

Short days out, not long ones

Nothing worth seeing is more than about three hours from anything else, which means a trip here can be built out of half-days with a sit-down in between — unusual in Scandinavia and the single best reason to choose Denmark over its neighbours.

Cobbles are the one hazard

The old quarters of Copenhagen, Aarhus, Odense and Ribe are cobbled and sometimes uneven, and the bicycle lanes run fast and close to the kerb. Look left before stepping off a pavement; the bikes have right of way and they are quiet.

Trains make it easy

Danish intercity trains are level-boarding or ramp-assisted, seat reservation is cheap, and staff will arrange help at both ends if you ask when booking. The fast trains between Copenhagen, Odense, Aarhus and Aalborg are the spine of any sensible itinerary.

Pharmacies and care are excellent

Standards are high and English is universal among medical staff. Bring your prescriptions in their original packaging with a copy of the prescription, as you would anywhere.

Budget honestly

Denmark is expensive, and the twenty-five per cent sales tax is already in every price you see. Where it rewards care is in eating: hotel breakfasts are generous and usually included, bakeries are superb and cheap, and an open sandwich at a lunch counter is a proper Danish meal rather than a compromise.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS — the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years — is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today — nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Denmark is a straightforward case. It has been in the European Union since 1973 and in the Schengen area since 2001, so everything above applies here in full, and flying in from Berlin, Amsterdam or Stockholm involves no passport control at all. What Denmark has not joined is the euro, so the currency is the krone. Two things catch people out. The crossing to Sweden over the Øresund Bridge is inside Schengen but Sweden has run identity checks on it on and off for years, so carry your passport on the train to Malmö even though it is a twenty-minute hop. And Greenland and the Faroe Islands, though Danish, are outside both the European Union and Schengen — a trip on from Copenhagen to either is an international journey with its own rules.

Denmark travel FAQ

When is the best time to visit Denmark?

Late May to early September for the long days, the open gardens and the coast; and within that, late May and the first half of September for the best balance of light, prices and calm. July is the warmest and the busiest on the beaches, though Copenhagen itself empties out as Danes go on holiday. Winter is mild but dark, with about seven hours of daylight at midwinter — worth it for the Christmas markets and the indoor life, but not for a first visit.

How many days do you need in Denmark?

Four days does Copenhagen properly with a day trip. A week lets you add Odense and Aarhus by train without hurrying. Ten days gets you to the top of Jutland and Skagen, which is the point at which the country stops being a city break and starts being a journey. Distances are short, so the limit is your appetite rather than the map.

Is Denmark in the euro?

No. Denmark is a member of the European Union but kept its own currency, the Danish krone, after a referendum in 2000, and has set no date for changing. The krone is held in a fixed band against the euro, so the rate barely moves, but euro notes are not legal tender and the few places that accept them give a poor rate. Pay in kroner, by card almost everywhere.

Do I need a visa or ETIAS for Denmark?

Not at present. American, Canadian, British, Australian and most other visa-free travellers may stay in the Schengen area for up to ninety days in any one hundred and eighty. The Entry/Exit System now takes your fingerprints and photograph at the border on your first crossing. ETIAS, the paid travel authorisation, is not yet in force and no applications are being taken.

Is Copenhagen expensive?

Yes, by most standards, though less eye-watering than Oslo or Zurich. Hotels and restaurant dinners are where it hurts; transport, museums and everyday food are closer to the rest of northern Europe. The cheap pleasures here are genuinely good ones — bakeries, lunch counters, the harbour baths, the parks and a hired bicycle — so a careful traveller eats well and spends most of the money on the room.

Do I need to rent a car in Denmark?

Usually not. Trains link Copenhagen, Odense, Aarhus and Aalborg frequently and quickly, and buses cover the gaps. A car earns its keep only for the west Jutland coast, the Wadden Sea and the smaller islands, where services thin out. In the cities a car is a liability: parking is dear and the street layout assumes you are on a bicycle.

What about Legoland and travelling with children?

Legoland is at Billund in central Jutland, next to the country’s second airport, and is a day in itself rather than a half-day from Copenhagen — stay nearby if it is the main event. In the capital, Tivoli does the same job for most families, and the open-air museums, the harbour buses and the aquarium fill several more days. Denmark is an easy country to travel in with children: short journeys, high chairs everywhere, and nobody minds the noise.

Is Denmark a good place to travel slowly, or in later years?

It is one of the best in Europe. The country is flat, the distances are short, the trains are level-boarding and English is universal. The only real hazards are cobbled old quarters and the bicycle lanes, which are fast and silent — look left before you step off a kerb.
