Montenegro

Montenegro Travel Guide

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Montenegro is the size of an English county and holds a fjord-like bay, three hundred kilometres of Adriatic coast, a canyon that rivals any in Europe and a plateau of glacial lakes at fifteen hundred metres. You can swim in the morning and be in deep mountains by mid-afternoon, and that compression is the whole case for coming. It is not in the European Union and not in Schengen, although it uses the euro, so the days you spend here do not eat into a Schengen allowance. The coast in July and August is as busy and as expensive as anywhere on the Adriatic; everywhere else, and every other month, is quieter and cheaper than you would expect.

Why visit Montenegro

A bay that looks like a fjord and is not one

The Boka Kotorska is a drowned river canyon, not a glacial fjord, which is why its walls are limestone rather than granite and why it is warm enough to swim in. It winds inland for nearly thirty kilometres with mountains standing straight out of the water, and the whole region is on the UNESCO World Heritage list. Kotor at the far end is the set piece, Perast the quiet one and Herceg Novi the town that actually lives there all year.

Mountains that most visitors never see

The north is a different country: Durmitor National Park with eighteen glacial lakes and dozens of peaks above two thousand metres, the Tara canyon cut several hundred metres into the limestone, and the old shepherds' summer villages on the plateau. It is three or four hours from the beaches and it is the half of Montenegro that gets left out of a short holiday.

Short distances, slow roads

Nothing is far in kilometres and most things are slower than the map suggests, because the roads bend round bays and climb through canyons. That is a feature rather than a fault — the drive up to Lovćen from Kotor, or along Lake Skadar to the sea, is one of the things people remember longest — but it means planning in hours rather than distances.

Outside the EU, and cheaper for it

Montenegro uses the euro without being in the eurozone, and prices outside the July and August coastal rush are noticeably below Croatia or Italy. The capital and the north are cheaper again. For a traveller watching a Schengen clock or a budget, both of those matter.

Montenegro at a glance

Capital

Podgorica, an unshowy modern city of under two hundred thousand in a hot inland basin. It is the transport hub and the place to pick up a hire car; Cetinje, up in the hills, is the old royal capital and still carries the ceremonial title.

Money

The euro, adopted unilaterally — Montenegro is not in the European Union and not in the eurozone. Cards are normal in hotels and in the larger coastal restaurants; carry cash for boats, beach bars, mountain guesthouses, the national park fees and anywhere inland.

Language

Montenegrin, which is mutually intelligible with Serbian, Croatian and Bosnian, written in both Latin and Cyrillic. English is widely spoken on the coast and by younger people everywhere; Russian and German are common in the resorts. A few words of greeting go a long way inland.

Borders

Outside the EU and outside Schengen, and an EU candidate in long-running accession talks. Days here do not count against a Schengen stay. Crossings to Croatia, Bosnia, Serbia, Kosovo and Albania are all ordinary land borders with passport checks.

Getting about

Driving is the practical choice and is on the right. Buses cover the coast well and the interior thinly; the Bar to Podgorica railway continues north towards Belgrade and is worth riding for the scenery. The ferry across the narrows at Kamenari saves a long loop round the bay.

Shape of it

About fourteen thousand square kilometres and roughly six hundred thousand people — smaller than Northern Ireland, with fewer people than Sheffield. The coast is around three hundred kilometres long, and the highest ground, in the Durmitor and Prokletije massifs, passes two and a half thousand metres.

The regions

Four of them, and the country is small enough that they are two or three hours apart — but they are genuinely different places to be.

The Bay of Kotor

The bay is the reason most first-time visitors come, and it is small enough to see properly in two or three days. Kotor sits at the blind end under a vertical mountain; Perast is a single row of baroque houses facing two islets; Herceg Novi guards the mouth; and Tivat across the water has the big marina and the country's main airport for the coast. The coastal road round the inner bay is beautiful and slow, and the ferry across the narrows is the local short cut.

The open coast and the riviera

South of the bay the shore opens out and turns into beach country. Budva is the busy centre of it with the nightlife and the longest choice of rooms; Bečići and Rafailovići just south have the better sand; Sveti Stefan is the islet on every poster; Petrovac is the quiet family town. Further south again, Bar is the working port and the railhead, and Ulcinj has the long sand beaches and a distinct Albanian-speaking character.

The capital, Lake Skadar and the old royal town

Inland from the coast the country opens into the Zeta plain and Lake Skadar, the largest lake in southern Europe and a national park shared with Albania. Podgorica is the practical base — airport, hire cars, trains, cheap rooms — and within an hour of it are the lake, the cliff monastery at Ostrog and Cetinje, the old royal capital with its palaces and its road up over Lovćen to the coast.

The high north: Durmitor, the Tara and Kolašin

The north is mountain country and takes a day to reach and several to do justice to. Žabljak at fourteen hundred and fifty metres is the base for Durmitor National Park, the Black Lake and the walking; the Tara canyon and the bridge at Đurđevića Tara are an easy drive from it; Kolašin to the south is the other mountain town, on the railway and closer to the capital. Biogradska Gora, one of Europe's last primeval forests, sits between them.

Things to do in Montenegro

Climb the walls above Kotor, early

The stone staircase from the old town up to the fortress of St John is well over a thousand steps and repays every one of them, with the bay unrolling below you the whole way. Go at first light or in the last hours of the day: the staircase is exposed, the middle of a summer day is punishing, and the early slot also puts you ahead of the cruise crowds. There is a fee at the gate in season, and the stone is treacherous in the wet.

Take the boat out to Our Lady of the Rocks

From Perast, small boats run the few hundred metres out to an artificial island built up over centuries by sailors dropping stones, with a church and a small museum on it. It takes an hour and it is the single most characteristic thing in the bay. The neighbouring islet of Sveti Đorđe is a monastery and is not open to visitors, which is why every photograph is taken from the water.

Drive the hairpins from Kotor up to Lovćen

The old road climbs the wall behind Kotor in a ladder of tight hairpins, with the bay dropping away beneath you at every turn, and comes out on the Lovćen massif at the mausoleum of Njegoš, the poet-prince, reached by a long tunnel of steps. It is narrow, it is not for nervous drivers or large vehicles, and it is one of the great roads in Europe. Cetinje is just beyond.

Walk the Black Lake and look into the Tara

At Žabljak the circuit of Crno Jezero is a flat, easy couple of hours through pine forest under the Bear's peak, and it is the gentlest possible introduction to Durmitor. Half an hour away the Đurđevića Tara bridge spans a canyon several hundred metres deep, with rafting trips running from the river below it through the warmer months.

Spend a day on Lake Skadar

The lake is a national park of reed beds, islands, monasteries and pelicans, and the usual way to see it is a boat trip from one of the lakeside villages. The viewpoint over the great meander of the Crnojevića river is the photograph everyone takes. It is half an hour from Podgorica and an easy half-day from the coast, and it is at its best in spring when the water is high and the birds are in.

Go up to Ostrog

The monastery is built into a vertical cliff face, white against the rock, and it is the most visited place in Montenegro for Montenegrins themselves. The approach road is steep and narrow and there is a climb from the lower monastery to the upper one. Dress modestly, go early, and expect real crowds on holy days — this is a working pilgrimage site rather than a sight.

When to go, and what it costs

Montenegro has two climates and they do not share a calendar. The coast is Mediterranean, with hot dry summers and mild, genuinely wet winters — the Bay of Kotor is among the rainiest corners of Europe, and almost all of that rain falls between October and April. The mountains are alpine, with deep snow from around December into the spring and cool nights even in August. The Guild's months are the shoulders: May, June, September and the first half of October, when the sea is warm enough to swim, the walking is comfortable, the cruise and bus crowds are thinner and prices on the coast fall by a long way from their August peak. July and August are the full experience — hot, busy, expensive and, for the nightlife in Budva, the whole point. Winter is the time for Žabljak and Kolašin and for seeing the coastal towns as the places where people live, with a good deal closed and a good deal of rain.

Fishing here

Montenegro's fishing is mostly saltwater and mostly small-boat: the bay and the open coast, with local skippers running trips out of the harbours at Kotor, Herceg Novi, Budva and Bar. Inland, the Tara and the Lim hold grayling and trout and are fished under national park and local rules that change from stretch to stretch, and Lake Skadar is a working fishery as well as a bird reserve. Permits are required almost everywhere and are the sort of thing best arranged through a local guide rather than worked out from a website. The Guild has no Montenegrin guide on the shelf yet; the general introduction is our fly fishing pages, and the whole shelf is in The Guild Fishing Library.

Travelling in your later years

The old towns are stone, and they are steep

Kotor, Budva and Herceg Novi are all polished limestone, uneven underfoot and slippery when wet, and Herceg Novi in particular is built as a stack of terraces joined by staircases rather than streets. None of this makes the country difficult — the modern parts are flat and ordinary — but it does mean choosing the address with care. Ask how many steps stand between the nearest road and the door, and do not take “central” to mean “level”.

Pick the flat line and stay on it

Almost every coastal town has one flat route: the promenade. Herceg Novi's Šetalište Pet Danica runs for kilometres at sea level, Budva's links the old town to the beach, and Dobrota's carries you into Kotor without a single step. Booking on that line, rather than above it, changes the whole character of a holiday here.

The heat is the real obstacle in summer

Podgorica is one of the hottest cities in Europe in July and August and the coastal towns hold the heat in their stone well into the night. May, June, September and October are kinder in every way — warm enough to swim, cool enough to walk, cheaper and far less crowded. If you have the choice of months, that is the Guild's advice for any age and particularly past seventy.

Boats do the work that legs would

The taxi boats across the mouth of the bay, the trip out to Our Lady of the Rocks and the Lake Skadar cruises all put you in front of the best of the country without a climb. So does the drive up to Lovćen, if someone else is driving. A day built around one boat and one long lunch sees more of Montenegro than a day built around a fortress staircase.

Getting in: Montenegro is outside the EU and outside Schengen

This is the part most visitors get wrong, and getting it right makes the trip easier. Montenegro is not in the European Union and not in the Schengen area — it is a candidate for membership and has been negotiating for years — even though it uses the euro. The practical consequence is a good one: days spent in Montenegro do not count against the ninety days in any hundred and eighty that Schengen allows you, and days spent in Schengen do not count against Montenegro’s own allowance. For anyone touring the region on a tight Schengen clock, that is worth knowing.

Citizens of the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, Australia and the European Union do not need a visa for a short holiday, and the usual allowance is ninety days within any hundred and eighty. Confirm your own nationality’s terms with your own foreign ministry before you travel rather than taking it from any website, ours included. Your passport should have been issued within the last ten years and be valid for at least three months beyond the day you plan to leave. Make sure it is stamped on the way in and on the way out: because this is not a Schengen border, the stamps are what prove how long you were in the country.

There is a registration rule, and it catches people out. Every stay has to be reported to the police. The Government of Montenegro’s own position is that this is the duty of whoever provides the accommodation, not of the visitor, and hotels and licensed apartments do it as a matter of course when they take your passport at check-in. British government advice puts it the other way round and tells visitors to make sure they are registered within twenty-four hours of arrival, warning that failing to be registered can end in a fine. Both can be true at once, and the sensible course is the same either way: if you are in a hotel it is already done, and if you are staying in a private flat or with friends, ask your host directly to confirm they have registered you and keep whatever slip they give you until you leave.

Crossing to or from Croatia is a Schengen border in both directions, so expect proper passport checks and, in July and August, queues. Europe’s Entry/Exit System now operates at those borders, which means your entry into and exit from the Schengen area is recorded biometrically; Montenegro itself is outside that system. If you are driving a hire car over the border, confirm with the rental company beforehand that it may leave the country and that you have the insurance paperwork the border may ask to see.

Montenegro — questions

How long do I need in Montenegro?

Five to seven nights sees the country properly. A good shape is two or three nights on the Bay of Kotor, two on the open coast around Budva or Petrovac, and two in the north at Žabljak, with the drive between them broken by Lake Skadar or Ostrog. Three or four nights is enough for the bay and the coast only, and that is a perfectly good holiday — it is the mountains that get squeezed out, and they are better kept for a return than done in a rush.

Do I need a car?

For the coast alone, no: buses between Herceg Novi, Kotor, Budva and Bar are frequent and cheap, and boats fill the gaps in summer. For everything else, effectively yes. Lovćen, Lake Skadar, Ostrog, the villages and the whole of the north are difficult and slow without one. If you drive, book parking with the room on the coast — Kotor and Budva in August are the hardest parking in the country — and confirm with the hire company before taking the car over any border.

Is Montenegro in the European Union, and what currency does it use?

It is not in the European Union and not in the Schengen area; it is a candidate country and has been negotiating membership for years. It does use the euro, which it adopted unilaterally, so it is a euro country without being a eurozone member. The practical upshot for a traveller is that your money works as it does in Italy or Croatia, while your days here do not count against a Schengen allowance.

Is it expensive?

Cheaper than Croatia and much cheaper than Italy, with one large exception: the coast between Budva and Sveti Stefan in July and August, where prices are Mediterranean and rooms book out months ahead. Move a month either side, or twenty kilometres inland, and the same money goes a great deal further. Podgorica and the north are the best value in the country.

How do I get there?

Tivat and Podgorica are the two airports, Tivat being the one for the bay and the coast and Podgorica the one for the capital and the interior. A great many visitors fly instead to Dubrovnik in Croatia, which has more flights, and drive down — it is a short run to Herceg Novi, but it means an external Schengen border crossing in each direction with queues in high summer, and a hire car that is explicitly permitted to leave Croatia.

Can I combine it with Croatia, Albania or Bosnia?

Yes, and a great many people do — Dubrovnik is close to the bay, Mostar and Sarajevo are a day's drive inland, and the Albanian border is just beyond Ulcinj. All are ordinary land borders with passport checks, so build in time for them in summer. The thing to check is your hire car: many companies permit some of these crossings and not others, and the paperwork has to be arranged before you collect the vehicle, not at the border.
