Pärnu

Where to Stay in Pärnu

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Pärnu is Estonia’s summer capital, which in a country this far north means a short, intense season and a long quiet one. It is a flat, low, green seaside town on a shallow bay, with a very wide sand beach, an avenue of lime trees behind it, a grand old bathing house at one end and a working old town a quarter of an hour’s walk inland. It has been a cure resort since the nineteenth century and the spa tradition is still the thing that keeps the town alive in February. Where you stay here depends almost entirely on when you come: in July the beach district is the whole point, and in November it is largely shut.

The beach district: Ranna puiestee and around

Steps from the sandSpa hotelsDearest in July

The grid of streets behind the beach is the reason most people come to Pärnu, and in summer it is where you want to be. Ranna puiestee runs parallel to the sand through an avenue of lime trees, with the grand old bathing house at one end, the park behind and the long shallow beach in front. The spa hotels — Pärnu has been a cure town since the nineteenth century and takes it seriously — are mostly here or just behind. Everything is flat and walkable, the centre is ten or fifteen minutes away on foot, and in July and August this is the most expensive accommodation in Estonia outside central Tallinn.

The old town and the centre

Restaurants and shopsOpen all yearShort walk to the sand

Pärnu’s centre is a low, pleasant few blocks of eighteenth and nineteenth-century houses between the river and the beach, with a pedestrian main street, the surviving Tallinn Gate from the old fortifications, and most of the town’s restaurants and shops. It is about fifteen minutes’ walk to the sea, which is nothing, and it stays open and populated in the months when the beach district shutters. If you are coming outside high summer, or if you would rather eat well than roll out of bed onto the sand, stay here.

Rae küla and the pine woods

QuietestPines and villasNeed to walk or drive

West of the beach the town thins into pine woods and wooden villas, with sandy tracks between them and the quieter end of the shoreline beyond. This is where Pärnu families have summer houses, and the accommodation is apartments and small guesthouses rather than hotels. It is genuinely peaceful and it smells of pine and sea, which is most of what people want from a Baltic summer. The trade is distance: you are twenty-five minutes or more on foot from the centre, so plan on a bicycle, a bus or a car.

Across the river

CheaperLocalOver the bridge

The north bank of the Pärnu river is ordinary residential town, and it is where prices drop away from the summer rates on the beach side. You cross a bridge to get into the centre, which takes a few minutes, and you give up the holiday atmosphere entirely in exchange. For a traveller passing through, or for anyone visiting outside the season when the sea is beside the point, it is sensible value. In August, when the beach is the whole reason you came, it is a false economy.

Getting around Pärnu

On foot and by bicycle

Pärnu is flat, small and built for walking, and the promenade along the beach runs for a long way in both directions. Bicycles are the local way to move in summer and are easy to hire; the town has good separated paths and no hills to speak of.

Buses

Pärnu sits on the main road between Tallinn and Riga, and the coach station in the centre is busy in both directions. The run from Tallinn takes roughly two hours by bus and the Riga services take rather longer. Local town buses cover the outer districts and the woods.

Driving

A car is useful here rather than essential: the town itself does not need one, but the bog boardwalks at Söömaa, the quiet coast towards Kihnu and the road on to Riga all open up with one. Parking near the beach is paid and busy in July. Driving is on the right, and dipped headlights are required day and night all year.

Getting to the islands

Kihnu, the small island off the coast with its own strong tradition of dress, song and a matriarchal reputation, is reached by ferry from Munalaid north-west of town and by a small plane in winter. Sailings are seasonal and weather-dependent, so treat a Kihnu day as something to arrange once you have arrived and seen the forecast rather than something to build the week around.

When to come, and what it costs

Pärnu is a summer town and it does not pretend otherwise. From late June to mid-August the beach fills, the terraces open, there are festivals most weeks and the place has the particular happiness of a northern seaside in its short season. The water is shallow for a very long way out and warms up more than anywhere else on the Estonian coast, which is why families come. June and late August are quieter and nearly as good. From October to April a large share of the beach-district businesses close for the winter, the spa hotels stay open and busy with people who are here for the treatments rather than the sea, and the town is cheap, bracing and rather melancholy in a way some travellers like very much.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Estonia itself is straightforward: in the European Union since 2004, in the Schengen area since the end of 2007 and on the euro since the beginning of 2011. An arrival from another Schengen country has no passport control at all, and an arrival from outside it is a single check at the airport you land in. The land borders with Latvia and the sea crossing from Finland are internal Schengen movements. The eastern border with Russia is an external EU frontier and the arrangements at its crossings have changed more than once in recent years, so if any part of your plan depends on one, check with the Estonian Police and Border Guard Board before you book rather than trusting anything written earlier, including this page.

One point of confusion worth clearing up: Estonian e-Residency is a digital identity for running a business online. It is not residency, it is not a visa and it gives you no right to enter or stay. If you are coming as a visitor, the Schengen rules above are the ones that apply to you.

Pärnu — questions

Is the sea actually warm enough to swim in?

By Baltic standards, yes, and that is why Pärnu exists as a resort. The bay is very shallow and sheltered, so the water warms through the summer far more than the open sea does and is comfortable for most people from around the middle of summer. It is not the Mediterranean and nobody should expect it to be. The same shallowness means you wade a long way before it is deep enough to swim, which is a nuisance for adults and the main reason families with small children choose this beach over any other in the country.

Beach district or old town?

Beach district in July and August, old town the rest of the year. In high summer the whole point is to be able to walk to the sand in two minutes, and the spa hotels and the promenade are all down there. Outside the season a good share of the beach-side businesses are shut, the sea is scenery rather than a plan, and the centre is where the restaurants, the shops and the life are — with the beach still only a quarter of an hour’s walk away.

What are the spas, and are they for tourists?

Pärnu has been a cure town since the nineteenth century, when the mud baths were built, and the tradition never stopped. Today the spa hotels run everything from ordinary saunas and pools to supervised treatment courses, and they are used by Estonians, Finns and visitors alike. They are a normal part of a stay here rather than a luxury add-on, and they are the reason the town stays open through a winter when the beach is frozen. Book treatments ahead in summer; the better-known places fill.

How far is Pärnu from Tallinn?

About two hours by bus on the main road south, with frequent services, which makes it an easy addition to a trip rather than an expedition. Riga is further — Pärnu sits roughly between the two capitals, which is why so much traffic passes through. If you are driving between Tallinn and Riga, stopping here for a night is the obvious way to break the journey.

Is there anything to do if it rains?

Yes, but not an enormous amount, and you should be honest with yourself about that before booking a week in bad weather. The spas are the main answer and a good one. Beyond them there is a small museum, the old town to walk, the Tallinn Gate, a handful of galleries and some very good eating. If a wet week is a real risk for your dates, the bog boardwalks at Söömaa are better in rain than in sun, and Tartu is a couple of hours away.
