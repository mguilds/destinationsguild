Poznan

Where to Stay in Poznan

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Poznan is where the Polish state began, a trade-fair city half-way between Warsaw and Berlin, and the most frequently overlooked of Poland’s big five. It has a painted old market square with a town hall where two mechanical goats butt heads at noon, a lake with a ski slope beside it on the eastern edge, and some of the cheapest hotel rooms in the country — except in the handful of weeks when the trade fair is on and there is not a bed to be had. Knowing which weeks those are is most of the art of booking here.

Where to stay in Poznan, area by area

Four parts of the city, and the one scheduling trap worth knowing about.

Stary Rynek — the Old Market Square

Most centralPrettiestWhere the eating is

Poznan’s old square is one of the largest in Poland, with a free-standing Renaissance town hall in the middle of it, painted merchant houses on all four sides and a row of little arcaded stalls along one edge. At noon a crowd gathers to watch two mechanical goats emerge above the town hall clock and butt their horns together twelve times, a performance that has been running since the sixteenth century and is sillier and better than it sounds. Staying on or near the square puts you among the restaurants and bars and within a short walk of the cathedral, the castle hill and the river. Rooms are mostly in small and mid-sized hotels in rebuilt townhouses — the square, like so much of Poland, was reconstructed after 1945 — and they are good value outside fair weeks.

Jeżyce — where Poznan eats, ten minutes out

RestaurantsLocalGood value

West of the centre beyond the station, Jeżyce is a district of handsome pre-war tenements that has become the city’s eating and drinking quarter: a covered market, a great many small restaurants and coffee places, and the kind of ordinary neighbourhood life that the old square no longer has much of. It is ten minutes on a tram or twenty-five on foot from the Stary Rynek, and the rooms and apartments are cheaper. Choose it for a stay of several nights, if you would rather eat where people live than where visitors eat, or simply if the square is full. It is plain to look at in parts and entirely comfortable.

Lake Malta and the eastern side

LakesideFamiliesTram to town

East of the river, Poznan has an artificial lake a couple of miles long with a regatta course down the middle, a path all the way round it, a thermal baths complex at one end and a dry ski slope and toboggan run on the bank above. The new zoo is next door. It is an odd and rather likeable arrangement, and the hotels out here are modern, well priced and popular with families. The tram into the centre takes about fifteen minutes. It suits a longer or a family stay and is the wrong choice for a single night built around the old town.

The station and the trade-fair quarter

TransportModern roomsFair-week prices

Poznan Główny station and the Międzynarodowe Targi Poznańskie, the international trade fair grounds, sit side by side immediately west of the old centre, and the quarter around them holds most of the city’s larger modern hotels. It is a flat fifteen-minute walk or a couple of tram stops to the Stary Rynek. For most of the year these are the best-value comfortable rooms in Poznan, with lifts, air conditioning and proper desks. During a major fair the same rooms treble in price and sell out months ahead, and that is the single most important thing to know about booking a hotel in this city: check the fair calendar before you fix your dates, not after.

Getting around

Poznan is compact and flat, and the old centre is a twenty-minute walk across. Trams are the backbone of everything beyond it, frequent and cheap, with buses filling the gaps and one time-based ticket covering both — buy from a machine at the stop or on board and validate as you get on.

The airport is about twenty-five minutes west of the centre by bus or taxi. Trains are where Poznan shines: it sits on the main line between Warsaw and Berlin, so Warsaw is about two and a half hours away, Berlin under three, and Wroclaw about two. For anyone putting together a trip across central Europe by rail, it is a natural and inexpensive stop.

Underfoot the old square and the streets around it are cobbled; the rest of the centre is ordinary pavement and easy going. The path around Lake Malta is flat, paved and about ten kilometres round, and makes the best walk or cycle in the city.

When to come, and what it costs

Poznan follows the same seasons as the rest of inland Poland — May, June and September the pick, July and August warm and quiet because the locals are away, November dark and cheap, December redeemed by a good Christmas market on the Stary Rynek. The city is never crowded with visitors in the way Krakow is.

The price pattern here, though, is unlike anywhere else in the country, and it is set by the trade fair rather than the weather. Outside fair weeks Poznan is among the cheapest cities in Poland for a comfortable room. During a major fair — and there are several a year, mostly in the first half — hotel rates across the whole city rise steeply and availability disappears. Look up the fair calendar before you choose your dates: moving a trip by a week can halve what you pay. Poland uses the Polish zloty (PLN, written zł), not the euro. Poland is a member of the European Union but has kept its own currency and has set no date for changing it, so euro notes are not legal tender and the few places that take them give a poor rate. Pay in zloty. Cards and phones are accepted almost everywhere, including on trams, buses and at ticket machines, and you need very little cash — though a small amount is handy for market stalls, church donation boxes and the odd rural bus. Prices in shops include tax. Tipping is modest and not chased: rounding up, or ten per cent for good table service, is plenty. Decline any card machine that offers to charge you in dollars; pay in zloty and let your own bank convert. Beware the standalone currency-exchange booths at the airports, which are much worse than the ones in town or simply drawing cash from a bank machine.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS — the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years — is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today — nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Poland is a straightforward case. It has been in the European Union since 2004 and in the Schengen area since 2007, so everything above applies here in full, and flying in from Berlin, Vienna or Amsterdam involves no passport control at all. What Poland has not joined is the euro, so the currency is the zloty. Note too that Poland’s eastern border — with Belarus and with the Russian enclave at Kaliningrad — is an external Schengen frontier and is not a casual crossing; the crossings into Ukraine are open but busy. None of this affects an ordinary trip to the cities in this guide.

Staying in Poznan — questions

Which area is best for a first visit to Poznan?

The Stary Rynek, the old market square, or the streets immediately around it. The centre is small and flat, so staying there puts the town hall and its goats, the cathedral, the castle hill and the restaurants all within a short walk. If the square is full or dear because of a trade fair, the station quarter is a fifteen-minute walk away with modern rooms, and Jezyce is ten minutes out by tram with better eating and lower prices.

What is the trade fair, and why does it matter?

Poznan has been central Europe's main trade-fair city for a century, and the fairgrounds sit beside the main station. Several large fairs a year fill every hotel in the city and push rates to two or three times their normal level, with availability gone months in advance. It is easily the biggest factor in what a Poznan trip costs. Check the fair calendar on the Poznan International Fair website before fixing your dates; shifting a visit by a few days can make a striking difference.

What are the goats on the town hall?

At noon each day two mechanical billy goats emerge from a hatch above the Renaissance town hall clock on the Stary Rynek and butt their horns together twelve times while a trumpeter plays. The story goes that a cook, having burnt the roast for a civic banquet, grabbed two goats from a nearby meadow as a replacement; they escaped, fought on the tower, and delighted the assembled dignitaries enough to be commemorated. It is the city's emblem, it draws a crowd every day, and it is thoroughly worth five minutes.

Is Poznan worth a stop between Warsaw and Berlin?

It is one of the easiest and best-judged stops on that route. Poznan sits on the main line, about two and a half hours from Warsaw and under three from Berlin, with frequent direct trains both ways, and the station is a fifteen-minute walk from the old square. One night gives you the square, the goats at noon and a good dinner; two lets you add the cathedral, the castle and Lake Malta. Rooms are cheap by the standards of either capital.

How long do you need in Poznan?

A day and a night covers the Stary Rynek, the goats, the cathedral on its island and the museum of Polish history in the castle. Two days is comfortable and lets you add Lake Malta, the thermal baths, Jezyce for an evening and the old fort or the zoo. It is not a city that needs a week, and it is a better stop on a longer Polish or central European trip than a destination on its own.

Is there much left of old Poznan?

Rather more than in Warsaw and rather less than in Krakow. The city was badly damaged in 1945, and the market square and much of the old centre were rebuilt afterwards in their earlier form. The cathedral on Ostrow Tumski, where the first Polish rulers were buried, is a reconstruction over genuinely ancient foundations, and the pre-war tenement districts such as Jezyce came through largely intact. What you see in the old square is a careful post-war recreation, as it is in so much of this country.
