Kaunas

Where to Stay in Kaunas

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Kaunas is Lithuania’s second city and stands where the Neris joins the Nemunas, a little over a hundred kilometres west of Vilnius. Its character comes from twenty particular years: from 1920 to 1939, with Vilnius held by Poland, Kaunas was the provisional capital, and it spent those two decades building a country’s worth of institutions in a clean, confident modernism. That quarter was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2023 as Modernist Kaunas: Architecture of Optimism. The city was a European Capital of Culture in 2022 and has not gone back to sleep since.

Where to stay in Kaunas, area by area

The parts of Kaunas a visitor actually chooses between, and who each one suits.

Senamiestis: the Old Town and the confluence

Most centralRiverside walksQuiet evenings

The oldest part of Kaunas fills the tip of the peninsula where the Neris runs into the Nemunas: the Town Hall Square with its white tower, the red-brick Gothic of the Vytautas church and the Perkunas House, the cathedral, and what is left of the castle at the very point. It is level, it is walkable end to end in fifteen minutes, and the two rivers meeting at the end of it is the best short walk in the city. Places to stay here are mostly small hotels and apartments in old buildings rather than anything with a lobby, and the evenings are quieter than in the New Town.

The New Town and Laisves aleja

Easiest first visitBest for the modernismLevel walking

Laisves aleja, the lime-shaded pedestrian avenue that runs for well over a kilometre from the Old Town to the blue-domed Church of St Michael the Archangel, is the spine of modern Kaunas and the easiest place to stay. Most of the larger hotels, the restaurants, the shops and the theatres are on it or a street away. This is also where the interwar architecture is: between 1920 and 1939, with Vilnius held by Poland, Kaunas was Lithuania’s provisional capital and built itself the ministries, banks, schools and apartment houses of a new state. That quarter, with Zaliakalnis above it, went onto the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2023.

Zaliakalnis: the Green Hill

QuietestFunicularResidential

The hill above the New Town, reached by one of the two surviving funiculars — small wooden cars from the nineteen-thirties that are themselves part of the UNESCO listing — or by a long flight of steps. Up top are quiet streets of interwar villas, the white bulk of the Christ’s Resurrection Basilica with a terrace on its roof and the whole city under it, and a good deal of garden. Accommodation is guesthouses and apartments rather than hotels. It suits a longer stay, or anybody who would rather look at the city than sleep in the middle of it.

The station and coach station end

Best valueTransport hubMixed streets

South-east of the New Town, around the railway station and the coach station, is the cheaper end of town: mid-range hotels, hostels and apartments, with the trains for Vilnius and the coaches for everywhere in one place. It is a plain quarter rather than an unpleasant one, and the walk up Vytauto prospektas into the New Town takes about fifteen minutes. Worth it if the budget or an early departure is driving the decision; not worth it otherwise, because the New Town is better and not much dearer.

Pazaislis and the reservoir

Furthest outBaroque monasteryBus or car

East of the city the Nemunas is dammed into a long reservoir, and on a wooded peninsula in it stands Pazaislis, a seventeenth-century monastery that is the finest Italian baroque in this part of Europe and hosts a music festival in summer. The lake shore has beaches, paths and a handful of places to stay. It is about nine kilometres from the Old Town, so it means a bus, a taxi or a car, and it is a choice for a quiet few days rather than for sightseeing in the city.

Getting around Kaunas

On foot

The Old Town and the New Town together form one flat, continuous walk of a little over two kilometres, and you can do the whole of central Kaunas without transport. The only climb is Zaliakalnis, and the funicular takes the work out of that.

The funiculars

Two of them survive from the nineteen-thirties, up Zaliakalnis and up Aleksotas on the far bank, and both still run as ordinary public transport. They are cheap, they take a couple of minutes, and the Aleksotas one lands you at the viewpoint with the best panorama of the Old Town and the confluence. Opening hours are shorter than a bus timetable, so check before relying on one late.

Trolleybuses and buses

A dense network covers the city and the suburbs on one ticket, bought from a kiosk, a machine or the city app and validated on board. Services are frequent on the main avenues and thinner out towards the reservoir.

Airports and trains

Kaunas has its own airport north-east of the city, used mainly by low-cost carriers, with a bus into town. Vilnius airport is about a hundred kilometres away and carries most of the long-haul routes; the train between the two cities takes a little over an hour and runs through the day, so flying into Vilnius and staying in Kaunas is perfectly practical.

Taxis and apps

Ride-hailing apps work throughout the city and are the simplest way to get a fair price. For street taxis use a marked rank and look for the fare card in the window.

When to come

Kaunas works all year and the season decides the kind of visit. Late May to early July is the prize: warm, long-lit, the avenue full and the river paths open. July and August are the warmest, and oddly among the quietest, because the city itself decamps to the coast. September is our pick — the students back, the festivals on, the light long and the weather still kind. October and November are grey and wet and the one stretch we would steer a first visit away from. December to February is cold, often properly snowy and the cheapest time of the year, and the view from the basilica terrace over a frozen city is worth a cold morning. Statehood Day on 6 July and midsummer on 23 and 24 June are the two dates that empty the offices and fill the hotels.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Lithuania is in the European Union, the Schengen area and the euro, so arriving from anywhere else in Schengen there is no check at all. Month-by-month weather and the dates worth planning around are in Best Time to Visit Lithuania, and the country guide is The Lithuania Travel Guide.

Kaunas — questions

Which part of Kaunas should I stay in?

The New Town, on or just off Laisves aleja, for almost everybody: it is level, central, has most of the hotels and puts the interwar architecture on your doorstep. The Old Town if you want the quieter, older, prettier version and do not mind smaller places. Zaliakalnis for a longer stay and a view. The station end only if the budget or an early train decides it.

Is Kaunas worth a night, or just a day trip from Vilnius?

Worth a night, and two if the architecture interests you. It is a little over an hour from Vilnius by train, so a day return is easy and a great many people do it — but it means seeing the Old Town and the avenue and missing the thing Kaunas is actually famous for, which is a whole quarter of nineteen-twenties and thirties buildings that takes a slow walk and ideally a guided one.

What is the UNESCO listing actually about?

Modernist Kaunas: Architecture of Optimism, 1919–1939, inscribed in 2023. Between the wars Kaunas was the provisional capital of Lithuania, because Vilnius was in Polish hands, and in twenty years it built itself the full apparatus of a capital city — ministries, banks, a university, schools, clinics, apartment blocks — in a restrained modernism of its own. The listing covers the New Town and Zaliakalnis, so the buildings are not in a museum: they are the street you walk down.

How long do I need in Kaunas?

One full day covers the Old Town, the confluence and Laisves aleja. Two lets you add the modernist walk, the Devil’s Museum or the Ciurlionis art museum, the Ninth Fort on the edge of town, and the funicular up to the basilica terrace. Three if you want Pazaislis and the reservoir as well, or if you are using Kaunas as a base for central Lithuania.

Is it walkable, and is it hilly?

The two central districts are flat and run into each other, so the walking is easy. Kaunas sits in a river valley, though, which means the edges of the centre go up sharply — Zaliakalnis on one side, Aleksotas across the Nemunas on the other. Both have a funicular, which is a pleasant way to avoid the stairs.

Is there anything on the rivers?

More than there used to be. There are riverside paths and cycle ways along both the Nemunas and the Neris, beaches on the reservoir east of town, and seasonal boat trips from the quay below the Old Town, including runs down the Nemunas. What runs and when changes from year to year, so ask locally rather than planning around a timetable you found online.

When should I come?

Late May to early July and then September are the best of it: warm, long-lit and green, with the city outdoors and the festivals running. July and August are the warmest and the quietest, because Kaunas itself goes to the coast. Winter is cold, often snowy and cheap, and the basilica terrace on a clear frozen day is worth the trip on its own.
