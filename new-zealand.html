New Zealand

New Zealand Travel Guide

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

New Zealand is two long islands and a scatter of smaller ones, holding a little over five million people and rather more sheep, set far enough from anywhere else that the flight is the first thing you have to make peace with. What you get for it is a country the size of Britain or Japan with the population of a mid-sized city, which is why the photographs look empty and are not cheating. The thing to understand before you book is the scale: the map is small and the roads are not fast, the two islands are genuinely different holidays, and almost every disappointed trip we hear about was one that tried to do both in ten days.

Why visit New Zealand

A small country that behaves like a continent, and an outdoors that is genuinely open to ordinary people.

The landscape changes every hour

Rainforest, glacier, fjord, desert plateau, vineyard and surf beach inside a single day’s drive is not marketing; it is simply what a long thin country sitting on a plate boundary looks like. The South Island in particular keeps producing a new country round each bend.

The outdoors is set up for you

The Department of Conservation maintains a network of marked tracks, huts and campsites across the whole country, well signposted and honestly graded. You do not need to be an expedition to get into real mountains for a day.

Maori culture is living, not exhibited

Te reo Maori is an official language, you will hear it daily, and the marae visits, kapa haka and guided walks on offer in places like Rotorua are run by the iwi whose land it is. It is the part of the trip visitors most often say they underestimated.

It is easy to travel in

English is spoken everywhere, the water is safe, the roads are good if slow, the food is better than its reputation, and there is nothing in the bush that wants to bite you. For a country this far away, remarkably little goes wrong.

New Zealand at a glance

Capital

Wellington, at the southern tip of the North Island. Auckland, further north, is much the largest city and the main international gateway.

Money

The New Zealand dollar (NZD, $). Cards and phones are taken almost everywhere. Prices on display include the 15 per cent GST.

Language

English, te reo Maori and New Zealand Sign Language are all official. English is universal in practice.

The two islands

North Island: cities, geothermal country, beaches, wine. South Island: Southern Alps, glaciers, fjords, lakes. Cook Strait between them.

Getting about

Hire car for the South Island, buses and two scenic trains for the North. Domestic flights are frequent and save whole days.

Seasons

Upside down. Summer is December to February, winter is June to August, and the clocks go forward in late September.

The regions

Five parts of the country, and a sensible first trip takes two or three of them.

Auckland & the north

The arrival point for most visitors, and a city built across two harbours and fifty volcanic cones. North of it the Bay of Islands and the Northland coast are the country’s warmest corner and the place where its written history starts.

The central North Island

The capital: compact, hilly, windy, and the best small-city eating and museum-going in the country. The Cook Strait ferries leave from here, and the Wairarapa wine country is an hour over the hill.

Things to do in New Zealand

Milford Sound

A fjord walled a thousand metres high, reached by a spectacular road from Te Anau or a long day trip from Queenstown. Go by boat, and do not be put off by rain — wet is when the waterfalls run.

The TranzAlpine

Christchurch to Greymouth across Arthur’s Pass in about five hours, through gorges and beech forest to the West Coast. One of the great train rides, and no walking required.

Geothermal Rotorua

Geysers, coloured lakes and boiling mud at Whakarewarewa, Wai-O-Tapu and Orakei Korako, and the Maori cultural evenings that go with them.

A Great Walk, or a day of one

The Routeburn, Abel Tasman, Milford and Tongariro tracks are the famous ones. Huts must be booked, often months ahead, but every one of them has a day section you can walk for nothing.

Aoraki and the dark sky

Mount Cook, the highest peak at 3,724 metres, sits in the Aoraki Mackenzie International Dark Sky Reserve. The Hooker Valley track and a clear night are a day and an evening well spent.

Kaikoura and the whales

A deep trench close inshore brings sperm whales, dusky dolphins and albatross within a short boat ride, year round, from a small town two and a half hours north of Christchurch.

Best time to visit & getting there

Start by turning the calendar over. Summer runs from December to February: this is peak season, with the warmest sea, the longest days, every track and cruise running and prices to match. It also coincides with the New Zealand school holidays from late December into January, when the whole country is on the road and the coastal towns are full of New Zealanders. Book early or avoid that fortnight.

The shoulders are our own choice. March and April bring settled weather, sea still warm from summer, autumn colour through Central Otago and Wanaka, and prices and crowds both stepping down. October and November give you the Alps still white, rivers and waterfalls at full volume, lambs in the paddocks and the gardens out, with the trade-off of more changeable weather and a few alpine tracks not yet open.

Winter, June to August, is not a closed season. It is ski season at Queenstown, Wanaka and Mount Ruapehu, and everywhere else it is quiet, inexpensive and often brilliantly clear, with snow on the mountains and nobody on the lakefront. Days are short, some high passes and tracks close, and Milford Road can need chains. Our month-by-month guide to the best time to visit New Zealand goes through it properly.

Getting there is the long part. Auckland takes the great majority of long-haul flights, with Christchurch and Queenstown taking Australian and some regional services. From North America it is a single long night-time hop to Auckland from the west coast, or a connection through Sydney, Melbourne, Fiji or an Asian hub; from Europe it is two long legs whichever way round you go. One tip that saves a day: fly into Auckland and out of Christchurch or Queenstown rather than doubling back, which airlines will usually price as an open-jaw without penalty.

Fishing here

New Zealand is, by a wide margin, one of the best trout countries on earth, and it is unusual in that the fish are wild, big and visible. Brown and rainbow trout were introduced in the nineteenth century, found the cold clear rivers of both islands entirely to their liking, and are now stalked by sight in water so clear that the fishing is closer to hunting. The Tongariro and the lakes of the central North Island are the famous rainbow water; the back-country rivers of Southland, Otago and the West Coast hold the brown trout that bring anglers across the world, usually with a guide, because finding and not spooking them is most of the skill. A licence is required everywhere and is sold online and in tackle shops; the Taupo district runs its own separate licence. Our full guide is fly fishing in New Zealand, and the whole shelf is in The Guild Fishing Library.

Travelling in your later years

An easy country to travel slowly in, and one where the flight, not the destination, is the thing to plan around.

The flight is the hard part

Nothing about New Zealand is as demanding as getting there. Break the journey with a night in Los Angeles, Singapore, Sydney or Fiji if you can, give yourself two easy days in Auckland on arrival, and do not book anything ambitious for the first morning.

The best views need no walking

The Milford boat, the TranzAlpine, the Doubtful Sound cruise, the Skyline gondolas at Queenstown and Rotorua and the Kaikoura whale boats all deliver the famous sights from a seat.

Short drives, not long ones

Two to three hours of winding two-lane road is a comfortable day here. Driving Auckland to Wellington or Christchurch to Queenstown in one go is legal, common and a mistake.

Accessibility is generally good

Newer hotels, the main museums, the scenic trains and most cruise boats are step-free, and mobility parking is widely provided. Older guesthouses and hill-suburb streets in Wellington and Dunedin are the exceptions.

Health care is good and far apart

Standards are high and English is universal, but a serious case in the back country may be a long way from a hospital. Bring prescriptions in their original packaging with a copy, and take travel insurance seriously.

Sun, not heat, is the risk

The ultraviolet here is among the strongest in the world, and it burns on cool, cloudy days. Hat, sleeves and sunscreen are not fussiness; they are what every local does.

Getting in: what you need, and what you pay

Most visitors from visa-waiver countries need a New Zealand Electronic Travel Authority, the NZeTA, requested before you fly. It is not a visa; it is permission to board, and airlines check it. It is requested through Immigration New Zealand’s own app or website, it covers multiple visits over a period of years, and it is usually granted quickly — but not instantly, so do it when you book rather than at the airport.

Alongside it, most visitors pay the International Visitor Conservation and Tourism Levy, which funds conservation and tourism infrastructure and is normally collected at the same time as the NZeTA. There is also a New Zealand Traveller Declaration to complete shortly before you arrive, which replaces the old paper arrival card and covers your customs and biosecurity answers.

Both the NZeTA fee and the levy have been changed more than once in the last few years, so we deliberately do not print the figures here — a number that is out of date is worse than no number. Read them off the official page at immigration.govt.nz at the moment you book. Request the NZeTA yourself from the official source; the sites that offer to do it for you charge a mark-up for filling in the same form.

Two exceptions worth knowing. Australian citizens do not need an NZeTA and are not charged the levy; Australian permanent residents who are not citizens do need one. New Zealand citizens travel on their own passport and need neither. Visa-waiver visitors are generally admitted for up to three months, with British passport holders allowed a longer stay, and in every case the officer at the border wants to see an onward ticket and enough money for the trip.

Finally, the biosecurity check on arrival is the real thing, not a formality. Food, plants, animal products, and any outdoor gear that has touched soil or fresh water must be declared. Clean your boots, tent and fishing tackle before you pack them. Declaring something costs you nothing; not declaring it can cost you an instant fine.

New Zealand travel FAQ

When is the best time to visit New Zealand?

Remember that the seasons are upside down. Summer is December to February: long days, warm coasts, every walking track and boat trip running, and the busiest and dearest time of year, with the Christmas and January school holidays filling the country with New Zealanders on their own holiday. Our own pick is the shoulders. March and April give you settled weather, warm sea, autumn colour in Central Otago and noticeably calmer prices; October and November give you snow still on the Southern Alps, waterfalls at full volume and lambs in every paddock. Winter, June to August, is ski season around Queenstown and Mount Ruapehu and a quiet, cheap and often clear time everywhere else, with the trade-off of short days and some alpine roads and tracks closed.

How many days do you need?

Two weeks is the honest minimum for both islands, and three is better. The commonest mistake we see is a ten-day trip with eleven stops on it. New Zealand looks small on a map and does not behave small: the roads are two-lane, winding and slow, and a drive the map calls four hours is five with a stop for a view. If you have a week, take one island. If you have ten days, take the South Island and accept that you are not seeing the North.

Do I need a car?

For the South Island, as good as yes. The landscape is the point and the pleasure is stopping for it, and the bus network, though real and inexpensive, does not take you to the trailheads and the lakeshores. For the North Island you can manage without: Auckland and Wellington are walkable and well served by buses and trains, the Northern Explorer train runs between them, and Rotorua and Taupo are easy coach trips. Remember that you drive on the left, that one-lane bridges are normal and signposted, and that a hire car cannot be taken across Cook Strait on the ferry under most agreements - you drop one car at the port and collect another on the far side.

North Island or South Island?

If you want mountains, glaciers, fjords, big empty lakes and the long alpine drives, the South Island is what you are picturing. If you want the Maori cultural heart of the country, geothermal country, warm swimming beaches, the wine districts and the two biggest cities, that is the North. Most first trips give the South Island rather more time and base the North Island half on Auckland, Rotorua and Wellington. Both islands have great walks, good food and far more coastline than you will get to.

What do I need to enter New Zealand?

Most visa-waiver visitors need a New Zealand Electronic Travel Authority, the NZeTA, requested before you fly, and they also pay the International Visitor Conservation and Tourism Levy. There is a separate New Zealand Traveller Declaration to complete shortly before you arrive. Australian citizens do not need an NZeTA; Australian permanent residents do. Both the NZeTA fee and the levy have been changed more than once in recent years, so we do not quote the figures here - read them off the official page at immigration.govt.nz when you book, and request the NZeTA yourself through the official app or website rather than through any agent offering to do it for you.

Is New Zealand expensive?

It is dearer than most visitors expect and it has got dearer. Accommodation, car hire and eating out are the three that catch people, and in Queenstown in high summer all three at once. Displayed prices include the 15 per cent goods and services tax, so the number on the menu is the number you pay, and tipping is not customary or expected. Where the country is generous is the outdoors: the national parks, the day walks, the lookouts and most of the beaches cost nothing at all, and that is the bulk of why you came.

Is there anything dangerous in the bush?

Almost nothing with teeth. New Zealand has no snakes and no dangerous wild mammals, and the only venomous resident, the katipo spider, is rare and shy. The real hazards are the weather and the water. Alpine weather changes within the hour in every season, rivers rise fast and drown trampers who try to cross them, and the surf on the west coasts has strong rips. Sandflies in Fiordland and on the West Coast are a genuine misery rather than a danger - take repellent. Carry warm and waterproof layers on any day walk in the hills, however fine the morning looks, and leave your intentions with someone.

What happens at the biosecurity check?

You will be asked about food, plants, animal products and outdoor equipment, and the questions are not a formality. New Zealand's farming and native species depend on keeping pests out, the inspectors are thorough, the dogs are good at their job and the fines for an undeclared apple are real. Declare everything, including sealed snacks you forgot about. If you are bringing hiking boots, a tent, fishing tackle or a bicycle, clean the soil off them before you pack - gear that arrives dirty is taken away to be cleaned, which costs you time at the airport and sometimes a fee.
