Where to Stay in Zlatibor

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Zlatibor is a mountain plateau in south-western Serbia, about a thousand metres up, and it has been the country's favourite mountain resort for a century and a half — since a Serbian king came to drink the water and the central settlement took the name Kraljeve Vode, the king's waters. It is not an alpine landscape of peaks and precipices: it is high rolling pasture and pine, with long views, famously clean air and a gentleness that is the whole appeal. People come to walk in summer, ski modestly at Tornik in winter, and eat a great deal of smoked ham and kajmak. The building boom of recent years has filled the centre with apartment towers, which is the honest caveat; the hillsides around it are still what people came for. Mokra Gora, the Sargan Eight railway, Drvengrad, Tara and the Uvac canyon are all within an hour or two.

Kraljeve Vode: the centre and the lake

Most centralEverything on the doorstepBusiest

The resort proper, built around the small lake and the park, with the promenade, the restaurants, the market stalls selling ham and cheese, the gondola station and the great majority of the beds. Staying here means walking to everything and not needing a car, and it is the right choice for a first visit or a short one. It is also where the new apartment blocks have gone up, so it is dense, it is busy in high season and at New Year, and a view is worth asking about rather than assuming.

The hillsides above the centre

ViewsQuieterPine and pasture

The slopes and side roads ringing Kraljeve Vode, where the older villas and the newer small hotels and guesthouses sit among pines with the plateau spread out below. Ten to thirty minutes' walk from the promenade, or a few minutes by car, and a different holiday: quiet, cooler, with paths from the door. This is where to stay if you came for the air and the walking rather than the resort. Most of it wants a car, or a willingness to walk up.

Tornik and the ski end

SkiingHighestSeasonal

Tornik is the mountain above the resort, the highest point of Zlatibor and where the ski runs and the lifts are, linked to the centre by the long gondola. There are hotels and apartments up at this end. In winter it is the obvious base if skiing is the point of the trip; in summer the gondola and the chairlift make it a walking and view base instead. Check what is actually running for your dates, because lift operation and the gondola's schedule change by season.

Cajetina

CheapestA real townOff the resort

The municipal town a few kilometres down from the plateau, which is where the shops, the bank, the clinic and the people who work in Zlatibor are. Rooms here are the cheapest in the area and it is an ordinary Serbian town rather than a resort, which some visitors much prefer. Buses and taxis run up to Kraljeve Vode in minutes.

Mokra Gora and the Sargan Eight

RailwayCharacterNeed a car

Half an hour west towards the Bosnian border, the village at the foot of the Sargan Eight narrow-gauge railway, with guesthouses, the Drvengrad wooden village on the hill above and the Tara national park beyond. It is a quieter, more rural stay than Zlatibor and the obvious base if the railway and Tara are what you came for. A car makes it work; without one you are tied to the train's own schedule.

Getting around Zlatibor

On foot

The centre around the lake and the promenade is level and easy, and the plateau's marked paths are gentle walking by mountain standards — rolling pasture and forest track rather than scrambling, which is exactly why Zlatibor suits people who want mountain air without mountain effort. Away from the centre there are no pavements, so walking along the roads wants care.

The gondola

The Gold Gondola runs from the centre of Kraljeve Vode up towards Tornik and is both transport and the main attraction, with a long view over the plateau. Running days, hours and the sections in operation change by season and with the weather, so check on the day rather than taking a timetable from any website, including this one.

Cars, buses and taxis

A car is the sensible way to do this corner of Serbia: the Sargan Eight, Drvengrad, Tara, the Uvac viewpoints and Mokra Gora are all day trips and public transport between them is thin. Buses connect Zlatibor with Cajetina and Uzice and run to Belgrade; taxis are cheap for short hops. Mountain roads here are decent but narrow and can be snowy from December.

Getting here

Zlatibor is about four hours from Belgrade by road, and coaches run from the main bus station; Uzice, twenty minutes away, is the nearest town with rail. There is no useful airport nearby — Belgrade is the arrival point. The drive down through the hills is a good one, and the last stretch climbs onto the plateau.

The Sargan Eight

The narrow-gauge heritage line at Mokra Gora climbs about three hundred metres by looping back over itself in a figure of eight, through tunnels and over viaducts, and takes roughly two and a half hours there and back with stops. It runs on a seasonal timetable with several departures on operating days. Book ahead in summer, and check the year's running days with the operator.

When to come

Zlatibor has two seasons and they are both busy. June to September is the walking and air season and the best of the year: warm days at altitude, cool nights, everything open, and the plateau at its greenest in June. August is the fullest month and Serbian families fill it. Late December to March is the ski season at Tornik, with New Year the single busiest and dearest week of the year; snow cover varies from winter to winter, so check conditions rather than assuming. May and October are the quiet, cheap and rather lovely shoulders — October especially, with the forests turning and the air sharp. April and November are the in-between months when some places close and the weather can be anything. At a thousand metres it is always several degrees cooler than Belgrade, which is the point in July.

Getting in: what applies, and what does not

Serbia is not in the European Union and not in the Schengen area, so it runs its own border. British passport holders may visit without a visa for up to 90 days in any 6-month period, with a passport valid for at least 90 days beyond the day they leave — that is what the UK government’s Serbia travel advice said when we read it on 26 November 2026. Many other nationalities including the United States are admitted visa-free for comparable periods; we could not open the American source on the day we wrote this, so check your own. Two Serbian rules catch people out: make sure your passport is actually stamped on entry, because leaving without an entry stamp can mean charges of illegal immigration, a heavy fine and a prison sentence; and you must register with the local police within 24 hours, which a hotel or registered apartment does for you automatically. If Kosovo is also in your plans, read the Kosovo paragraph in The Serbia Travel Guide before you book anything — entering Serbia from Kosovo without an existing Serbian entry stamp usually means being refused.

If your trip also crosses into Hungary, Croatia, Bulgaria, Greece or anywhere else in the Schengen area — and most flights from North America reach Serbia through a Schengen hub — the following applies to that crossing and not to Serbia:

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Zlatibor — questions

Is Zlatibor worth it in summer?

That is its best season. The plateau is high enough to be several degrees cooler than the cities in July and August, the air has been the selling point since the nineteenth century, and the walking is gentle rolling pasture and pine rather than anything strenuous. Add the gondola, the Sargan Eight railway, Drvengrad, Tara and the Uvac canyon within an hour or two, and it fills a week easily.

How good is the skiing?

Modest, and honestly so. Tornik has a small set of runs and lifts above the resort, which suits beginners, families and anyone who wants a few days rather than a serious ski holiday; Kopaonik, further south, is the bigger Serbian ski resort. Snow cover varies considerably from winter to winter, so check the conditions and what is actually open before booking a ski trip here.

Where should I stay, the centre or outside it?

The centre at Kraljeve Vode if you want to walk to restaurants and the gondola and not need a car — accepting that recent building has made it dense and busy. The hillsides above if you came for quiet, views and paths from the door, which is what most returning visitors choose. Cajetina for the cheapest rooms and an ordinary town. Mokra Gora if the railway and Tara are the real reason for the trip.

What is the Sargan Eight?

A narrow-gauge heritage railway at Mokra Gora, half an hour from Zlatibor, rebuilt from the old Belgrade–Sarajevo line. To gain about three hundred metres it loops back over itself in the shape of a figure eight, through tunnels and across viaducts. The round trip takes roughly two and a half hours with photograph stops. It runs seasonally, so check the operating days, and book ahead in summer.

Do I need a car?

Not for Zlatibor itself — the centre is walkable and coaches reach it from Belgrade. You do want one for the area, because the Sargan Eight, Drvengrad, Tara national park and the Uvac canyon viewpoints are scattered and public transport between them is thin. A driver for a day is an alternative and not expensive.

What is there to eat?

This is smoked ham country. Zlatibor prsuta, kajmak, fresh and matured cheeses, lamb and veal cooked under the sac, cornbread, and the market stalls in the centre selling all of it to take home. Portions are large and it is cheap. The region's rakija is usually plum, and the mountain honey is worth a jar.
