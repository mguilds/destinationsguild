Romania

The Romania Travel Guide

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Romania is the big one in this corner of Europe, and the one that surprises people most. It is about the size of the United Kingdom, a third of it mountain, with the Carpathians curling through the middle like a question mark and enclosing the high plateau of Transylvania — where walled Saxon towns founded in the twelfth century still stand around their squares, and village churches were built with walls and towers so a community could shut itself in. Beyond them: Bucharest on its southern plain, grand and battered and far better to eat in than its reputation; the Habsburg west around Timisoara, built in brick and baroque plaster; painted monastery churches in the north-east that have held their colour for five hundred years; wooden churches and hand-made haystacks in Maramures; and in the south-east the Danube finishing in the largest river delta in Europe. It joined the European Union in 2007 and the Schengen area on 1 January 2025, it is not in the euro, and it remains one of the best-value countries on the continent. The roads are slower than the map suggests, which is the one thing to plan around.

Why visit Romania

Transylvania is real, and better than the stories

The name has been doing duty as a horror setting for so long that the actual place comes as a surprise: a high plateau ringed by the Carpathians, with walled Saxon towns founded in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, pastel squares, hilltop citadels and villages where the church is still fortified against raids that stopped four hundred years ago. Brasov, Sibiu and Sighisoara are the set pieces, and they are genuinely beautiful rather than merely atmospheric.

Castles that are actually castles

Peles at Sinaia is a late-nineteenth-century royal residence of a quality that would be famous anywhere, with its interiors intact. Corvin Castle at Hunedoara is the real Gothic article, towers and drawbridge and all. Bran is a handsome fourteenth-century border fort whose Dracula connection is a marketing invention, and we would rather tell you that than sell you the story. Between them they make a better castle week than most of Europe can offer.

Painted monasteries and wooden churches

In Bucovina, in the north-east, a group of sixteenth-century monastery churches are painted inside and out with frescoes that have survived five centuries of weather — Voronet's blue is the famous one — and they are on the UNESCO World Heritage list. Over in Maramures, a separate UNESCO listing covers wooden churches with tall shingled spires built without a nail. Neither is on the usual tourist circuit and both are worth the drive.

The Carpathians, and two of Europe's great mountain roads

The mountains take up about a third of the country and rise past two and a half thousand metres in the Fagaras range, with bears, long forests and real walking. Two roads cross them for the sake of it: the Transfagarasan over the Fagaras past Balea Lake, and the Transalpina higher still to the west. Both are summer-only at the top, both are among the finest drives on the continent, and both need checking before you set out.

The Danube Delta

Where the Danube finishes, it spreads into the largest and best-preserved river delta in Europe — a UNESCO World Heritage site and biosphere reserve of reedbeds, channels and floating islands, with pelicans, egrets and several hundred bird species. You see it from a small boat out of Tulcea or one of the villages, and it is unlike anywhere else in the European Union.

It is in the EU and in Schengen, and it is still inexpensive

Romania joined the European Union in 2007 and became a full member of the Schengen area on 1 January 2025, so arriving from elsewhere in Europe is now an internal journey. It is not in the euro area, and it remains one of the better-value countries on the continent for eating, sleeping and travelling — markedly cheaper than Austria or Italy for a comparable room, and with food worth the trip on its own.

Romania at a glance

Capital

Bucharest, out on the Wallachian plain in the south, with something like two million people in the city and its suburbs. Henri Coanda International at Otopeni, sixteen kilometres north of the centre, carries nearly all of the country's long-haul and international traffic, with a rail link and an express bus into town. Cluj-Napoca and the other regional airports handle a wide spread of European routes.

Money

The Romanian leu, written RON or lei in the plural and divided into bani. Romania is in the European Union but not in the euro area, and adopting the euro remains an intention without a firm date, so do not arrive with euro notes expecting to spend them. Cards are taken almost everywhere, contactless is normal and city transport mostly accepts it; keep a little cash for markets, village churches and rural guesthouses.

Language

Romanian, which is a Romance language — the eastern survivor of Latin — so anyone with French, Italian or Spanish will read signs and menus better than they expect, even though the spoken language sounds unfamiliar. Hungarian is spoken by a substantial minority in Transylvania, German by the remaining Saxon community, and English by most people under forty and nearly everyone in tourism.

Borders

In NATO since 2004, in the European Union since 2007, and a full member of the Schengen area since 1 January 2025 — air and sea borders opened in March 2024 and the land borders followed. Neighbours are Ukraine and Moldova to the north and east, Bulgaria across the Danube to the south, and Serbia and Hungary to the west. Older guidebooks still describe border checks that no longer happen.

Getting about

Driving is on the right. The motorway network is patchy and still being built, so cross-country road journeys take longer than the distance suggests, and the mountain passes are slow by design. Trains reach most places and are cheap, comfortable enough and unhurried; the Bucharest–Brasov line through the Prahova valley is the one a visitor will actually enjoy. For Cluj or Timisoara from the capital, almost everyone flies.

Shape of it

About two hundred and thirty-eight thousand square kilometres — close to the size of the United Kingdom — with a population of roughly nineteen million that has been falling for years through emigration and a low birth rate. Around a third of the country is mountain, the Carpathians curling through the middle like a question mark and rising to Moldoveanu at 2,544 metres; the rest is plain, hill and the Danube. A semi-presidential republic, and a member of the EU's single market.

The regions

Six parts of the country that feel genuinely different from one another — the southern plain and the capital, Transylvania inside the mountains, the Habsburg west, the monastery country of the north-east, the wooden-church north, and the coast with the delta.

Bucharest, Wallachia and the Danube plain

The south: the capital out on its plain, the royal summer resorts where the Prahova valley climbs into the mountains, and the Danube running along the Bulgarian border to the Iron Gates.

  • BucharestGuild guide
  • Sinaia — the royal resort in the Prahova valley, and the base for Peles Castle and the Bucegi mountains an hour and a half from Bucharest by train
  • Peles Castle — a late-nineteenth-century royal residence above Sinaia with its interiors intact, and the finest building of its kind in the country
  • Horezu — a late-seventeenth-century monastery in the Wallachian foothills, a UNESCO World Heritage site and the best example of the Brancovenesc style
  • The Iron Gates — the gorge where the Danube cuts between the Carpathians and the Balkans on the Serbian border, with the rock carving of Decebalus above the water

Transylvania

The high plateau inside the curve of the Carpathians, and the reason most people come: walled Saxon towns, hilltop citadels, fortified village churches and real castles.

  • BrasovGuild guide
  • SibiuGuild guide
  • Cluj-NapocaGuild guide
  • Sighisoara — the best-preserved inhabited medieval citadel in the region, a UNESCO World Heritage site, reached up a covered wooden stairway
  • Alba Iulia — the great star-shaped Habsburg citadel, restored, with the cathedral where the kings of Romania were crowned in 1922
  • Bran and Rasnov — the border fortress marketed as Dracula's castle - handsome, genuine and nothing to do with Vlad Tepes - and the hilltop citadel on the same road
  • The fortified churches — Viscri, Biertan, Prejmer and a dozen more: village churches walled and towered so a community could shut itself in, a UNESCO site collectively
  • Corvin Castle — at Hunedoara, west of Sibiu - a fifteenth-century Gothic castle with towers and a drawbridge, and the real article after Bran

The Banat and the west

The flat Habsburg west, nearer Belgrade and Budapest than Bucharest, built in brick and baroque plaster, with the Apuseni mountains behind it.

  • TimisoaraGuild guide
  • Oradea — a town of exceptional Art Nouveau architecture on the Hungarian border, restored over the last decade and much the most surprising city in Romania
  • The Apuseni mountains — limestone country of caves, gorges and scattered villages between Oradea and Cluj, with the Scarisoara ice cave among them
  • Turda — the worked-out salt mine forty minutes from Cluj, a hundred metres down, with a boating lake and a Ferris wheel improbably installed in the bottom of it

Moldavia and Bucovina

The north-east: the old Moldavian capitals, and the painted monastery churches that are the country's finest single sight.

  • Iasi — the cultural capital of the east, a university city of churches, the Palace of Culture and the Union of 1859
  • Suceava — the old Moldavian seat and the practical base for the painted monasteries, with its own princely fortress above the town
  • The painted monasteries — Voronet, Sucevita, Moldovita, Humor and Arbore - sixteenth-century churches frescoed outside as well as in, a UNESCO World Heritage site
  • Neamt and Agapia — working monasteries in wooded hills, quieter than Bucovina and a long tradition of icon painting

Maramures and the north

The most traditional corner of the country, over the mountains and a long way from anywhere: wooden churches, carved gates and haymaking still done by hand.

  • Baia Mare — the regional town and the usual base, an old mining centre at the edge of the wooden-church country
  • The wooden churches — tall shingled spires built without a nail at Barsana, Ieud, Surdesti, Poienile Izei and others - a separate UNESCO listing from the Transylvanian churches
  • The Merry Cemetery at Sapanta — every grave marker carved, painted and inscribed with a rhyming epitaph about the life it marks, a good many of them cheerfully rude
  • The Mocanita — a narrow-gauge steam forestry railway up the Vaser valley from Viseu de Sus, still working, and one of the last of its kind in Europe

The Black Sea and the Danube Delta

The south-east corner: a short, hot beach season on the coast, Greek and Roman ruins along it, and the delta behind Tulcea, which is like nothing else in Europe.

  • Constanta — the old Black Sea port, Greek Tomis in its first life, with a good archaeology museum and the Roman mosaic floor
  • Mamaia — the resort strip of sand just north of Constanta, which is where Romania goes to the beach in July and August
  • Tulcea — the working port at the head of the Danube Delta and where the boats go from
  • The Danube Delta — the largest and best-preserved river delta in Europe, a UNESCO World Heritage site and biosphere reserve of reeds, channels and several hundred bird species
  • Histria and Vama Veche — the oldest Greek settlement on these shores, out by the lagoons, and the scruffy, much-loved village beach down at the Bulgarian border

Things to do in Romania

Go inside the Palace of the Parliament

Ceausescu levelled a square mile of old Bucharest to build it and did not live to see it finished. It is one of the largest and heaviest administrative buildings on earth, and the scale of the marble staircases and the empty halls says more about the regime than any museum caption. It is visited on a booked guided tour and you will need identification; book ahead, because walk-up places are limited.

Drive the Transfagarasan, if it is open

The DN7C climbs the Fagaras range in a long sequence of hairpins to a tunnel at over two thousand metres, with Balea Lake at the top and the Vidraru dam on the southern approach. It is a summer road: the high section is shut by snow for much of the year and usually opens somewhere around late June or July, closing again in October, with the dates set each year by conditions. Check the current status before you commit a day to it, and have the Transalpina or a valley route as a fallback.

See the painted monasteries of Bucovina

Voronet, Sucevita, Moldovita, Humor and Arbore are sixteenth-century monastery churches with their outside walls covered in frescoes — Last Judgements, saints, sieges — that have held their colour through five hundred Carpathian winters. They are a UNESCO World Heritage site and they are still working monasteries. Suceava is the base; a car or a hired driver makes a long, memorable day of three or four of them.

Take a boat into the Danube Delta

From Tulcea, or better from one of the villages inside the delta, a small boat takes you off the main channels into the reeds, which is where the birds are. Pelicans are the headline and there are several hundred other species. Go early or late in the day, go in a small boat rather than a large one, and give it a full day at least — the delta rewards slowness and punishes a dash.

Walk the Saxon towns and their fortified churches

Sibiu's three linked squares, Brasov's Council Square under the mountain and the citadel hill at Sighisoara are the towns; out in the villages between them, churches like Viscri, Biertan and Prejmer were built with walls, towers and storerooms so that a whole community could shut itself in. The villages with fortified churches are a UNESCO site collectively, and they are the part of Transylvania most visitors miss.

Go north to Maramures

The far north is the most traditional corner of the country: wooden churches with tall shingled spires on the UNESCO list, carved gates, haymaking done by hand, the narrow-gauge Mocanita steam train up the Vaser valley, and the Merry Cemetery at Sapanta where every grave marker carries a painted portrait and a rhyming and frequently rude epitaph. It is a long way from anywhere and it is the trip people talk about afterwards.

When to go, and what it costs

Romania keeps three calendars at once: a hot southern plain, a mountain interior and a short Black Sea summer. May, June and September are the best weeks almost everywhere — warm but not punishing, the Transylvanian squares full of tables, the villages green and the walking open. July and August are hot, well above thirty degrees on the Bucharest and Timisoara plains, and they are the only time the Black Sea coast really works; they are also the busiest weeks in Brasov, at Bran and on the mountain roads. October is lovely, with the Carpathians turning and the crowds gone, though the high passes begin to close. December to March is ski season at Poiana Brasov and Sinaia, cold and often snowy, the cheapest time for the cities and the best time to see Sibiu and Brasov under snow; the Christmas markets in Sibiu, Brasov, Bucharest and Timisoara run through December. The high sections of the Transfagarasan and the Transalpina are summer-only, usually open from around late June or July until October with the dates set each year by conditions — check before you plan a day around either. The dates that fill hotels are 1 December, the national day, and Orthodox Easter, which moves each year and is the biggest family holiday in the country.

Fishing here

Fishing in Romania means three quite different things. The Danube Delta is the big one — pike, perch, zander, carp and catfish among the reeds and channels, fished from a small boat with a local guide, and inside a biosphere reserve where permits come from the reserve authority rather than over a counter. The Carpathian rivers hold brown trout and grayling in fast mountain water, with a short, strictly dated season and stretches managed by local angling associations. The Danube itself is big-river fishing, and one thing to know plainly: sturgeon are protected and sturgeon fishing in the Romanian Danube has been under moratorium for years, so anyone offering it is offering something they should not. Licences, seasons and the delta permits are all arranged locally and all change, so this is a trip to set up with a guide on the ground rather than to work out in advance from abroad. Travelling in your later years

The flat cities are flat, and the hill towns are not

Bucharest, Timisoara and Sibiu's upper town are all genuinely level walking, and Sibiu in particular is the easiest beautiful town in the country to get around. Brasov's old centre is flat but the Schei and Tampa streets climb, and Sighisoara's citadel is reached up a covered wooden stairway of a couple of hundred steps. Choose the town to match the legs, and Sibiu first if the choice is open.

Cobbles, and what they cost you

Every historic centre worth seeing here is cobbled, and the cobbles are the original uneven sort rather than smoothed setts. They are hard on an unsteady ankle, worse in the wet and awkward for a wheeled case. Flat shoes with a real sole make more difference than anything else, and a taxi to the door of a hotel inside the old town is worth arranging in advance, because some lanes are closed to cars.

Think hard about the driving

Romanian motorways are incomplete, two-lane national roads carry heavy lorry traffic with impatient overtaking, and the mountain roads are long sequences of hairpins at altitude. None of it is beyond an experienced driver, but it is tiring, and journeys take half again as long as the map suggests. Trains between Bucharest, Brasov and Sibiu are a comfortable alternative, and a hired driver for a day in the countryside costs far less than it would in Western Europe.

Where the ground is difficult

Peles Castle is a tour on stairs with no lift; Corvin Castle has uneven ramps and towers; the painted monasteries involve standing on gravel and stone for long stretches; the Danube Delta means stepping in and out of a small boat from a low jetty; and the Mocanita and the Maramures villages are rough underfoot. The Palace of the Parliament tour, by contrast, is long but level and partly lift-served. Pick deliberately rather than ruling the country out.

Getting in: what applies, and what does not

Romania is a member of the European Union, which it joined in 2007, and since 1 January 2025 it has been a full member of the Schengen area — air and sea borders opened in March 2024 and the land borders followed on New Year’s Day 2025. That is a genuine change from the advice in older guidebooks: arriving from another Schengen country is now an internal journey with no routine passport check, and the rules below are the ones that apply when you first enter the area.

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen external border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave. United States, Canadian, British and Australian citizens may visit as tourists without a visa for up to ninety days in any one hundred and eighty, counted across the whole Schengen area rather than against Romania alone — so a longer European trip uses the same allowance.

One thing worth knowing: Romania is in the European Union but not in the euro area. The currency is the Romanian leu, and adopting the euro remains a stated intention with no firm date.

Getting here: Bucharest has nearly all of the long-haul and international traffic, and Cluj-Napoca, Timisoara, Iasi and Sibiu all have a useful spread of European routes — flying into Cluj or Sibiu can save a long haul across the country. Trains reach Romania from Hungary, Austria and Bulgaria, and the western border at Timisoara and Oradea is the natural way in by road from Budapest or Belgrade. Flights, trains and coach timetables all change by season, so confirm them with the operator for your own dates rather than trusting a figure on any website, including this one — which is why we do not print one.

Romania — questions

How many days do I need in Romania?

A week is the sensible first trip and gets you Bucharest for two nights and Transylvania for four or five — Brasov with Bran and Peles, then Sibiu and Sighisoara. Ten days lets you add either the Danube Delta or the painted monasteries of Bucovina without rushing. A fortnight gets you Maramures as well, which is the one most people wish they had allowed for. The country is larger than it looks and the roads are slow, so plan fewer places than you think.

Is Romania expensive?

No, by Western European standards it remains good value, and food and drink in particular are inexpensive for the quality. Bucharest and the Black Sea coast in high summer are the priciest, Brasov and Sibiu rise sharply during festivals and the Christmas markets, and the countryside is very cheap. We do not print figures because they move, but the pattern holds across the year.

Is Romania safe?

For an ordinary visitor it is a normal European country and most trips pass without incident; the usual city cautions about pickpockets on crowded transport and unmetered taxis are the practical ones. Two things are particular to Romania: brown bears are genuinely present in the Carpathians, so follow local advice about rubbish and about not stopping to feed them at the roadside, and stray dogs still exist in rural areas. We could not read the current United States advisory on the day this page was written, so check it yourself at travel.state.gov before you travel.

Do I need a car?

Not for the cities, and not if your trip is Bucharest and Brasov by train. For Transylvania's villages, the fortified churches, Maramures, Bucovina and the mountain roads, a car makes a very large difference, because the interesting places are spread along roads rather than rails. A good compromise is the train from Bucharest to Brasov or Sibiu and a hire car from there for the days you need it. Driving is on the right.

Does Romania use the euro?

No. The currency is the Romanian leu, and although Romania has been in the European Union since 2007 it is not in the euro area and has no firm date for joining it. Cards work almost everywhere and contactless is normal, so most visitors barely handle cash; draw lei rather than carrying euro, and keep small notes for markets and village churches.

Is Romania in the Schengen area now?

Yes. Romania became a full member of the Schengen area on 1 January 2025, after its air and sea borders opened in March 2024. Arriving from another Schengen country is now an internal journey with no routine passport check, which is a change many guidebooks and websites have not caught up with. The Entry/Exit System applies when you first enter the Schengen area from outside it.

What should I eat?

Sarmale, the cabbage rolls that are the national dish, and mamaliga, the polenta that comes with everything. Ciorba, a sour soup, in a dozen regional versions. Mici, the grilled minced-meat rolls that are what Romania eats outdoors. Papanasi, a fried doughnut with sour cream and jam, for pudding. Add good bread, excellent tomatoes and sheep's cheese in summer, surprisingly good wine from Dealu Mare and Dragasani, and tuica or palinca, the plum and fruit brandies, if someone offers.
