Where to Stay in Cusco

Where to Stay in Cusco — Best Hotels & Neighbourhoods

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated August 2026

In Cusco, the question is not only which hotel but how many steps. The old city is built on a hillside at 11,150 feet, and a room three streets uphill from the Plaza can be a genuinely hard walk on your first day. Around the Plaza de Armas is the flattest and most convenient ground, within a few level minutes of the cathedral, the Qorikancha and every tour meeting point. San Blas, above it, is the loveliest quarter in the city and reached by long flights of stone steps — wonderful if you are sure-footed, punishing if you are not. Wanchaq, east of the centre, is flatter, quieter and cheaper, but you will take a taxi each time you go out. Below are the hotels we would send our own readers to, by style, each with an honest note and a Book Me link straight to Trip.com.

A word for travellers taking it gently. Cusco is high — about 11,150 feet, higher than any city most North Americans have slept in. Give yourself an easy first day, drink far more water than feels necessary, eat lightly, and treat the coca tea in the lobby as the local courtesy it is. Better still, spend your first two nights lower down in the Sacred Valley and come up to Cusco afterwards. The cobbles are uneven and often wet in the afternoon, so bring shoes with grip, and take a taxi uphill even when the map says it is only four blocks. None of this is medical advice; if you have heart or lung disease, talk to your own doctor before you book.

Luxury

Cusco’s grandest hotels are converted convents and colonial palaces, all within a few level streets of the Plaza.

Monasterio, A Belmond Hotel, Cusco

$$$$ LuxuryPlaza de Armas — 2 min1592 seminaryHistoric monument

A Spanish seminary of 1592 built on Inca foundations, arranged around a cloistered courtyard with a cedar tree at its centre, one level block from the Plaza de Armas. The setting is the point — you are sleeping inside a national monument — and the ground floor is flat and easy, though a building of this age has corridors and thresholds rather than modern uniformity. If a step-free route matters, ask for a courtyard-level room when you book.

Cusco Hotels — Frequently Asked Questions

Which part of Cusco should I stay in?

For most visitors, within three or four level streets of the Plaza de Armas. That puts the cathedral, the Qorikancha, the restaurants and the tour meeting points within a short walk on the flattest ground in the old city. San Blas is the prettiest quarter and the steepest, reached by long flights of stone steps, so choose it only if stairs at 11,150 feet are genuinely no trouble. Wanchaq, east of the centre, is flatter and quieter but a taxi ride from the sights.

Should I sleep in Cusco or the Sacred Valley first?

The Sacred Valley first, almost always. Cusco sits at about 11,150 feet and the Valley at roughly 9,400, and arriving from sea level straight into the higher of the two is what makes people ill. Two nights at Urubamba or Ollantaytambo before coming up to Cusco is gentler on everybody, and costs nothing extra, because you have to pass through the Valley on your way to Machu Picchu in any case.

Do Cusco hotels provide oxygen?

Many of the larger and more expensive Cusco hotels keep oxygen at reception or offer enriched-air rooms, and coca tea is offered everywhere as a matter of course. Because provision varies by property and changes without notice, we have not listed it hotel by hotel here — we would rather leave a gap than tell you something that turns out not to be true at check-in. If supplementary oxygen matters to you, ask the hotel directly by email before you book and keep the reply.

Are there adults-only hotels in Cusco?

Not that we can verify. Cusco’s hotels are historic convents and colonial houses that take guests of all ages, so rather than fill the category with something that does not fit, we have left it open and given you the full Cusco list instead.

Do I need a car in Cusco?

No, and we would advise against one. The old centre is a maze of one-way cobbled lanes with almost nowhere to park, and much of it is pedestrian. Taxis are inexpensive and everywhere. For the Sacred Valley and the airport, book a driver through your hotel; for Machu Picchu you travel by train from Ollantaytambo or Poroy in any case.
