Paphos

Where to Stay in Paphos

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Paphos is really two towns on top of one another. Down at the water, Kato Paphos is the holiday half: a small harbour with a Crusader fort at the end of the breakwater, a UNESCO archaeological park of Roman mosaic floors beginning about two minutes from the restaurant tables, and the hotels and bars that go with them. Up the hill, about two kilometres and a steep walk away, Ktima is the town Paphians actually live in, with the market, the municipal gardens, the bus station and the ordinary prices. North of both runs a long strip of larger hotels along the Tombs of the Kings road, and beyond that the coast opens out towards the sand at Coral Bay and eventually the empty Akamas. Which of those you pick decides almost everything about the holiday, and the difference between them is bigger here than in most Cypriot towns.

Kato Paphos: the harbour and the mosaics

Most centralWalk to the ruinsLively evenings

This is the part people picture when they book Paphos, and for a first visit it is the right answer. The harbour front is closed to traffic, the fort sits at the end of it, and the gate of the archaeological park — where the Roman mosaics are — is a short stroll from the last restaurant table. Everything a visitor needs is inside a fifteen-minute walk, including the seafront path that runs for miles in both directions. The trade is noise and price: the bar streets a block back from the water run late through the summer, so ask for a room away from Agiou Antoniou if you sleep lightly, and expect to pay most here.

Ktima: the upper town

Where the town livesBest valueSteep walk down

Two kilometres up the hill, Ktima is the working town: a covered market, the municipal gardens, the bishop’s palace, a good small archaeological museum and streets of tavernas where the menu is not in four languages. Prices for both rooms and dinner are noticeably lower than down at the harbour, and you are among residents rather than visitors. The catch is the hill. It is a genuinely steep twenty-five minutes down to Kato Paphos and a much longer haul back up; there is a frequent local bus and taxis are cheap, but plan on using one or the other rather than walking both ways in August.

The Tombs of the Kings road

Big hotelsFlat seafront pathPools rather than sand

The road running north from Kato Paphos past the Tombs of the Kings is where most of the larger resort hotels stand, many of them with their own stretch of waterfront. It is a flat fifteen to thirty minutes along the coastal path into the harbour, which is the main attraction of staying here, and the rock-cut tombs themselves — a startling underground necropolis, free of the crowds that fill the mosaics — are at the top of it. Be clear about what the sea does here before you book: most of this shore is rock and platform rather than beach, so if sand matters to your party, this is not the stretch for it.

Coral Bay and Peyia

Real sandFamiliesNeed a car or the bus

Twenty minutes or so north of town, Coral Bay is the proper sandy beach of the Paphos coast, a sheltered crescent between two headlands with the shallow water and the easy swimming that families come for. The village of Peyia sits on the slope above it with a strip of restaurants and a large population of long-term British and other European residents, which gives the area a slightly suburban feel that some people like very much and others do not. You are committed to a car or the bus for anything in Paphos itself, and the last bus back is earlier than you think.

Latchi and Polis: the quiet north-west

CalmestBase for the AkamasLittle to do after dark

Three-quarters of an hour north, at the far end of the Paphos district, Polis is a small inland town and Latchi below it a working fishing harbour with a long pebble beach and the boats that run along the Akamas coast. This is the quietest corner of holiday Cyprus and the natural base if the peninsula, the Baths of Aphrodite and the turtle beaches are what you came for. Getting around Paphos

On foot

Kato Paphos is flat and compact and you will walk all of it. The seafront path north past the Tombs of the Kings and south towards the lighthouse is level, paved and lit, and is the best thing about the town at either end of the day. Ktima, by contrast, is built up a hillside, and the link between the two halves of town is the one serious slope most visitors meet.

Buses

Paphos has the best town bus network on the island, with frequent services between Kato Paphos, Ktima, the Tombs road and Coral Bay, and a direct airport bus. Services thin out markedly in the evening and on Sundays, and the intercity coaches to Limassol and Nicosia go from the Karavella station up in Ktima rather than from the harbour.

Driving and parking

A car is what unlocks the west: the Akamas, the hill villages, the Troodos monasteries and the coast road to Aphrodite’s Rock are all an easy drive and awkward any other way. Driving is on the left. Parking in Kato Paphos is metered and tight in season, and Ktima’s old centre is narrow and one-way in places, so check your hotel has a space before you commit. One warning that catches people every week: the track along the Akamas to Lara Bay needs a genuine four-wheel drive, and ordinary hire cars are not insured on it.

The airport

Paphos international airport is about fifteen minutes south-east of town and is the right arrival point for anywhere in the west. It is small, seasonal in character and far quieter than Larnaca. If you are flying into Larnaca instead, allow around an hour and a half by road on the motorway.

When to come, and what it costs

Paphos is the mildest corner of the island in winter and one of the pleasanter ones in high summer, because the west gets a sea breeze that inland Cyprus does not. July and August are hot, busy and dearest, and the archaeological park in the middle of a summer afternoon is hard work — there is almost no shade over the mosaics. April, May, September and October are the months we would choose, with the sea at its warmest in the second half of that range. Winter is quiet, green and cheap, with some hotels and restaurants closing from November to March and the almond blossom out in February.

Getting in: Cyprus is in the EU but outside Schengen

The Republic of Cyprus belongs to the European Union and uses the euro, but it is not in the Schengen area. Days you spend in Cyprus do not count against the ninety days in any hundred and eighty that Schengen allows, and days spent in Schengen countries do not count against your Cyprus allowance; the two are tracked separately. Cyprus is also outside the EU’s Entry/Exit System, so there is no biometric registration on arrival. Citizens of the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, Australia and the European Union need no visa for a holiday of up to ninety days. British advice is a passport issued within the last ten years and valid three months beyond departure; United States advice recommends six months of validity. Confirm your own nationality’s terms with your own foreign ministry.

One rule matters more than any other here. Enter the Republic only through its own points of entry — the airports at Larnaca and Paphos and the southern seaports. Arriving through the north and crossing south is treated as illegal entry and can mean a fine or a refusal. Crossing north for a day from the south is the opposite: legal, routine and well worth doing, with your passport in your pocket. Time spent in the north still counts towards your ninety days, there are limits on goods brought back, and many hire companies restrict taking their cars across — check your rental agreement first.

Paphos — questions

Kato Paphos or Ktima?

Kato Paphos if it is your first visit, if you want to walk to the mosaics and the harbour, and if evenings out matter. Ktima if you have been before, want to pay less and eat where Paphians eat, and do not mind a steep hill between you and the sea. The honest compromise for many people is Kato Paphos for the location with a room a street or two back from the bar strip, which is quieter and cheaper than the front without losing the walk.

Is there a proper sandy beach in Paphos itself?

Not really, and this surprises people. The shore along the Tombs of the Kings road and much of the town front is rock, platform and imported pockets of sand, which is fine for swimming off a ladder and poor for children with buckets. The nearest real sand is Coral Bay, about twenty minutes north, and the municipal beach east of the harbour is small. If sand is the point of the holiday, consider Coral Bay or look at the south-east of the island instead.

How long do I need for the archaeological park?

Two hours is enough to see the main mosaic houses without rushing, and half a day if you want the odeon, the lighthouse and the far corners of the site as well. It is much bigger and much more exposed than it looks from the entrance, so go early or late, take water and a hat, and remember that the Tombs of the Kings is a separate site a couple of kilometres north and well worth its own hour.

Can I get to the Akamas without a four-wheel drive?

Yes, by boat, and most people should. Trips run from Latchi along the peninsula to the Blue Lagoon and back, which gives you the coast, the swimming and the scenery with none of the risk. On land you can drive as far as the Baths of Aphrodite on tarmac and walk the marked trails from there. What you should not do is take an ordinary hire car up the Lara track: it is rough, remote and specifically excluded by most rental agreements.

Is Paphos a good base for the rest of Cyprus?

For the west, entirely. Limassol and Kourion are about an hour, the Troodos villages an hour and a half by the mountain roads, and the Akamas and the wine villages are on the doorstep. Larnaca is about an hour and a half and Ayia Napa closer to two and a quarter, which makes the far south-east a long day out rather than an afternoon. If you want both ends of the island, most people split the week between Paphos and somewhere east.
