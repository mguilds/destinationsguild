Sri Lanka

Sri Lanka Travel Guide

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Sri Lanka is a single island about the size of Ireland, hung off the southern tip of India, and it holds more variety than any country of its size we know: surf coasts and whale water, a cool green hill country of tea estates crossed by one of the world’s great train rides, four ancient capitals out in the dry northern plains, national parks with wild elephants and leopards, and eight UNESCO World Heritage sites inside a space you can drive across in a day. The one thing to understand before you book is the weather, because Sri Lanka has two monsoons that arrive at opposite ends of the island at opposite times of year. There is almost always somewhere dry and sunny here. It is just not always the same somewhere.

Why visit Sri Lanka

A small island that behaves like a subcontinent, and one of the best-value places in Asia to travel slowly.

More island than its size suggests

You can drive from the surf on the south coast to tea estates at eighteen hundred metres in half a day, and on to the dry-zone plains and a fifth-century rock fortress by evening. Sri Lanka is about the size of Ireland and holds eight UNESCO World Heritage sites, two monsoons and a genuinely different landscape every couple of hours.

Wild elephants, and leopards you might actually see

Yala, Udawalawe, Wilpattu and Minneriya hold wild elephants in real numbers, and Yala has one of the densest leopard populations anywhere. A park morning here is a short drive from a beach hotel rather than an expedition, which is unusual.

One of the world’s great train rides

The line from Kandy up through the tea country to Ella and Badulla costs very little, takes most of a day, and spends it crossing viaducts and plantation hillsides with the doors open. A great many visitors rate it the best thing they did here.

Easy to travel in, and inexpensive

English is widely spoken, the distances are short, a car with a driver is affordable rather than extravagant, and the food — rice and curry, hoppers, string hoppers, kottu — is one of the underrated cuisines of Asia.

Sri Lanka at a glance

Capital

Colombo is the commercial capital and the city you fly into. Parliament actually sits at Sri Jayawardenepura Kotte, a suburb on its eastern edge.

Money

The Sri Lankan rupee (LKR, Rs). Cards work in hotels and city restaurants; carry cash for tuk-tuks, markets, buses and small towns.

Language

Sinhala and Tamil are both official. English is widely spoken in business, on signs, and almost universally in hotels.

Two monsoons

The southwest monsoon wets the west, south and hills from about May to September. The northeast monsoon wets the east and north from about November to February.

Getting about

Trains for the hill country and the south coast, buses everywhere for almost nothing, and a hired car with a driver for the rest — much the commonest way visitors cover the island.

Full moon days

Every full moon, or poya, is a public holiday. Shops and transport run as usual, but the sale of alcohol is restricted and bars and bottle shops close.

The regions

Five parts of the island, and a sensible first trip takes two or three of them.

Colombo and the west coast

The arrival side of the island. Colombo is the commercial capital and holds the best eating in the country; Negombo, ten minutes from the airport, is where most trips begin and end. The beaches along here are workmanlike rather than beautiful, and the whole strip is dry and bright from December to April.

The south coast

Galle’s Dutch walled town is the finest colonial survival in South Asia, and east of it the coast runs on through Unawatuna, Weligama, Mirissa and Tangalle: surf bays, reef swimming, stilt fishermen, turtles, and the blue whale boats from about November to April.

Kandy and the hill country

Up in the middle, and several degrees cooler. Kandy holds the Temple of the Sacred Tooth Relic and the last royal capital; beyond it the tea country climbs through Nuwara Eliya, Haputale and Ella, with the great train ride threading all of it and Adam’s Peak to climb overnight between December and May.

The Cultural Triangle

North of the hills, in the dry zone, stand the ruined capitals: Anuradhapura and Polonnaruwa, the painted caves at Dambulla and the rock citadel of Sigiriya, with Minneriya’s elephants and Wilpattu’s leopards between them. We have no Guild town guides out here yet; they are on the list.

The east coast and the north

The other half of the weather. Trincomalee, Pasikuda and Arugam Bay are dry, calm and at their best from about May to September, when the south-west is wet — the single most useful fact in planning a Sri Lankan trip. Further north, Jaffna is a different culture, cuisine and history again.

Things to do in Sri Lanka

The Kandy to Ella train

Five to seven hours through the tea estates, over the Nine Arch Bridge at Demodara and along plantation hillsides, for the price of a sandwich. Reserved seats go weeks ahead in season; unreserved always exists, often standing.

Sigiriya and Dambulla

A fifth-century palace on top of a two-hundred-metre rock, reached by about twelve hundred steps, with the Lion’s paws and the mirror-wall frescoes on the way up. Dambulla’s painted cave temples nearby ask far less of your knees.

Galle Fort

A seventeenth-century Dutch walled town on a headland, lived in rather than merely preserved, and the finest colonial survival in South Asia. Walk the ramparts at sunset, and stay the night so you get it without the day-trippers.

The Temple of the Sacred Tooth Relic

Kandy’s golden-roofed temple holds a tooth of the Buddha and is the most venerated site in the country. Go for the evening offering, with the drums; cover shoulders and knees and leave your shoes at the gate.

A park morning

Yala for leopards, Udawalawe for elephants almost guaranteed, Wilpattu for quiet and Minneriya for the Gathering, when hundreds of elephants come to the reservoir between about July and September. Go at dawn, and take a jeep with a driver who does not chase.

Whales, in the right season

Blue whales and sperm whales pass close inshore: off Mirissa on the south coast roughly November to April, and off Trincomalee on the east roughly May to October. The season is the whole trick — out of it the boats do not run.

Best time to visit & getting there

Sri Lanka has two monsoons, and almost every disappointing trip here comes from not knowing it. The southwest monsoon wets the west coast, the south coast and the hill country from roughly May to September. The northeast monsoon wets the east and the north from roughly November to February. They do not overlap, which means there is nearly always a dry half of the island — the question is only which half your dates point at.

December to April is the main season, and the one that suits the route most people want: Colombo, the Cultural Triangle, Kandy and the hill country, then the south coast for a week. Late January to March is the best of it, with February often the driest month on the south coast. Christmas and the New Year are the busiest and dearest fortnight of the year and want booking well ahead.

May to September is the time to go east instead. Trincomalee, Pasikuda and Arugam Bay are dry and calm while the south-west is wet, the Gathering of elephants at Minneriya is at its height from about July to September, and prices in the south and west fall a long way. October and November are the inter-monsoon weeks, when thunderstorms arrive on both coasts at once; they are the cheapest and the least reliable time to come, and we would not pick them for a first visit. Getting there means Bandaranaike International at Katunayake, 35 kilometres north of Colombo and about ten minutes from Negombo, which is why so many itineraries start and end in Negombo rather than the capital. Most visitors arrive on a one-stop routing through a Gulf hub, an Indian city or Singapore; direct long-haul service is limited. There is a second international airport at Mattala in the south-east with very few scheduled flights, so do not plan around it. Flights land at awkward hours far more often than not, so book the first night before you fly.

Fishing here

Sri Lanka’s fishing is saltwater rather than trout water, and a good deal of it is serious. The flats and the blue water off Kalpitiya on the north-west coast draw fly and light-tackle anglers for giant trevally, queenfish and barracuda, with sailfish beyond the reef; Trincomalee and the east work on the opposite monsoon calendar, so between them there is a season most of the year. The lagoons at Negombo and Puttalam make easy, cheap days out in an outrigger with a local boatman. Inland, the great reservoirs built by the old kings hold introduced tilapia and the giant snakehead, and the cool streams around Nuwara Eliya and Horton Plains were stocked with brown and rainbow trout in the British period and hold them still, which surprises people. Travelling in your later years

An easy island to travel slowly in, where the heat and the steps, not the distances, are the things to plan around.

Heat and humidity, not altitude

The lowlands sit a few degrees off the equator and are hot and humid all year. Do the sightseeing early and late, keep the middle of the day for lunch and shade, and drink more than you think you need. The hill country is the relief: Kandy is several degrees cooler and Nuwara Eliya genuinely cool.

Hire a car and a driver

This is the normal way to see Sri Lanka and it is not a luxury. It costs a fraction of the European equivalent, it removes the buses and the station steps, and a good driver is a guide and a problem-solver as well. Agree the route, the days and the price in writing before you set off.

Short days, not long drives

Roads are narrow, winding and slow, and forty kilometres of hill road is comfortably two hours. Three or four hours of driving is a full day here. The itineraries that disappoint people are the ones with seven stops in ten days.

Temples mean bare feet

Shoes and hats come off at every Buddhist temple, shoulders and knees must be covered, and the stone can be painfully hot by the middle of the day. Carry a pair of socks and go early; most sites have somewhere to leave shoes for a few rupees.

Steps and surfaces are the real obstacle

Accessibility is limited. Pavements are uneven or missing, and the great sites — Sigiriya above all, at about twelve hundred steps — involve serious climbing. Ask specifically rather than generally, and note that Dambulla, Polonnaruwa and the Galle ramparts give a great deal for much less effort.

Health care, and the sensible precautions

Private hospitals in Colombo and Kandy are good and used to foreign patients; standards fall away in rural areas. Dengue is present all year, so use repellent at dusk; drink bottled or filtered water; bring prescriptions in their original packaging with a copy, and take travel insurance seriously.

Getting in: what you need, and what you pay

Most visitors need an Electronic Travel Authorisation, the ETA, arranged before they fly. It is not a visa in the old sense; it is permission to board, and airlines check it. It is normally granted quickly, but not always instantly, so do it when you book rather than at the airport. Your passport should have at least six months left to run, and the officer at the border may ask to see an onward ticket and enough money for the trip.

We deliberately do not print the fee or the eligible-nationality list here. Sri Lanka has revised both more than once in the last few years, including periods when some passports were admitted free of charge and a change of portal part way through — and a number that is out of date is worse than no number at all. Read the current position off the Department of Immigration and Emigration’s own site at immigration.gov.lk at the moment you book.

Apply through that official site. There is a large industry of look-alike sites that will fill in the same form for a mark-up, and they rank well in search results; some are merely expensive and some are worse. If a site asks for payment in a currency the government does not use, or promises an “express” service for a premium, close it.

Two more practical points. Draw rupees or change a little money on arrival, because tuk-tuks, markets, buses and small restaurants want cash and small notes are useful; there are banks and machines in the arrivals hall. And note the rules on bringing in alcohol and tobacco are tighter than many visitors expect and are enforced, so read the customs allowance rather than assuming the usual duty-free limits apply.

Sri Lanka travel FAQ

When is the best time to visit Sri Lanka?

It depends entirely on which half of the island you want, because the two monsoons arrive at opposite ends at opposite times. For the classic route - Colombo, the south coast, Galle, the hill country and the Cultural Triangle - the season is December to April, and late January to March is the pick of it. For the east coast, Trincomalee and Arugam Bay, it is roughly May to September. October and November are the inter-monsoon weeks and the least reliable time anywhere on the island.

How many days do you need?

Ten days is a reasonable first trip and two weeks is comfortable. A sensible ten-day shape is a night near the airport, the Cultural Triangle for two, Kandy for two, the hill country and the train for two, and three or four on the south coast. The commonest mistake is treating a small map as a quick one: the roads are slow and forty kilometres of hill road is two hours.

Do I need a car, or is the train enough?

Most visitors hire a car with a driver for the bulk of the trip and take the train for the one leg that is worth it, Kandy to Ella. That is genuinely the best of both. A driver for a week or ten days costs a fraction of the European equivalent, covers the places buses do not reach, and removes all the logistics. Buses go everywhere for almost nothing if you would rather do it yourself.

Is Sri Lanka expensive?

It is one of the better-value destinations in Asia, with the usual caveat that the top end is priced internationally. Rice and curry in a local shop, a bus or train fare and a tuk-tuk across town cost very little; a boutique villa inside Galle Fort in February does not. Park entry fees for foreigners are the line item that surprises people most, so budget for them separately.

What do I need to enter Sri Lanka?

Most visitors need an Electronic Travel Authorisation, the ETA, arranged before they fly. Sri Lanka has changed its entry rules, its fees and its eligible-nationality list more than once in recent years, including spells when some passports were admitted free of charge, so we do not print a figure here. Read it off the Department of Immigration and Emigration's own portal at immigration.gov.lk when you book, and apply there rather than through an agent charging a mark-up for the same form.

Is it safe, and what should I watch for health-wise?

Sri Lanka is generally an easy and welcoming country to travel in, and the ordinary precautions are the ones that matter: traffic, the sea, and the sun. Rip currents and reef breaks drown people on both coasts every year, so swim where locals swim and ask which end of the bay is safe. Dengue is present all year, so use repellent at dusk. Drink bottled or filtered water. Check your own government's current travel advice before you book, as you would anywhere.

What should I wear at temples?

Shoulders and knees covered, shoes and hats off, and no turning your back on a Buddha image for a photograph. Carry a scarf or a sarong and a pair of socks, because the stone gets very hot. One rule worth taking seriously: visible Buddha tattoos and photographs posed irreverently with Buddha images have caused real trouble for visitors here, so treat the imagery with care rather than as decoration.

Which coast should I pick?

Go by the calendar rather than the brochure. Between December and April the south and west are dry and the east is wet, so Galle, Mirissa and Tangalle are the answer. Between May and September it reverses and Trincomalee, Pasikuda and Arugam Bay are the answer. If your dates fall in October or November, accept that it will be showery wherever you go and plan the hill country and the ancient cities instead of a beach.
