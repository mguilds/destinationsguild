Žabljak

Where to Stay in Žabljak

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Žabljak is a small, plain mountain town at about 1,450 metres — the highest town in the Balkans — and it exists for one reason, which is Durmitor National Park at its back door. Nobody comes for the architecture. What you get instead is a base within walking distance of the Black Lake, an hour or two from eighteen glacial lakes and a great many peaks, and a short drive from the Tara canyon. The town has a supermarket, a bus station, a row of restaurants and a scatter of guesthouses, chalets and small hotels. It is the one place in Montenegro where the weather, rather than the crowds, decides your holiday.

The town centre

Shops and busesWalk to the park gatePlainest surroundings

Central Žabljak is a few streets around the main road with the bus station, the shops, a bakery and most of the restaurants. It is not pretty, but it is where you can buy what you forgot, eat without driving and catch a bus, and it is a flat fifteen or twenty minutes' walk to the national park entrance and the Black Lake. For a first visit, or for anyone without a car, this is the sensible base. Rooms range from simple guesthouses to a handful of larger hotels.

The Black Lake road and the park entrance

Closest to the parkQuiet at nightShort walk to town

The road running west from the town towards Crno Jezero is lined with chalets, apartments and small hotels, and it is where most people who come specifically to walk choose to stay. You are minutes from the park gate and the lake circuit, the pine woods start at the door, and the town is still close enough to walk to for dinner. Prices are a little higher than in the centre and the places here fill first in summer and over the ski weeks, so book early for July, August and the winter holidays.

The Tara canyon side and Đurđevića Tara

Canyon viewsRafting baseNeed a car

North-east of the town, towards the bridge at Đurđevića Tara, there are guesthouses and eco-villages strung along the canyon road. This is the side to stay on if rafting or the canyon itself is the main event, and the views from the rim are the best in the region. You will need a car or an arranged transfer: it is a drive from Žabljak rather than a walk, and there is little within walking distance of any one property. Village houses and katun stays

Most authenticSimpleSeasonal

Scattered around the plateau are village houses and katuns — the summer shepherds' huts, some of them now fitted out for visitors. Staying in one is the closest thing to how this plateau has always been used, with cooking on wood, very dark nights and a long way to anything. Expect simple arrangements rather than hotel comforts, check heating and hot water carefully, and be realistic about the access road in bad weather. Most operate in summer only.

Kolašin as the alternative base

An hour and a half south, Kolašin is the other mountain base in the country, closer to Podgorica, on the railway, and with its own ski area at Kolašin 1450 and 1600. It suits anyone who wants the mountains without the drive up to the Durmitor plateau, or who is travelling by train. Getting around Žabljak

On foot

The town is small and walkable, and the park entrance and the Black Lake are a flat twenty minutes or so from the centre — one of the few places where the main attraction really is within walking distance of the beds.

Walking in the park

Durmitor's paths are properly waymarked and range from the easy lake circuit to serious full-day mountain routes. Weather changes fast at this altitude and snow lies late into the spring on the higher ground; check conditions at the park office before anything ambitious, and do not rely on a phone signal. There is an entrance fee for the park.

Driving

A car is close to essential for the canyon, the Sedlo pass and the villages, and the drive up from the coast is long — budget most of a day from Kotor or Budva, less from Podgorica now that the motorway has taken out the worst of the Morača canyon. In winter, winter tyres are a legal requirement in the mountains, chains are sometimes needed, and roads over the passes can close with little notice.

Buses

Buses connect Žabljak with Podgorica, Nikšić and the north, and they are a real option in summer. In winter the service is thinner and weather-dependent. There is no railway station at Žabljak; the nearest line runs through Mojkovac and Kolašin, well to the south-east.

When to come, and what it costs

Žabljak has a proper mountain climate and is the coldest inhabited place in the country, with deep snow in winter and cool nights even in midsummer. July and August are the walking months, warm in the day and fresh at night, and they are also the only months when everything is reliably open. June and September are quieter and lovely, with some risk of snow on the high routes at either end. Winter brings skiing at Savin Kuk and Javorovača and a very different, much slower town. Spring and late autumn are the thin seasons, when the snow is going or coming and a good deal is shut — check directly with anywhere you book.

Getting in: Montenegro is outside the EU and outside Schengen

Montenegro uses the euro but is not in the European Union and not in the Schengen area. That has one very useful consequence for a traveller: days spent here do not count against the ninety days in any hundred and eighty that Schengen allows, and the reverse is also true. Citizens of the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, Australia and the European Union do not need a visa for a short holiday, with ninety days in any hundred and eighty the usual allowance — confirm your own nationality’s terms with your own foreign ministry rather than with any website. Your passport should have been issued within the last ten years and be valid for at least three months beyond your departure, and you should make sure it is stamped on the way in and out, because those stamps are the record of your stay.

Every stay must be reported to the police. Montenegro’s own government says that duty falls on the accommodation provider, and hotels do it automatically at check-in; British government advice tells visitors to make sure they are registered within twenty-four hours and warns of a fine if they are not. In a hotel it is handled for you. In a private flat, ask your host to confirm it is done and keep the slip. Crossing to or from Croatia is a Schengen border with full checks and, in high summer, queues — and if you are driving a hire car across it, confirm first that the rental company permits it.

Žabljak — questions

How long do I need at Žabljak?

Two nights lets you walk the Black Lake circuit, drive out to the Tara bridge and see something of the plateau. Three or four is better, and is what we would plan: it gives you a full day on a proper mountain route, a day for the canyon and rafting, and a margin for the weather, which at this altitude will take a day away from you sooner or later. A single night means a long drive for a short look.

Is it worth coming up from the coast?

If you have a week or more in Montenegro, yes — this is the other half of the country and it looks nothing like the Adriatic. If you have four or five days and they are mostly beach days, the drive will eat two of them and you will see Durmitor through a car window. In that case keep it for another trip rather than doing it badly.

Can I go rafting on the Tara?

Yes, and it is the main organised activity in the area, run from bases around Đurđevića Tara and from Žabljak. The season runs through the warmer months and the water is highest in late spring, which is when the trips are at their most exciting; by late summer much of what is sold as rafting is a gentler float. Operators vary a great deal, so read what is actually included — how long you are on the water, the transfer and the equipment — before booking on price alone.

What about skiing?

There is skiing at Savin Kuk above the town and on the gentler slopes at Javorovača, and Kolašin to the south has the more developed area. It is small-scale by Alpine standards — a handful of lifts, short seasons that depend entirely on the snow, and simple facilities — and it is priced accordingly. Come for cheap, uncrowded days on the snow in genuinely beautiful country, not for a big resort.

Will the roads be open in winter?

The main roads to Žabljak are kept open, but the high passes — Sedlo in particular — close with snow, and conditions can change within hours. Winter tyres are required in the mountains in the cold months and you may need chains. If you are driving up between November and April, check the road situation that morning, keep the tank full, and allow far more time than the distance suggests.
