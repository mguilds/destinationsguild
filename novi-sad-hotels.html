Novi Sad

Where to Stay in Novi Sad

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Novi Sad is the easiest city in Serbia to be a visitor in. It is flat, it is compact, its centre is pedestrianised and handsome in a Habsburg way the rest of the country is not, and the Danube runs along the bottom of it with the great bulk of Petrovaradin Fortress on the far bank. It was founded as a free royal city under the Habsburgs in 1748, grew up as the cultural capital of the Serbs inside Austria-Hungary, and still carries that: a theatre, a gallery quarter, cafes that take their time. It is also the home of EXIT, which fills the fortress and every bed in the city for one week each July. Most people come for a day from Belgrade on the fast train; it is worth two or three nights, and it is the sensible base if walking is a consideration.

Where to stay in Novi Sad, area by area

The parts of Novi Sad a visitor actually chooses between, and who each one suits.

The pedestrian centre: Zmaj Jovina and Dunavska

Most centralFlatBest for a short stay

The obvious choice, and an unusually good one. Zmaj Jovina runs from Liberty Square — the cathedral, the City Hall — to the Bishop's Palace, and Dunavska carries on from it towards the river, all of it pedestrian, level and lined with cafes. The theatre, the museums, the market and the quay are each a few minutes away. Rooms are mostly small hotels and apartments above the streets. This is the dearest part of town and the only noise is the cafe trade, which is not late by Serbian standards.

The Danube quay and Beogradska Kapija

River viewsWalk to centreQuiet evenings

The streets between Dunavska and the water, running out to the old Belgrade Gate and the embankment path. It is five minutes from the pedestrian centre, quieter, and gives you the river and the view across to the fortress from the doorstep. The Strand, the city's sand beach on the Danube, is a short way upstream and is where Novi Sad spends its summer. Good for a longer, slower stay.

Petrovaradin and Podgradje

Fortress viewsCharacterA climb

Across the bridge on the Srem bank, under and on the rock. Podgradje, the lower town, is a small grid of baroque streets at the foot of the fortress; the fortress itself holds the clock tower, the museum, artists' studios and the terrace with the best view in the city back over the Danube at Novi Sad. Staying here is atmospheric and a little apart, and it is a fifteen-minute walk over the bridge into the centre. The climb to the upper fortress is steep; a taxi goes to the top.

Liman and the university side

CheapestParksResidential

South-west of the centre along the river, built from the 1960s as numbered Liman districts, with the university, the Dunavski park end and a long green embankment. It is residential, inexpensive, flat and about twenty minutes' walk or a short bus ride from Zmaj Jovina. The right choice if you want value, space and somewhere to run or cycle along the water, and the wrong one if you want to step out of the door into the old town.

Sremska Kamenica and the Fruska Gora side

Wine countrySpaceNeed a car

Out of the city across the bridge and up, where the Fruska Gora ridge begins: village guesthouses, wine cellars, the monasteries at Krusedol and Grgeteg within half an hour, and the national park paths above. There are good small places to stay out here and it is a different holiday from the city one — it wants a car, and it is the answer if Novi Sad is a base for the countryside rather than a city break.

Getting around Novi Sad

On foot

This is the point of Novi Sad. The centre is flat, pedestrianised and small enough to cross in fifteen minutes, the quay path is level, and the pavements are in good order. The only real climb in the city is Petrovaradin Fortress, and the only cobbles are in Podgradje under it. If you have been warned off Belgrade by the hills, this is the city to stay in instead.

Buses

City buses cover everything a visitor needs and cost very little; buy a ticket and validate it. The network radiates from the centre, so a cross-town journey can mean changing. Services to Petrovaradin and Sremska Kamenica run over the bridges regularly.

Taxis and apps

Cheap, plentiful and the easy way to the top of the fortress or out to Fruska Gora. Ride-hailing apps operate in the city. For a street taxi use a rank and check the meter.

The fast train to Belgrade

The Soko service runs from Novi Sad station to Belgrade Centre in well under an hour on modern double-deckers, and north to Subotica. It has turned a day trip into a commute and is much the best way between the two cities — book at the station or online. The station is a fifteen-minute walk or a short bus ride from the centre. The through service on to Budapest has been delayed more than once, so check it with the operator rather than assuming.

EXIT week

For one week in July the fortress becomes a festival site and the city fills completely: rooms go months ahead and at several times the usual price, and the bridges and the Petrovaradin side get very busy at night. The dates move from year to year. If you are coming for the festival, book early; if you are not, check the dates before you book anything in July.

When to come

Novi Sad keeps the same continental calendar as Belgrade, an hour down the motorway: cold foggy winters, thundery Junes, hot still Augusts, long golden autumns. May, June and September are the pick, with the cafe tables out on Zmaj Jovina and the quay pleasant in the evening. July is the exception to everything — EXIT fills the city for a week and prices go with it, so check the dates first. August is hot and the Strand is the centre of city life. October is lovely and is the month for Fruska Gora, the vineyards and the monasteries. December to February is cold and often foggy off the Danube, cheap, and fine for a city that does most of its living indoors anyway.

Getting in: what applies, and what does not

Serbia is not in the European Union and not in the Schengen area, so it runs its own border. British passport holders may visit without a visa for up to 90 days in any 6-month period, with a passport valid for at least 90 days beyond the day they leave — that is what the UK government’s Serbia travel advice said when we read it on 26 November 2026. Many other nationalities including the United States are admitted visa-free for comparable periods; we could not open the American source on the day we wrote this, so check your own. Two Serbian rules catch people out: make sure your passport is actually stamped on entry, because leaving without an entry stamp can mean charges of illegal immigration, a heavy fine and a prison sentence; and you must register with the local police within 24 hours, which a hotel or registered apartment does for you automatically. If Kosovo is also in your plans, read the Kosovo paragraph in The Serbia Travel Guide before you book anything — entering Serbia from Kosovo without an existing Serbian entry stamp usually means being refused.

If your trip also crosses into Hungary, Croatia, Bulgaria, Greece or anywhere else in the Schengen area — and most flights from North America reach Serbia through a Schengen hub — the following applies to that crossing and not to Serbia:

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Novi Sad — questions

Is Novi Sad worth staying in rather than visiting from Belgrade?

Yes, if you want an easier city or you are using it for Fruska Gora. The fast train makes the day trip very simple, so plenty of people do only that and see the centre and the fortress. But Novi Sad is flatter, quieter and cheaper than Belgrade, the evenings on the quay and in the pedestrian streets are the best part of it, and the wine country and the monasteries are half an hour away. Two or three nights is a good holiday.

Where is the best view of the city?

From the terrace of the upper Petrovaradin Fortress, looking back across the Danube at Novi Sad with the bridges and the cathedral spire. It is a steep walk up from Podgradje or a short taxi to the top. Late afternoon is the time. The clock tower on the terrace has its hands reversed — the large hand shows the hours — so boatmen on the river could read it from a distance.

When is the EXIT festival, and should I avoid it?

It runs for several days at the Petrovaradin Fortress in July, and the dates move from year to year, so check the year's dates before booking anything in that month. If you want the festival, book months ahead. If you do not, it is the one week when Novi Sad is expensive, full and loud at night, and a different week would serve you better.

How long does the train to Belgrade take?

Well under an hour on the Soko fast service, on the rebuilt Belgrade–Budapest main line. Trains are modern double-deckers and inexpensive, and run frequently enough to be used casually. The same line runs north to Subotica. Buy at the station or online; the station is a short walk or bus ride from the pedestrian centre.

Is Novi Sad good if walking is difficult?

It is the best city in Serbia for that. The centre is flat and pedestrianised, the Danube quay is level, distances are short, and taxis are cheap and everywhere. The exception is Petrovaradin Fortress, which is a real climb from the lower town — take a taxi to the upper terrace and walk down if you want to, rather than the reverse.

What is there to do in the countryside nearby?

Fruska Gora, the low wooded ridge above the Danube, is a national park with sixteen working monasteries in it, marked walking paths and the best wine district in northern Serbia — Sremski Karlovci, ten minutes away, is the pretty baroque town at the centre of it. Further out, Sombor and Zrenjanin are quiet Habsburg towns, and the Tisa and the Danube backwaters are serious fishing water.
