Odense

Where to Stay in Odense

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Odense is the third city of Denmark and the middle of the island of Funen, and most visitors come for one reason: Hans Christian Andersen was born here, and the town has built a serious museum around the fact. It is a flat, compact, easy place to stay, and the practical choice is only between the old quarter, the station and the river — all within a quarter of an hour of each other on foot.

The old centre and the Andersen quarter

Most convenientWalk everywhereThe museums

Odense keeps its sights in one place: the cathedral, the pedestrian streets, the small coloured houses of the old quarter and the Hans Christian Andersen museum that was rebuilt underneath them in 2021. Staying in the middle means walking to all of it, and the centre is compact enough that “central” genuinely means central. It is also where the rooms are dearest, which in Odense is not saying a great deal.

Around the station — the north side

Near the trainsBest valueTen minutes to everything

The main station sits just north of the old centre, with the concert hall and the big shopping centre beside it, and most of Odense’s chain hotel rooms within two or three streets. If you are stopping here for a night between Copenhagen and Jutland — which is what a good many visitors do — this is the sensible address. The walk south into the old quarter takes about ten minutes and is entirely flat.

Munke Mose and the river side

Green and quietRiverside walksShort walk in

South-west of the centre the river opens into a park, and the path along it runs for miles out of town past the open-air museum at Den Fynske Landsby. This side is quieter and greener, with a handful of smaller hotels and guest houses, and the river walk is the single nicest thing to do in Odense on a fine morning. You are still only fifteen minutes on foot from the cathedral.

Getting around

The centre is small, flat and almost entirely pedestrian, and you will walk most of it. City buses cover the rest on a ticket bought in the app or at a machine, and the light rail line that opened in 2022 runs from the hospital through the station and out to the university, which is useful if your hotel is off the middle.

Odense calls itself a cycling city and means it: lanes are separated, the ground is flat and hire is easy. For Egeskov, the archipelago or the rest of Funen you will want a car or a regional bus; in the town itself a car is simply something to park.

When to come, and what it costs

Odense keeps the Danish pattern — long light and open terraces from late May to early September, mild and dark in winter — and it is cheaper than Copenhagen or Aarhus in every season. Summer is when the river park and the open-air museum are at their best; the Hans Christian Andersen festival fills the town for a few days in late August.

If you are passing through rather than staying, any month works: the museums are indoors and the walk from the station is short. Our month-by-month guide to Denmark sets out the year.

Denmark uses the Danish krone (DKK, written kr), not the euro. Denmark is a member of the European Union but voted to keep its own currency and has set no date for changing it, so euro notes are not legal tender — though the krone is held in a fixed band against the euro, which is why the rate barely moves from one year to the next. Pay in kroner. Denmark is one of the most nearly cashless countries in the world: cards and phones are taken everywhere, including on buses, at museum desks and at market stalls, and a good many places no longer handle notes at all, so you need very little cash and may need none. Prices in shops include the twenty-five per cent sales tax, which is among the highest in Europe and is already in the figure on the shelf. Tipping is not expected and never chased — service is in the bill, and rounding up is plenty. Decline any card machine that offers to charge you in dollars; pay in kroner and let your own bank convert.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS — the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years — is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today — nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Denmark has been in the European Union since 1973 and in the Schengen area since 2001, so everything above applies here in full and an arrival from anywhere else in Schengen involves no passport control at all. Denmark did not join the euro, so the currency is the krone. If you plan to cross to Malmö in Sweden, carry your passport even though the bridge is inside Schengen: Sweden has run identity checks on that crossing on and off for years.

Odense hotels FAQ

Is Odense worth a stop?

For a night, yes — particularly if you are crossing between Copenhagen and Jutland anyway, since the train stops here and the old quarter is a ten-minute walk from the platform. The Hans Christian Andersen museum, rebuilt in 2021, is genuinely good and not the gift-shop exercise people expect. Two nights is plenty unless you are using it as a base for the rest of Funen.

Where should I stay in Odense?

The old centre if you want to walk out into the sights, the streets around the station if you want value and an easy arrival, and the river side at Munke Mose if you would rather have a park outside the window. The whole town centre is flat and about twenty minutes across, so the difference between them is small.

How far is Odense from Copenhagen?

About an hour and a quarter by train, with frequent departures across the Great Belt bridge, and roughly the same again on to Aarhus. Trains run direct from Copenhagen Airport, so you need not go into the city to change. There is no useful passenger airport at Odense itself; Billund is about an hour away by road.

What else is there on Funen?

Egeskov, a moated Renaissance castle with large gardens, about half an hour south; the open-air museum of old Funen farm buildings on the edge of Odense itself; and the small sailing islands of the southern archipelago, reached by ferry from Svendborg. A car or a bicycle helps for all of them.
