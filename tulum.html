Tulum Travel & Adventure Guide

The only Maya city built on the sea — temples on a cliff above the Caribbean, jungle cenotes a few minutes inland, and a beach road of barefoot, candlelit hotels.

Find Tulum Hotels Search Flights

Tulum is two places wearing one name. There is the archaeological site — a walled Maya port whose temples sit on a low limestone cliff with the Caribbean breaking below, the only major Maya city built facing the sea. And there is the town it gave its name to: a highway pueblo of taco stands and dive shops, and a long beach road south of it where jungle-chic hotels run on generators and candlelight and charge accordingly. Between the two lies the real draw — a jungle floor riddled with cenotes, the freshwater sinkholes of the Yucatán, and beyond them the vast, empty Sian Ka'an Biosphere Reserve. Tulum is smaller and slower than Cancún or Playa, and it rewards travellers who came for the ruins, the water and the quiet more than for the nightlife.

The walled city was a working Maya port between roughly the 13th and 15th centuries, trading obsidian, jade and salt up and down the coast. Its original name is thought to have been Zamá — "dawn" — which makes sense the moment you stand on the cliff and look east. "Tulum" means "wall", the name given later for the stone rampart that still rings the site on three sides, the sea guarding the fourth.

The modern town is split in two, and the split matters more here than anywhere else on the coast. Tulum Pueblo sits on Highway 307 — this is where the ordinary restaurants, supermarkets, bike rentals and affordable hotels are. The Zona Hotelera is the beach road, a single strip running south along the coast, where the boutique hotels sit right on the sand at prices that can startle. There is no bank of ATMs down there, cell signal is patchy, and many properties run on solar and generators — which is precisely the point for some travellers and a nasty surprise for others.

A bicycle is the single best investment you can make in Tulum. The beach road is long, flat and shaded, taxis between town and the coast add up fast, and cycling from your hotel to the ruins at sunrise — before the buses arrive from Cancún — is the way most seasoned visitors do it.

Why visit Tulum

Four reasons Tulum is unlike anywhere else on the Riviera Maya.

Ruins above the sea

No other major Maya city was built on the coast. The temples stand on a cliff with the Caribbean breaking below — and a small beach beneath them you can swim from.

Cenote country

Tulum sits on the densest concentration of cenotes on the peninsula. Gran Cenote, Dos Ojos and Calavera are all minutes away.

Sian Ka'an

South of town lies a UNESCO biosphere reserve the size of a small country — mangrove channels, lagoons, birds and almost no one.

A different kind of hotel

The beach road specialises in small, design-led, low-rise hotels. No high-rises, no wristbands — but bring a torch and low expectations for Wi-Fi.

Top things to do in Tulum

The experiences worth building the trip around.

● The Tulum ruins

Compact, spectacular and best at opening time. The cliff-top Castillo above the sea is the picture everyone comes for — go early, before the coaches.

● Gran Cenote

Clear water, turtles and a swim-through cavern a few minutes up the Cobá road. Snorkel gear rents on site.

● Dos Ojos

Two linked cenotes and one of the world's great cavern dives — good enough for certified divers, easy enough to snorkel.

● Sian Ka'an Biosphere

Boat into the mangrove channels, float the ancient Maya canals, and watch for dolphins, turtles and hundreds of bird species.

● Cobá

An inland Maya city swallowed by jungle, 45 minutes northwest. Rent a bike at the gate — the site is far bigger than it looks.

● Laguna Kaan Luum

A shallow turquoise lagoon with a deep cenote at its heart, just south of town — and far quieter than the beach.

Where to stay in Tulum

The choice is unusually stark here. Tulum Pueblo — the town on the highway — is where the value is: guesthouses, small hotels, real restaurants, bike rental on every corner, and ATMs that work. You are a 10-minute cycle or a short taxi from the sand. The Zona Hotelera, the beach road, is where the famous hotels are: low-rise, design-led, palm-thatched, directly on the beach — and priced for it, often with limited power, patchy signal and cash-only bars. The northern end of the beach road, nearest the ruins, is the liveliest; the far south, towards Sian Ka'an, is the quietest and most remote. Pick the town if you want value and convenience, the beach road if the hotel itself is the holiday.

Best time to visit & getting there

When to go

December–April is the dry season — the best weather, the biggest crowds, the highest prices. May and November are the value months. June–November is hurricane season, and sargassum seaweed can hit the beaches hardest in the warm months. If the beach is the whole point of the trip, aim for the dry season.

Getting there

Tulum now has its own airport — Felipe Carrillo Puerto (TQO), opened at the end of 2023 — which puts you within about half an hour of town. Cancún (CUN) remains the most widely served gateway, roughly 1.5 to 2 hours north; the ADO bus runs frequently and cheaply from the airport, and private transfers are easy to book.

Tulum travel FAQ

Should you stay in Tulum town or on the beach road?

Tulum Pueblo, the town on the highway, is where the everyday restaurants, shops and cheaper hotels are — and it's far better value. The Zona Hotelera, the beach road, is where the boutique, jungle-chic hotels sit directly on the sand, at a considerable premium. Many travellers stay in town and cycle or taxi to the beach.

Which airport do you fly into for Tulum?

Tulum now has its own international airport, Felipe Carrillo Puerto (TQO), which opened in December 2023. Cancún International (CUN) is still the most widely served option and sits roughly 1.5 to 2 hours north, with frequent ADO buses and private transfers.

Are the Tulum ruins worth visiting?

Yes. Tulum is the only major Maya city built on the coast, and its temples stand on a cliff directly above the Caribbean — a sight you get nowhere else. The site is compact, so go early to beat both the heat and the tour buses from Cancún.

When is the best time to visit Tulum?

December through April brings the driest, most reliable weather — and the highest prices and crowds. May and November are good value. June to November is hurricane season, and sargassum seaweed can affect the beaches most in the warmer months.
