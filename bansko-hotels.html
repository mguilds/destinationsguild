Bansko

Where to Stay in Bansko

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Bansko is the biggest ski town in Bulgaria and the one that made the country’s reputation for cheap, serious winter sports. It sits at about 925 metres at the foot of the Pirin, with Vihren — 2,914 metres, the second-highest peak in the country — behind it and a gondola rising from the edge of town to the ski area at Banderishka Polyana. The skiing is genuinely good for the money: a decent vertical, tree-lined runs, reliable snowmaking on the lower slopes, and a season that usually holds from mid-December to late March. The town is two things at once, and knowing which part you are booking matters: an old quarter of heavy stone houses, cobbled lanes and mehanas where the food and the rakia are the best argument for the place, and a ring of large modern hotels and apartment blocks built fast in the boom years. In summer it is a walking and mountain-biking base under a UNESCO national park, at a fraction of winter prices.

Where to stay in Bansko, area by area

The parts of Bansko a visitor chooses between — and in a ski town the only question that really matters is how far you are from the gondola.

The gondola base

Ski in reachModern hotelsQueues

The streets immediately around the gondola station at the southern edge of town, where most of the big modern hotels and apartment complexes stand. This is the convenient choice in winter: you can walk to the lift carrying skis, which in a town with no ski-in ski-out accommodation is as good as it gets, and the hotels here are the ones with pools and spas. Two things to know. The morning gondola queue in the February half-term and the New Year week is long enough to be a real subject of complaint, and many hotels run their own shuttle which does not beat it. And this quarter is modern and a little bleak out of season.

The Old Town and the mehanas

Most characterBest eatingCobbles

The historic centre around Holy Trinity church: heavy stone houses with high walls and wooden gates, narrow cobbled lanes, the Velyanov House museum, and the mehanas — cellar taverns with open fires serving Bansko’s own cooking, which is the real pleasure of the town. A scattering of small hotels and restored guest houses sit among them. It is a ten to twenty-minute walk from the gondola, which in ski boots in the dark is further than it sounds, so this is the choice for anyone who cares more about the evenings than the morning queue, and for summer.

Pirin Street and the ski road

Middle groundShuttlesMixed value

The spine between the old town and the gondola, lined with hotels, apartments, bars, ski hire and the English-language pubs that Bansko has acquired. It is the compromise: five to ten minutes from the lift, walking distance to the old town, and the widest range of prices in the resort. It is also where the nightlife is, which cuts both ways — ask about the position of the room if you intend to sleep early before a first lift.

Dobrinishte and the Razlog valley

QuietMineral springsNeeds transport

The villages spread along the valley floor north and east of Bansko: Razlog with the mountains on three sides, and Dobrinishte six kilometres away with mineral springs and its own small ski area and lift. Prices here are well below Bansko and the setting is better, with the Rila on one side and the Pirin on the other. You will need a car or a taxi for the gondola each morning, which for a week is a real consideration in winter and no trouble at all in summer, when this is the nicer place to be.

Staying for the summer mountain

WalkingCheap in summerNational park

Worth treating as its own choice, because the town is a different proposition from June to September and a great deal cheaper. Pirin National Park is on the UNESCO World Heritage list and begins above the town: Vihren, the Banderitsa and Demyanitsa huts, glacial lakes, and the Baikusheva Mura, a Bosnian pine reckoned to be well over a thousand years old, a short walk from the road. The gondola runs in summer for walkers and bikers. Any part of town works; the old town and the valley are the pleasant ones.

Getting around Bansko

The gondola, and the queue

One gondola carries everybody from the town up to the ski area, and it is the single fact that shapes a Bansko ski day. In quiet weeks it is quick. In the New Year week and the February half-term the morning queue can run to an hour or more, and the hotel shuttles all arrive at the same time, so they do not help. The ways round it are to be at the base before it opens, to ski the afternoons and take a long lunch, or to come in late January or mid-March instead.

Getting about the town

Bansko is small and walkable in summer, and in winter it is small and walkable in everything except ski boots. The old town to the gondola is ten to twenty minutes on foot. Local taxis are cheap and plentiful and are the normal answer for the trip up with equipment, and most of the larger hotels run a shuttle to the lift. There is no need for a hire car unless you are staying out in the valley.

Getting there from Sofia

About two and a half hours by road, roughly 160 kilometres south, and almost everyone arrives by pre-booked transfer or coach from Sofia Airport — it is the standard package arrangement and much the simplest. Buses run from Sofia’s central bus station. There is also a narrow-gauge railway from Septemvri to Dobrinishte, which is slow, lovely and not a sensible way to carry luggage.

Ski hire, lessons and the pass

Hire shops are spread along the ski road and around the gondola base, and the price difference between them and the Alps is the reason people come. Lessons, lift passes and equipment booked together through a hotel or an operator are usually the cheapest route. The ski area has nursery slopes up at Banderishka Polyana rather than in the town, so beginners take the gondola too.

Summer in the Pirin

The gondola runs for walkers and mountain bikers, and from the top the paths lead up to the Banderitsa hut, the Vihren hut and the glacial lakes, with Vihren itself a long and serious day. The Baikusheva Mura, one of the oldest trees in the country, is a short walk from the road above town. Weather at that height turns fast and the huts are seasonal, so check before you set out and treat the last gondola down as a deadline.

When to come

Mid-January to early March is the heart of the ski season and the most reliable snow. Mid-December to mid-January works but depends on the year, and the week between Christmas and New Year is the most expensive and most crowded of the winter. The February half-term is the other peak, and the gondola queue at that point is the resort’s weak spot. Late January and mid-March are the value weeks, with cheaper beds and shorter queues; by late March the lower runs go patchy even when the top is good.

June to September is the other Bansko: walking and biking in a UNESCO national park, warm days, cool nights at 925 metres, the mehanas still open and prices a fraction of the winter. The high huts and the lakes are a July-to-September proposition. April, May, October and November are the in-between months when the town is genuinely quiet and a good deal is shut — cheap, but not much to do.

Getting in: what applies, and what does not

Bulgaria is in the European Union and, since 1 January 2025, a full member of the Schengen area — air and sea borders opened in March 2024 and the land borders followed on New Year’s Day 2025. Arriving from another Schengen country is now an internal journey with no routine passport check, which is a real change from older guidebooks. Bulgaria is also in the euro area: it adopted the euro on 1 January 2026 at a rate fixed at 1.95583 lev to the euro, and the lev stopped being legal tender on 1 February 2026, so prices here are in euro and there is no currency to change.

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen external border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave. Ninety days in any one hundred and eighty is the visa-free tourist allowance for American, Canadian, British and Australian visitors, and it is counted across the whole Schengen area rather than against Bulgaria alone.

Bansko — questions

Where should I stay in Bansko for skiing?

Near the gondola if the skiing is the whole point — you can walk to the lift with your skis, and the bigger hotels with pools and spas are there. Near the old town if the evenings matter more, accepting a ten to twenty-minute walk or a cheap taxi each morning. Pirin Street and the ski road are the compromise and have the widest range of prices. The Razlog valley is cheaper and prettier and needs a car or a daily taxi.

How bad is the Bansko gondola queue?

In a quiet week, not bad at all. In the New Year week and the February half-term it is the resort’s real weakness and can run to an hour or more in the morning, because one gondola serves the whole town and every hotel shuttle arrives at once. Be at the base before it opens, or ski afternoons, or — best of all — book late January or mid-March instead, when the snow is still good and the queue is not a story.

Is Bansko good value?

Yes, and it is the main reason to come. Lift passes, lessons, equipment hire, food and accommodation are all well below Alpine prices, and a week here with everything included costs a fraction of a comparable French or Austrian week. What you give up is size and altitude: the ski area is modest next to a big Alpine resort, and the lower slopes depend on snowmaking in a warm winter.

Is there anything to do in Bansko in summer?

A great deal, and it is the town’s best-kept secret. Pirin National Park is on the UNESCO World Heritage list and starts above the town: marked paths from the top of the gondola to the mountain huts, glacial lakes, Vihren at 2,914 metres for a serious day, and the Baikusheva Mura, a pine well over a thousand years old, a short walk from the road. Mountain biking is good, the mehanas stay open, and prices are a fraction of the winter.

What are the mehanas?

Cellar taverns in the old stone houses, with open fires, long wooden tables and Bansko’s own cooking — grilled meat, bean dishes, cured sausage, sheep cheese and a great deal of rakia, usually with live folk music. They are the best thing about the town, they are cheap, and they are concentrated in the old quarter rather than up by the gondola, which is an argument for staying down there or being happy to walk.

Do I need cash in Bansko?

Some. Bulgaria has used the euro since 1 January 2026 and the hotels, ski hire and lift passes all take cards, but the smaller mehanas, a mountain hut and the village shops out in the valley are easier with cash. Prices quoted in lev on older websites or operator pages are from before the changeover.
