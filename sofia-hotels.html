Sofia

Where to Stay in Sofia

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Sofia is a capital with a 2,290-metre mountain at the end of the tram line, which tells you most of what you need to know about it. It is high itself, in a basin at around 550 metres, which makes the summers bearable and the winters properly cold. The centre is a compact square of yellow cobbled boulevards where a Roman street, a sixth-century church, an Ottoman mosque, a synagogue and a gold-domed Orthodox cathedral stand within a few hundred metres of one another, and where the metro excavations kept turning up more Roman city until the builders gave in and left it on display under the Largo. It is flat, green, cheap by any European measure, and it has the country’s only year-round international airport with a metro line straight into town. Most Bulgarian trips start here, and a good many should give it two nights rather than one.

Where to stay in Sofia, area by area

The parts of Sofia a visitor actually chooses between, and who each one suits.

The centre: the Largo, Serdika and the cathedral quarter

Most centralWalk everywhereMetro

The square half-mile that holds almost everything a first visit wants: the Largo with the excavated Roman streets of Serdica underneath it, Saint Alexander Nevsky cathedral with its gold domes, the fourth-century rotunda of Saint George in a courtyard behind the presidency, Sveta Sofia, the Banya Bashi mosque, the synagogue and the covered market all within a short walk, and the National Archaeological Museum in the old mosque. Serdika and Sofiyski Universitet metro stations are both here. Stay in this quarter if it is your first time and you have two or three nights — you will walk to nearly everything and the pavements are level.

Vitosha Boulevard and the NDK

PedestrianRestaurantsShopping

The pedestrianised shopping street that runs south from the centre towards the National Palace of Culture, with the mountain filling the view at the far end on a clear day. It is the city’s promenade: cafe tables down both sides, the best concentration of restaurants and bars in Sofia in the side streets around it, and the NDK gardens at the bottom. Quieter at night than the old-town quarters of most capitals, because the drinking is spread through the side streets rather than stacked in one lane. A good choice if you want to eat well and walk home.

Oborishte and the Doctor’s Garden

QuietLeafyEmbassies

East of the cathedral, the embassy quarter: wide streets of large nineteenth and early twentieth-century houses, mature trees, small parks including the Doctor’s Garden, and a handful of the better small hotels. It is ten to fifteen minutes on foot from the Largo and feels a world quieter. This is the choice for a longer stay, for anyone who wants to be central without the street noise, and for sleeping with the window open in summer.

Lozenets and the Vitosha foothills

ResidentialNear the mountainNeeds transport

South of the NDK, running up towards the mountain: a comfortable residential district of apartment blocks and newer buildings, with the good end of the city’s restaurant scene spread through it and the ring road and Vitosha beyond. It is the natural base if you have a car, if you intend to spend days on the mountain, or if you are here for business in the southern office districts. You will use the metro or a taxi to reach the historic centre, which is five to fifteen minutes away depending where exactly you are.

The Central Station and Lion’s Bridge end

Best valueTransportRougher edges

North of the centre around the railway station and the central bus station, with Lion’s Bridge on the way in. This is where the cheapest beds in the city are, and it is on the metro with one stop to Serdika, so it is genuinely convenient for an early train or a morning bus to Rila. It is also the least handsome part of central Sofia and the one place a visitor should keep an ordinary eye on their bag at night. Worth it for a short stay or a tight budget; not where we would spend a week.

Getting around Sofia

The metro

Two lines, clean, cheap and the thing that makes Sofia easy. Line 1 and Line 2 cross at Serdika in the middle of the historic centre, and Line 1 runs out to Sofia Airport Terminal 2 in one direction and to Business Park Sofia in the other. Tickets are bought at machines or by contactless card at the gate, and the same ticket covers the trams and buses within its time. Trains run from roughly five in the morning to midnight.

Trams, buses and trolleybuses

A dense old network that covers everything the metro does not, including the tram up towards Vitosha. Some of the rolling stock is venerable and some is new. Contactless payment works on most vehicles now, and a validated ticket covers a set period across metro, tram and bus. Stop announcements are in Bulgarian, so count the stops or follow the map on your phone.

Taxis, and the one thing to watch

Taxi overcharging is the most common complaint visitors make about Sofia, and it is entirely avoidable. Legitimate taxis display their per-kilometre rate on the rear side window; the scam is a car with a rate ten times the normal one in small print, parked where tourists appear. Book through an app, or use a hotel-called cab, or look at the window sticker before you get in. From the airport, the metro removes the problem altogether.

To and from the airport

Metro Line 1 runs from Terminal 2 to the city centre in about twenty minutes for the price of a city ticket, which makes Sofia one of the easiest airport arrivals in Europe. Terminal 1, used by some low-cost carriers, is linked to Terminal 2 by a free shuttle bus. If you land late, after the metro stops, pre-book a transfer or use an app rather than the rank.

Getting out of town

Rila Monastery is the great day trip, about two hours south by road, and is easiest as an organised day or a hired car — public transport involves a change and a long wait. Boyana Church and the National History Museum are both on the city’s southern edge and can be done in an afternoon by bus or taxi. Vitosha is reached by bus and chairlift from the Simeonovo or Dragalevtsi side. Plovdiv is about two hours by road and a little more by train, and Borovets is an hour.

When to come

Sofia keeps a fairly even calendar because it is a working capital rather than a resort, but the shoulder months are plainly the best. May, June, September and early October give warm days, cafe tables out, the parks at their best and the mountain open for walking. July and August are hot but much less so than the Thracian plain, thanks to the altitude, and the city empties of locals. December is good: Christmas markets, serious museums and the ski resorts at Borovets and Vitosha within an hour. January and February are cold, often grey and sometimes snowy, which is fine for a museum trip and poor for walking. November is the one month we would not choose.

One practical note on the air: Sofia sits in a basin and in cold, still winter weather it can hold a serious smog, which is worth knowing if you have a chest condition. It passes with the wind, and it is a winter problem rather than a year-round one.

Getting in: what applies, and what does not

Bulgaria is in the European Union and, since 1 January 2025, a full member of the Schengen area — air and sea borders opened in March 2024 and the land borders followed on New Year’s Day 2025. Arriving from another Schengen country is now an internal journey with no routine passport check, which is a real change from older guidebooks. Bulgaria is also in the euro area: it adopted the euro on 1 January 2026 at a rate fixed at 1.95583 lev to the euro, and the lev stopped being legal tender on 1 February 2026, so prices here are in euro and there is no currency to change.

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen external border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave. Ninety days in any one hundred and eighty is the visa-free tourist allowance for American, Canadian, British and Australian visitors, and it is counted across the whole Schengen area rather than against Bulgaria alone.

Sofia — questions

Which area of Sofia should I stay in?

For a first visit of two or three nights, the historic centre around the Largo and the cathedral: you will walk to almost everything and the ground is level. For eating and a livelier evening, the streets around Vitosha Boulevard. For quiet with a short walk in, Oborishte and the Doctor’s Garden. For a car, the mountain or a longer stay, Lozenets. For the cheapest bed and an early train, the Central Station end — one metro stop out and noticeably rougher.

Is Sofia walkable?

The centre very much so. It is flat, the historic quarter is compact enough that the cathedral, the Roman remains, the mosque, the synagogue and the market are all within a few hundred metres of each other, and the pavements are wide. The yellow cobbles on the central boulevards are the one hazard: they are slick in the wet and uneven in places. Beyond the centre the distances grow and the metro takes over.

How do I get from Sofia airport to the city?

Take metro Line 1 from Terminal 2 into the centre: about twenty minutes, the price of an ordinary city ticket, and it avoids the taxi problem entirely. Terminal 1 has a free shuttle to Terminal 2. If you arrive after the metro has stopped for the night, pre-book a transfer or use a ride-hailing app rather than taking a car from the rank.

Are Sofia taxis a problem?

They can be, and it is the single most common complaint from visitors. The scam is a car parked where tourists gather with a per-kilometre rate many times the normal one printed small on the window. The rate is legally required to be displayed on the rear side window, so look at it before you get in, or book through an app, or have the hotel call one. Most Sofia taxis are honest and very cheap.

Is Rila Monastery a day trip from Sofia?

Yes, and it is the day trip to make. It is about two hours south by road, in a forested valley, and it is on the UNESCO World Heritage list. By public transport it involves a change and an awkward wait, so most visitors take an organised day trip or hire a car. Allow the whole day rather than half of one: the church, the museum and the walk up to the hermitage behind the monastery are all worth the time, and it is a working monastery, so dress accordingly.

Do I need cash in Sofia?

Not much. Bulgaria has used the euro since 1 January 2026, cards are taken almost everywhere a visitor goes, contactless works on the metro and most trams, and the airport metro takes a card at the gate. Keep a little cash for a market stall, a church candle or a monastery donation box. Any price you see quoted in lev is from before the changeover.
