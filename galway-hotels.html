Where to Stay in Galway

Where to Stay in Galway — Best Hotels & Neighbourhoods

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated August 2026

Galway is small in a way that changes how you should book. The whole medieval centre — Eyre Square, Shop Street, the Latin Quarter, the Spanish Arch — is about fifteen minutes end to end on foot, so almost any central hotel walks to almost everything. The real decision is not distance but noise, and after that whether you want the sea. Below are Galway’s most dependable hotels by style, each with an honest note and a Book Me link straight to Trip.com.

The Latin Quarter and Eyre Square — the middle of the noise

The old walled town runs from Eyre Square down Shop Street and High Street to Quay Street and the river at the Spanish Arch. This is the Galway people picture: buskers, painted shopfronts, trad music out of doorways, a slow crowd moving both ways. Stay here and you walk everywhere and never need a taxi — but you hear all of it. The noise is real: at weekends and right through festival season these streets are loud until one or two. Ask for a room at the back or upstairs, and pack earplugs.

Salthill — the seafront, and usually the better value

Salthill is the seaside suburb two kilometres west along the bay: a proper promenade, a long view to the hills of Clare, and the local ritual of walking to the end and kicking the wall. It is a half-hour walk from the Spanish Arch along the water, or a short bus from Eyre Square, and rooms are generally quieter and cheaper than the same standard in town — often with a sea view. The trade is the walk or ride back after dinner, and a prom that is spectacularly windy in winter.

The docks and the Spanish Arch — the middle ground

Between the two sits the strip along the harbour and Lough Atalia: the docks, the Claddagh basin across the river, and the streets around Ceannt Station. Five to ten minutes’ walk into the Latin Quarter, so you keep the convenience but sit a street back from the singing. It is also where the larger hotels are, which in Galway means lifts, car parks and swimming pools — things the medieval centre almost never has.

Out along the Connemara road — space, and a car

West through Barna towards Spiddal, or east to Oranmore, you get grounds, parking, quiet and often a better room for the money. You also need a car, or you will pay for taxis twice a day. It is the right choice if Connemara and the Burren are the point of the trip and the city is an evening out; the wrong one if you came to step out of the door into the music.

Landmark & luxury

Galway does not do grand hotels the way Dublin does. It has two genuine special-occasion addresses, and both of them sit a little apart from the medieval streets.

The Latin Quarter & Eyre Square

Walk-everywhere central. Every one of these is inside ten minutes of the Spanish Arch — and inside earshot of Shop Street.

The Hardiman

$$$ LandmarkOn Eyre SquareVictorian railway hotelLiftBeside the station

The grey cut-stone building that has stood on Eyre Square since the railway arrived, known to older visitors as the Great Southern and for some years as the Hotel Meyrick. It remains the most Galway address in Galway: high ceilings, a lobby people meet in, a lift, and the train and coach station next door — the easiest arrival in the city with luggage. It is a period building, so rooms vary a good deal in size and outlook — ask what you are getting — and the square is lively late on summer nights.

Salthill & the seafront

Two kilometres west of the Spanish Arch, along the bay. Quieter, usually cheaper, and the only part of Galway where a sea view is a realistic expectation rather than a lucky upgrade.

The Galway Bay Hotel

A large, well-run four-star directly on the Salthill promenade, with the bay and the Clare hills out of the front windows and the walk into the city starting at the door. It is the standard family choice on this stretch and earns it: a pool and leisure centre for wet afternoons, generous rooms, a lift, and parking you need not hunt for. Book a sea-facing room deliberately — the outlook is the whole reason to be out here — and expect a busy lobby, since it hosts a good many local weddings.

Budget & value

Galway is not a cheap city in summer, and in race week it is briefly one of the dearest in Ireland. These two take the guesswork out rather than the cost.

Park House Hotel

A cut-stone hotel on Forster Street, a minute from Eyre Square and the station, and one of very few genuinely central Galway hotels with its own parking — which here is worth real money. It is traditional rather than fashionable: comfortable rooms, a proper dining room, long-serving staff, and the sort of solid breakfast that sets up a day in Connemara. You are a step off the main drag, which most guests count as a benefit. Confirm the parking arrangement when you book; availability and charges change.

Family-friendly & self-catering

In Galway, family-friendly usually means a swimming pool and a car park, and both of those live outside the medieval centre.

The Ardilaun

$$$ FamilyTaylor’s HillOwn groundsLeisure clubFree parking

A country-house-style hotel in its own mature grounds on Taylor’s Hill, on the leafy road between the city centre and Salthill — one of the few places in Galway where children can be let out of the door onto grass. There is a leisure club with a pool, generous family rooms, plenty of parking and a genuinely quiet night. It is about fifteen minutes’ walk to the sea and twenty to the Latin Quarter, on gentle but real hills, so an evening in town means a stroll back or a short taxi. A favourite for local weddings at weekends.

For a week or more, or three generations together, self-catering usually beats a hotel here on price and sanity. Galway and Salthill have plenty of apartments and the Connemara villages have cottages with the sea at the end of the garden — individually let and constantly changing, so we will not name one. Out of town — Barna, Oranmore & the Connemara road

Space, quiet and value, in exchange for a car. This is where to sleep if Connemara rather than Galway city is the reason you came.

The Twelve

$$$ BoutiqueBarna villageConnemara roadNear the seaParking

A boutique hotel in Barna, ten minutes west of Salthill on the coast road towards Spiddal and Connemara, named for the Twelve Bens on the horizon beyond. It is comfortably the most characterful place to stay on this side of the city: modern, warm, informally run, with the shore a short walk away and Connemara starting at the end of the village. You will drive or taxi in for a night out — that is the deal. In return: quiet, parking and a sunset over the bay.

Getting there, parking, and using Galway as a base

The practical things that decide how well a Galway trip goes.

The train from Dublin Heuston to Ceannt Station takes roughly two and a quarter to two and three quarter hours and sets you down beside Eyre Square; book online, because the walk-up fare is the dearest. Direct coaches from Dublin city and Dublin Airport take a little longer and cost less again. Shannon is the nearer airport, about an hour and a half by road, and worth pricing from North America, though Dublin has far more flights; Galway’s airport at Carnmore has had no scheduled service for years.

Assume parking is a problem. The medieval streets were laid out for carts, much of the centre is pedestrianised or one-way, and few buildings inside the old walls have a car park, so either book somewhere that states plainly that it has parking — Salthill, the docks, the Dublin road, Barna and Oranmore mostly do — or use a multi-storey and leave the car there. What you get in return is reach: no other city in the west takes you to Connemara, the Cliffs of Moher and the Aran Islands ferry at Rossaveal from a single bed. Leave a spare day, because Atlantic weather cancels crossings.

One matter of timing. Galway Races week at Ballybrit, at the end of July, is the event most likely to wreck a casual booking: rooms for miles around go months ahead and prices roughly double. The Galway International Arts Festival and the film fleadh do the same on a smaller scale, as do bank holidays. Fixed dates mean booking early; flexible dates mean May, June or September.

Galway Hotels — common questions

Which part of Galway should I stay in?

For a first visit, inside the little triangle of Eyre Square, Shop Street and the Spanish Arch — what everyone calls the Latin Quarter. The medieval core is tiny, perhaps fifteen minutes end to end on foot, so staying in it means you walk to everything you came for and walk home again without a taxi. The trade is noise: those streets carry music and crowds until well after midnight at weekends and right through festival season. If you would rather sleep, Salthill — the seafront a half-hour walk or short bus ride west — is quieter, usually better value, and gives you a sea view instead. The docks and the Spanish Arch sit between the two and split the difference.

Is the Latin Quarter really that noisy at night?

Yes, and it is fairer to say so plainly than to let you find out at two in the morning. Shop Street, High Street, Quay Street and the lanes off them are the busking and drinking heart of the city, and the sound carries up between tall buildings and in through old sash windows. On a wet February Tuesday it is quiet; on a Saturday, in the July arts festival, or in race week, it is loud until one or two. That is the reason most people come — but it is also a reason to ask for a room at the back or upstairs, to pack earplugs, and to think about Salthill or the docks if you sleep lightly.

Do I need a car in Galway, and where would I park it?

You do not need one for Galway itself, and in the medieval streets a car is closer to a liability: the lanes are narrow, much of the centre is pedestrianised or one-way, and few old buildings ever came with a car park. Staying centrally, arrive by train or coach and hire a car only for the days you want Connemara or the Burren. If you drive the whole trip, book somewhere that states plainly that it has parking — Salthill, the docks, the Dublin road, Barna and Oranmore mostly do — or leave the car in a multi-storey for the duration, and ask what your hotel charges rather than assuming it is free.

How do I get to Galway from Dublin or Shannon?

The train from Dublin Heuston is the pleasant way: roughly two and a quarter to two and three quarter hours depending on the service, straight into Ceannt Station beside Eyre Square, a five-minute walk from most central hotels. Direct coaches also run from Dublin Airport through the day in around two and a half to three hours, saving you a crossing of Dublin with your cases. Shannon is the nearer airport, about an hour and a half by road, and worth checking from North America — though Dublin has far more flights and cheaper fares. Galway’s own airport at Carnmore has had no scheduled passenger service for years, so ignore it.

Is Galway a good base for Connemara, the Cliffs of Moher and the Aran Islands?

It is the natural one, and the strongest argument for sleeping here rather than somewhere prettier and smaller. Connemara begins immediately west: the coast road through Barna and Spiddal, and the inland road towards Oughterard, both start minutes from the centre. The Cliffs of Moher are about an hour and a half south through the Burren, a full day by hire car or on a coach tour out of Eyre Square. The Aran Islands ferry sails from Rossaveal, forty minutes west, with a connecting bus timed to the sailings — book the two together, and leave a spare day, because Atlantic weather cancels crossings.

When is the best time of year to visit Galway?

May, June and September, for the same reasons as everywhere on this coast: the longest useful daylight, the best odds of a dry afternoon, and rates below the summer peak. July and August bring the warmest weather and the biggest programme — the arts festival, the film fleadh, the races at Ballybrit — wonderful if that is why you came and expensive if it is not, since rooms go months ahead in race week and prices roughly double. Winter is cheap, dark and often beautiful, the pubs at their most local. Whatever the month, bring a proper waterproof and forget the umbrella; the Atlantic wind will fold it inside out on the Salthill prom.
