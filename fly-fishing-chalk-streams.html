Fly Fishing the English Chalk Streams — The Test, the Itchen and the Dry-Fly Rules

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Checked against the Environment Agency, GOV.UK and Natural England, 11 October 2026

The chalk streams of southern England are small rivers, most of them a cast or two wide, that rise from springs in the chalk hills of Hampshire, Wiltshire, Berkshire and Dorset. Rain soaks into the chalk, is filtered for months, and comes out cold, gin-clear and steady all year. Weed waves in the current, the insects hatch in clouds, and wild brown trout rise in water so clear you can count the spots on them. There are very few of these rivers anywhere in the world, and most of them are here.

This is also where modern fly fishing grew up. Izaak Walton fished the Itchen and is buried in Winchester Cathedral. In the late nineteenth century Frederic Halford, fishing the Test, set down the doctrine of the floating dry fly cast upstream to a rising fish, and on the Itchen G.E.M. Skues answered him with the nymph. Those arguments still shape the rules on the bank today. This page explains how the fishing actually works for a visitor — the private beats and the day rod, the mayfly, the rules of the river — with the licence and the seasons read from the Environment Agency’s own pages and dated. Where we could not confirm something, we say so.

How chalk-stream fishing works — read this first

This is the part that surprises Americans most. There is almost no public water.

Private beats, day rods and a ticket for the river

In England the right to fish a river belongs to whoever owns the bank, and on the Test and the Itchen nearly every yard of it is owned by an estate, a syndicate or a club. A river is divided into beats — a stretch of perhaps half a mile to a mile, often with its own thatched fishing hut — and each beat takes only a few rods a day. A visitor fishes by booking a day rod: a ticket for one angler on one beat for one day, bought from the estate itself, from a beat-letting agency, or from a fly shop that holds bookings. Many beats are let by the season to syndicates, so the best days, above all in the mayfly, sell out months ahead.

That means two pieces of paper. The owner’s permission — your day-rod booking — and the Environment Agency rod licence, which the law requires whoever owns the water. Neither replaces the other. A day on a famous Test beat costs a few hundred pounds a rod; quieter carriers and winter grayling days cost much less. The estates we checked publish their prices, and we quote them below.

Confirmed with GOV.UK, 11 October 2026 — the national rod fishing byelaws: “You must also have permission from the fishery owner to fish and remove fish from angling club waters”, and the same applies to private fisheries and waters on privately owned land. The rod licence page says you need a licence on private waters too.

The River Test — where the dry fly was born

About forty miles from the springs above Overton to the sea at Southampton Water, through Whitchurch, Longparish, Stockbridge and Romsey.

The upper and middle Test — Whitchurch to Stockbridge and Houghton

The Test is the most famous trout stream in the world, and its middle reaches are the reason. Around Stockbridge — a single wide high street with a fly shop, two old coaching inns and the river running under the road — the Test splits into several channels and carriers across water meadows, and the beats on either side carry names anglers have heard of for a century and a half. The water is wide by chalk-stream standards, slow, clear and weedy, and the trout lie in plain sight. Upstream, around Whitchurch and Longparish, the river is smaller and more intimate. The Bossington Estate, just below Stockbridge, calls itself “an iconic sporting estate on the River Test, the birth-place of dry fly-fishing.”

Season and what to expect: the law opens the Test to trout on 3 April, but the estates set their own seasons inside that — Bossington’s 2026 season ran from 13 April to 11 October, and Broadlands’ from 3 April to 16 October. Expect sight fishing to rising or nymphing fish with a single fly, mostly from the bank, and stocked brown trout alongside wild ones on many beats. The big event is the mayfly, from mid-May into early June; most estates charge their top price then.

Confirmed with the Environment Agency, 11 October 2026 — the Test lies in the Southern byelaw area, where “the close season for brown and rainbow trout in rivers, streams and drains is 1 November to 2 April”, and “you must not take any trout less than 23cm”. The legal season is the outer limit. Each estate sets its own opening and closing dates and its own rules — use the estate’s dates.

The lower Test — Romsey and Broadlands, and the salmon

Below Mottisfont the Test grows bigger and deeper as it runs past Romsey to the sea. The lower river is where its salmon and sea trout run, and where the Broadlands Estate fishes four and a half kilometres of water on five beats for brown trout, salmon, sea trout and grayling. The salmon of southern England are scarce now, and we would treat a Test salmon as a lucky extra rather than a reason to go.

Confirmed with the Environment Agency, 11 October 2026 — Southern area: “the close season for salmon in all waters is 3 October to 16 January” and “the close season for migratory trout in all waters is 1 November to 30 April”. Nationally: “It is illegal to catch and remove any live or dead salmon from any waters or banks before 16 June”; “Before 16 June, you can only fish for salmon with rod and line using an artificial fly or artificial lure”; and “It is illegal to sell, or keep with the intention of selling, any salmon or sea trout”. A salmon and sea trout licence holder must “submit a catch return”. The Test is not on the Environment Agency’s list of rivers where every salmon must be released all season, but the estates set their own rules, and many ask that all salmon go back. Ask before you book.

Confirmed with Broadlands Estates, 11 October 2026 — from the estate’s own rules: trout fishing on all beats with “natural imitations only”, upstream dry fly and nymph, hooks no larger than size 10; two trout of 14 inches or more may be kept per rod per day and wild fish returned; wading to knee depth only; “It is the responsibility of all anglers to be in possession of an appropriate valid EA rod licence.”

The River Itchen — Winchester’s river, and the nymph

Smaller and gentler than the Test, from the springs around New Alresford through Winchester to Southampton.

The upper Itchen — Alresford, Avington and Abbots Worthy

The Itchen gathers its springs around New Alresford, a Georgian market town of watercress beds and wide streets, and runs down a green valley past Avington and Abbots Worthy to Winchester. It is a smaller, clearer and more intimate river than the Test, and many think it the harder of the two: the trout are wild, the water is thin, and they see you before you see them. Skues fished it, which is why the Itchen is the river of the upstream nymph as much as the dry fly. It is also one of the best grayling rivers in England, which gives the Itchen a winter season most trout rivers do not have.

Season and what to expect: trout from early April (estates set their own dates), the mayfly in late May, small olives and sedges through the summer, and grayling from the autumn through the winter. At Avington Trout Fishery, outside Winchester, the Itchen carrier is fished to strict wild-fish rules beside three stocked lakes — a gentle first day for someone new to the chalk.

Confirmed with Avington Trout Fishery, 11 October 2026 — the estate’s Itchen carrier rules: “Catch and release only. All fish, whether trout, grayling or coarse to be returned.” “Dry fly or nymph, single fly, upstream.” “No wading under any circumstances.” “Maximum hook size No.10 short shank barbless only.” The Itchen, like the Test, is in the Environment Agency’s Southern byelaw area — trout close season 1 November to 2 April.

The lower Itchen and the river’s protected status

Below Winchester the Itchen widens through Shawford and Twyford towards the sea. The whole river is protected in law for its chalk-stream plants and creatures, which is one reason the estates are so careful about wading, weed and how fish are handled.

Confirmed with the JNCC and Natural England, 11 October 2026 — the River Itchen is a “Designated Special Area of Conservation (SAC)”, whose features include chalk river with water-crowfoot, the southern damselfly, the bullhead, the white-clawed crayfish and Atlantic salmon. Natural England’s plans refer to “the River Itchen SAC and River Test SSSI”.

The other chalk streams — the Avon, the Wylye, the Kennet and the Frome

Quieter rivers, often cheaper, and no less beautiful.

The Hampshire Avon and the Wylye — Salisbury’s rivers

The Hampshire Avon rises in the Vale of Pewsey and flows through Salisbury to the sea at Christchurch, gathering the Wylye, the Nadder and the Bourne on the way. Its upper reaches and the Wylye are classic small chalk streams for wild brown trout and grayling; the lower Avon is a big river, once famous for salmon and still known for very large coarse fish. These rivers fall under different byelaws from the Test and the Itchen, so the dates differ.

Confirmed with the Environment Agency, 11 October 2026 — Wessex area, which takes in the Hampshire Avon: brown trout and rainbow trout close season for “All rivers in Wessex Area”, “16 October to 31 March”; salmon on the Avon falls under “All other rivers in Wessex”, closed “1 September to 31 January”; sea trout, “1 November to 14 April”; and “Salmon and migratory trout – artificial fly only before 15 May.” The byelaw page does not name the Wylye or the Nadder separately; they sit within “all rivers in Wessex”. We could not verify a Wylye or upper Avon club whose own site would open for us.

The Kennet — Hungerford and the Berkshire mayfly

The Kennet runs east from the Marlborough Downs through Hungerford and Newbury to the Thames at Reading. Its upper reaches are a true chalk stream, famous for one of the heaviest mayfly hatches in the country. Fishing Breaks and Upstream Dry Fly both let Kennet beats, among them Barton Court near Kintbury, which Upstream Dry Fly describes as having “thick, long-running mayfly hatches”.

Confirmed with the Environment Agency, 11 October 2026 — the Kennet is in the Thames byelaw area, where the dates are different again: “the close season for brown and rainbow trout in rivers, streams and drains is 1 October to 31 March”, and “you must not take any trout that are less than 25cm”. So the Kennet’s trout season ends a month before the Test’s.

The Dorset Frome and the Piddle

West again, the Frome flows past Dorchester to Poole Harbour, with its little sister the Piddle beside it. Both are chalk streams with wild brown trout, grayling and a run of salmon and sea trout, and they are far less fished than Hampshire’s rivers.

Confirmed with the Environment Agency, 11 October 2026 — Wessex area: salmon close season on the “Frome (Dorset) and Piddle”, “1 September to the last day of February”; brown trout as for all Wessex rivers, 16 October to 31 March. We could not open a Frome fishery’s own site this time, so we list none.

The dry-fly rules, and how to behave on the bank

The law sets the seasons. The estates set the manners — and on the chalk streams the manners are the rules.

Upstream, single fly, to a fish you can see

On most Test and Itchen beats the rule is upstream dry fly, and on many, upstream dry fly or nymph. That means you walk the bank, find a trout that is feeding, and cast one fly upstream of it to drift down over it naturally. You do not “fish the water” blind, you do not cast across and downstream, and you do not use lures, streamers, weighted flies or more than one fly. Some beats allow the nymph only after a set date, or only to a fish you can see. The rules differ from beat to beat and are usually printed in the fishing hut; read them before you string up.

Confirmed with the estates, 11 October 2026 — Broadlands, on the Test: “natural imitations only”, upstream dry fly and nymph, hooks size 10 or smaller. Avington, on the Itchen carrier: “Dry fly or nymph, single fly, upstream.” Aardvark McLeod, describing a mayfly day on the Test, calls the fishing “upstream dry fly only”. These are estate rules, not the law, and they differ beat by beat. Your booking will tell you which apply.

River manners — our advice

Stay low and back from the edge. In water this clear a trout sees a figure on the skyline long before you see it. Kneel, use the cover, and approach from downstream. Wade only where the beat allows it, and on many beats that means not at all — the weed and the gravel are protected. Keep to your beat; its boundaries are usually marked by posts or described in the hut. Fill in the catch book at the end of the day; Broadlands, for one, asks every angler to record every fish caught, kept or released, at its fishing hut. Use a landing net, wet your hands, and keep a fish you will release in the water. Ask before you take a fish; where the estate allows trout to be kept, it is usually two a day, stocked fish only. And bring a dog only if your booking says so.

A note on sources — this etiquette is ours, written from general chalk-stream practice, except where we name an estate. Your beat’s own rules come first.

When to go — a month-by-month sketch

Built from the Environment Agency’s dates and the estates’ own seasons. Hatches vary from year to year; use it to choose the month, then ask the beat.

The year on the chalk

April: the Test and Itchen open to trout on 3 April under the Southern byelaws, the Kennet and the Wessex rivers on 1 April; many estates open a little later (Bossington on 13 April in 2026). Cold water, olive hatches in the middle of the day, and the cheapest rods of the season.

May into early June — the mayfly: the great hatch of the year, when the biggest trout rise to the biggest fly. Broadlands charged its peak price from 13 May to 7 June in 2026. Book months ahead.

June to August: long evenings, sedges at dusk, small flies and low clear water. The fishing is at its most technical, and the evening rise is the best of the day.

September to mid-October: terrestrials, olives again, and quieter rivers; prices drop. The Kennet closes to trout on 30 September; Wessex rivers on 15 October; the Test and Itchen by law on 31 October, though most estates stop sooner.

October to February — grayling: the chalk streams’ winter fish. Fishing Breaks says grayling “on the chalkstreams continues right through to the end of February”, and its winter days cost a fraction of the summer’s. Upstream Dry Fly gives the Southern byelaw dates for grayling and coarse fish as 16 June to 15 March.

15 March to 15 June: the national coarse-fish close season on rivers. It does not stop trout fishing.

Rods and flies — light, short and quiet

Small rivers and wary fish. Less is more.

What to bring

The rod: an 8- to 9-foot rod for a 4- or 5-weight line is right for almost every beat; on the bigger Test beats in a wind, a 5-weight. A floating line, and a long, fine leader — nine to twelve feet, tapering to 5X or 6X.

The flies: olive and pale watery dry flies in small sizes, a few sedges for the evening, mayfly patterns (duns and spent spinners) for late May, black gnats and ants for summer, and small Pheasant Tail and Gold-Ribbed Hare’s Ear nymphs where the beat allows them. Check the hook rule: several estates ask for size 10 or smaller, and some for barbless.

Everything else: clothes in greens and browns, polarising glasses (you will be looking for fish all day), a landing net, a priest only if you are allowed to keep a fish, waterproofs and knee-high boots — on many beats you will not wade at all.

A note on sources — this tackle advice is ours, written from general practice on these rivers, not from any agency. Your beat’s rules come first.

Licences and the law, plainly

One licence from the government, plus the owner’s permission. Visitors from abroad buy the same licence as everyone else.

The Environment Agency rod fishing licence

Anyone aged 13 or over who fishes for trout, salmon, sea trout or coarse fish in England needs a rod fishing licence. It costs a few pounds for a day, you buy it online in minutes before you go, and you carry it (on your phone is fine) while you fish. For the chalk streams, the trout and coarse licence covers brown trout and grayling. If there is any chance of a salmon or sea trout — on the lower Test, the Avon or the Frome — buy the salmon and sea trout licence instead.

Confirmed with GOV.UK, 11 October 2026 — trout and coarse (up to 2 rods): £7.30 for 1 day, £14.70 for 8 days, £36.80 for 12 months. Salmon and sea trout: £13.50 for 1 day, £30.50 for 8 days, £93.10 for 12 months. A 12-month concession for those aged 66 or over, or disabled: £24.50 (trout and coarse) or £62.00 (salmon and sea trout); “There are no discounted prices for a 1-day or an 8-day licence.” “Children under 13 do not need a licence”; “Junior licences are free” for ages 13 to 16. Buy online at get-fishing-licence.service.gov.uk, up to 30 days before it starts, or by phone on 0344 800 5386, Monday to Friday, 8am to 6pm. If you cannot show a valid licence when asked, “You can get a fine of up to £2,500.” The licence covers England (except the River Tweed), Wales and the Border Esk.

Seasons by area

England’s trout and salmon seasons are set by area, not nationally, and the chalk streams fall into three of them. The table in your head is simple: Test and Itchen (Southern), trout 3 April to 31 October; Kennet (Thames), trout 1 April to 30 September; Avon, Wylye and Frome (Wessex), trout 1 April to 15 October. Each estate then sets its own, shorter season inside those dates.

Confirmed with GOV.UK, 11 October 2026 — “You need to read the local byelaws for close seasons for salmon, brown trout and rainbow trout on rivers.” “You cannot fish for coarse fish and eels on rivers from the 15 March to 15 June.” Close seasons for trout in rivers: Southern, “1 November to 2 April”; Thames, “1 October to 31 March”; Wessex, “16 October to 31 March”. In the Southern area, “you must only fish for sea trout, trout or rainbow trout with an artificial fly or lure during the coarse fish close season.” The regional byelaw pages carry old “last updated” dates; we found no later change, but check the page before you fish. We found no stated close season for grayling on these pages.

Check, Clean, Dry — especially if you fly in with your own kit

Britain is free of the salmon parasite Gyrodactylus salaris, and the chalk streams are home to the native white-clawed crayfish, which an American crayfish disease can wipe out. If you bring waders, boots or nets from home, clean and dry them completely before you arrive.

Confirmed with the GB Non-Native Species Secretariat and GOV.UK, 11 October 2026 — “Check your gear after leaving the water for mud, aquatic animals or plant material.” “Clean everything thoroughly as soon as you can, paying attention to nets, waders, and areas that are damp and hard to access.” Dry everything, because “some invasive plants and animals can survive for two weeks in damp conditions.” “It’s even more important to Check Clean Dry if you’re taking your kit abroad.” GOV.UK: “Great Britain is free from GS”; equipment should be “thoroughly cleaned and disinfected between sites”, “especially important if you have used your angling or water sports equipment abroad.”

Where to base yourself

Most chalk-stream anglers drive out to the beat each morning. Choose a town in the middle of the rivers you will fish.

The towns that serve each river

For the middle Test: Stockbridge is the fishing village itself — fly shop, inns and the river under the high street. Andover, ten minutes north, has larger hotels and the railway.

For the lower Test: Romsey, a market town with an abbey, beside Broadlands.

For the Itchen: Winchester, the old capital, with its cathedral, hotels, restaurants and fast trains from London Waterloo; or New Alresford at the top of the river, quieter, with the Watercress Line steam railway.

For the Avon and the Wylye: Salisbury, with its cathedral and under two hours by train from London. For the Kennet: Hungerford. For the Frome: Dorchester.

From London: Winchester and Andover are about an hour by train, and a day trip from a London hotel is quite possible — but the evening rise is the best of the day, and the last train comes too soon. Stay out on the rivers if you can. See our London hotels guide and United Kingdom guide.

Questions anglers ask us

Can I just turn up and fish the River Test?

No. Almost all of the Test and the Itchen is privately owned, and you need the owner's permission as well as a licence. Visitors book a day rod on a beat, from the estate itself, from a beat-letting agency such as Fishing Breaks or Upstream Dry Fly, or from a fly shop such as Orvis in Stockbridge. The best beats in the mayfly sell out months ahead.

Do I need a fishing licence as a visitor from abroad?

Yes. Anyone aged 13 or over fishing for trout, salmon, sea trout or coarse fish in England needs an Environment Agency rod fishing licence, on private water too. On 11 October 2026 GOV.UK listed the trout and coarse licence at 7.30 pounds for one day, 14.70 for eight days and 36.80 for a year, and the salmon and sea trout licence at 13.50, 30.50 and 93.10. Under-13s need none, and juniors aged 13 to 16 get one free. Buy it online at get-fishing-licence.service.gov.uk before you go.

What does upstream dry fly only mean?

It means you cast a single floating fly upstream of a trout you can see feeding and let it drift down to the fish naturally. You do not fish blind, cast downstream, or use lures, streamers or more than one fly. Many beats also allow a nymph fished upstream. The rules are set by each estate, not by law, and are usually posted in the fishing hut.

When is the mayfly on the chalk streams?

Usually from mid-May into early June. Broadlands, on the lower Test, charged its peak price from 13 May to 7 June in 2026, and other estates price the same weeks highest. It varies from year to year and river to river, so ask the beat when you book.

When is the trout season on the Test and the Itchen?

The Environment Agency's close season for brown and rainbow trout in rivers in its Southern area, which includes the Test and the Itchen, is 1 November to 2 April, so the legal season is 3 April to 31 October. Each estate then sets its own, usually shorter, season. On the Kennet the trout close season is 1 October to 31 March, and on the Hampshire Avon and other Wessex rivers it is 16 October to 31 March.

Can I fish the chalk streams in winter?

Yes, for grayling. Fishing Breaks says grayling fishing on the chalkstreams continues right through to the end of February, and winter grayling days cost much less than summer trout days. Each estate sets its own grayling dates.
