When to Go to Thailand

The Best Time to Visit Thailand

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated August 2026

Short answer: November through February is the cool, dry season and the best all-round time to go — sunny days, tolerable humidity, and calm seas on the Andaman side. December and January are the pick of those months. Below is the full picture: Thailand’s three seasons month by month, the cheapest time to fly, the crucial fact that the Gulf and Andaman coasts have opposite rainy seasons, and how Songkran and Loy Krathong fall.

Thailand does not have four seasons so much as three moods: cool and dry, blisteringly hot, and gloriously wet. Timing changes the trip completely. The country you meet in January — blue skies over the Grand Palace, flat turquoise water off Krabi, cool mornings in the northern hills — is a different place from the furnace of April, or the green, rain-washed, half-price September that most travelers never see. There is one further wrinkle that catches many first-timers out: the two coasts get their rain at different times of year, so there is almost always a beach in Thailand having good weather. Here is how to find it.

The Seasons at a Glance

The quick read on what each season feels like — weather, crowds, price, and who it suits.

Cool & Dry — November to February

$$$$ PeakSunny & dryLowest humidityBook early

The northeast monsoon sweeps drier air down over the country, and Thailand shows its best face. Bangkok still runs warm — daytime highs in the low 90s Fahrenheit, around 32–33°C — but December and January are its driest months by a wide margin, averaging only a few millimetres of rain. Nights turn genuinely cool in Chiang Mai and the northern hills. The Andaman beaches are calm and clear. This is high season for good reason, which means the highest prices and the fullest hotels of the year; book flights, island transfers and rooms well in advance.

Hot Season — March to May

$$$ ShoulderVery hotSongkranNorthern haze

The heat builds relentlessly. April is the hottest month of the year in Bangkok, with average highs pushing 96°F (about 36°C) and humidity to match, and the interior can feel hotter still. Sightseeing needs an early start, a long lunch and an afternoon indoors. Two things shape these months: Songkran, the Thai New Year water festival on 13–15 April, which is a joy but packs the whole country onto the roads; and the smoke haze in the far north, caused largely by agricultural burning, which typically runs from around January or February into April and is usually at its worst in March. Prices ease from the December peak, especially in May.

Rainy Season — roughly Mid-May to October

$ Low seasonGreen & lushAfternoon downpoursBest value

The southwest monsoon arrives around mid-May and the country turns emerald. Do not picture British drizzle: Thai rain usually comes as a dramatic hour-long downpour in the afternoon, often with clear spells either side. September and October are the wettest months in Bangkok, and the Andaman coast takes its heaviest rain in the same stretch. In exchange you get the lowest prices of the year, thin crowds at the temples, waterfalls in full flow and rice fields at their greenest. The genuine drawbacks are humidity, occasional flooding after heavy rain, and boat crossings to the smaller islands being cancelled in rough seas.

Month by Month

The concise version — weather, crowds, and what each month does best.

January

$$$$ PeakDriestCool nights up north

Arguably the finest month of the year. Bangkok is about as dry as it ever gets, the Andaman sea is glassy, and Chiang Mai mornings are cool enough for a light jacket. Everything is open, everything is busy, and prices sit at or near their annual high. Book well ahead, particularly for the islands, and expect company at the headline sights.

February

$$$$ PeakDry & warmHaze begins north

Still excellent: dry, sunny and comfortable, with the heat starting to creep upward by month’s end. The Andaman coast is at its best. In the far north, the burning-season haze often begins to build during February, so if crisp mountain views are the point of your trip, early in the month is safer than late.

March

$$$ ShoulderHeat climbingHaze peak north

The hot season takes hold, with Bangkok pushing into the mid-90s Fahrenheit and the beaches ideal for swimming and lazing. Prices begin to soften from the peak. The northern haze is typically at its worst this month, so consider swapping Chiang Mai for the coast if air quality concerns you.

April

$$$ Songkran spikeHottest monthWater festival

The hottest month, and the most exuberant. Songkran falls on 13–15 April every year, turning the streets into a nationwide water fight and filling every train, plane and guesthouse. It is a wonderful thing to see once. If crowds and 96°F heat are not your idea of a holiday, plan around mid-April rather than through it.

May

$$ ValueMonsoon arrivesGulf still good

The southwest monsoon usually arrives around mid-month, breaking the heat with the first heavy showers and dropping prices noticeably. The Andaman coast starts to get lively seas. The Gulf islands, meanwhile, are heading into their better half of the year — a useful piece of timing if you want a May beach.

June

$$ ValueGreen & warmGulf sweet spot

Rainy but far from washed out, with plenty of sun between the downpours and everything lush. Low-season pricing is in full effect in Bangkok and on the Andaman side. This is one of the best months for Koh Samui and the Gulf islands, which stay comparatively dry while the west coast gets wet.

July

$$ ValueWarm rainFamily travel

Similar to June: hot, humid and green, with showers that come and go. Western school holidays lift demand a little on the popular islands, but rates stay well below the winter peak. A good month to combine Bangkok, the north and a Gulf-coast beach without paying high-season prices.

August

$$ ValueWet & lushWaterfalls

Properly wet in most of the country, and beautiful for it — jungle at its greenest, waterfalls thundering, and few people at the temples. The Gulf islands remain the better beach bet. Pack quick-drying clothes and a small umbrella, and treat rain as a scheduling detail rather than a disaster.

September

$ CheapestWettest in BangkokFewest visitors

Statistically the wettest month in Bangkok and one of the wettest on the Andaman coast, and consequently the cheapest time to travel. Some smaller island resorts and dive operators close for the season. For a rain-tolerant traveler on a budget, the value and the emptiness are remarkable.

October

$ Low, then risingRain easing westWettest on the Gulf

A month of transition. The Andaman side begins to dry out as the month goes on, and by late October the first high-season arrivals appear. The Gulf coast does the opposite: October is when Koh Samui and its neighbours take their heaviest rain. Prices are still low, and it can be a shrewd month for Bangkok and the north.

November

$$$ RisingCool season startsLoy Krathong

The cool, dry season opens, and Bangkok and the Andaman coast turn lovely while rates have not yet reached their December peak — one of the best value-for-weather windows of the year. Loy Krathong, the festival of floating lanterns, falls on the November full moon; in 2026 that is 24 November, with Chiang Mai’s Yi Peng lantern release on the same nights. The Gulf islands are still wet.

December

$$$$ HighestDriest & sunniestHoliday peak

Superb weather and the busiest, priciest fortnight of the year around Christmas and New Year. Bangkok is dry and comparatively comfortable, the Andaman islands are at their postcard best, and the north is cool and clear. Book months ahead for the holiday period, or come in early December for nearly the same weather at gentler rates.

Two Coasts, Two Monsoons

The single most useful thing to know about timing a Thai beach holiday.

Andaman coast — Phuket, Krabi, Khao Lak, Phi Phi

Best: Dec–MarWettest: Sep–OctCalmest seas in winter

The west-facing Andaman coast takes the full force of the southwest monsoon from roughly May to October, with the heaviest rain and roughest seas around September and October. From December to March the picture flips: rainfall drops away sharply, the sea flattens, and the limestone islands look exactly as they do in the brochures. Long-tail boat trips to Phi Phi and the Similans are far more reliable in those months; in the wet season crossings are sometimes cancelled outright.

Gulf coast — Koh Samui, Koh Phangan, Koh Tao

Best: Jun–Sep & late Jan–AprWettest: Oct–NovOpposite pattern

The Gulf islands sit in the rain shadow of the peninsula for much of the southwest monsoon, so their driest, sunniest stretch runs through the middle of the year, roughly June to September, when the Andaman coast is at its wettest. Their own wet season comes later, driven by the northeast monsoon: October and November are the heaviest months, sometimes running into December. In practice this means a July beach holiday belongs on the Gulf side and a January one on the Andaman side.

The Cheapest Time to Go

If price is your deciding factor, the pattern is clear and consistent.

The green season — May to October, with September the lowest

$ Rock bottomFlights & hotelsRain trade-off

Long-haul airfares and hotel rates fall through the rainy months from May to October, and bottom out around September, when rain in Bangkok and on the Andaman coast is at its heaviest. Resorts that command eye-watering rates in January can be a fraction of the price, and the temples and markets are blessedly uncrowded. Because Thai rain tends to fall hard and briefly rather than all day, plenty of seasoned travelers now prefer these months on purpose.

Want good weather and value? Aim for the shoulder edges: early November, before the December surge, and May, just as the monsoon is beginning. Both give you largely dry days at well under peak rates. As a rule, avoid the Christmas–New Year fortnight and mid-April around Songkran, when domestic demand pushes everything up, and remember that fares from the United States are usually lowest when you book several months out and fly midweek.

Festivals, Holidays & Crowds

Thailand’s busy weeks are predictable, which makes them easy to plan around.

The dates that move the whole country

Songkran 13–15 AprLoy Krathong 24 Nov 2026Christmas & New YearLunar New Year

Songkran, the Thai New Year, is a public holiday on 13–15 April every year, with celebrations frequently spilling over extra days in Chiang Mai and other cities. Expect city-wide water fights, sold-out transport and many businesses shut for several days. Loy Krathong follows the lunar calendar — the full moon of the twelfth Thai lunar month — so its date shifts annually; in 2026 it falls on 24 November, coinciding with Chiang Mai’s Yi Peng lantern festival, which is spectacular and books out months ahead. The Christmas and New Year fortnight is the priciest window of all on the islands, and Chinese New Year in late January or February brings a further regional surge; both dates are worth checking before you fix your trip.

To have the great sights closer to yourself, travel in the shoulder weeks — early November, May and late October — and start early in the day: Bangkok’s Grand Palace and Wat Pho, and the temples of Chiang Mai, are calmest at opening. Best Time by Region

Thailand runs a long way north to south — the ideal month shifts depending on where you are headed.

Bangkok & the central plains

Best: Dec–FebHottest: AprWettest: Sep–Oct

Bangkok is warm year-round, so the deciding factors are humidity and rain. December to mid-February is the comfortable window, with the lowest rainfall and the least oppressive air — the right time for temple-hopping, river boats and the day trip to Ayutthaya. April is punishing, and September and October bring the heaviest downpours and occasional street flooding. See where to stay in our Bangkok guide.

Chiang Mai & the north

Best: Nov–JanCoolest nightsAvoid: Feb–Apr haze

The northern hills are the coolest corner of Thailand, with night-time temperatures in the cool season dropping to the high 50s Fahrenheit (around 14–15°C) — bring a fleece for early starts and mountain viewpoints. November to January is the prime window, taking in Loy Krathong and Yi Peng and offering clear air and comfortable trekking. The caution is the agricultural burning season, roughly January or February into April and generally worst in March, which can leave the valley hazy for weeks. See our Chiang Mai guide.

Frequently Asked Questions

The questions travelers ask most when timing a trip to Thailand.

What is the best month to visit Thailand?

November through February is the sweet spot, and December and January are the pick of the bunch. The northeast monsoon brings dry, sunny days and the lowest humidity of the year, and Bangkok averages only a few millimetres of rain in December and January. It is the one window that suits Bangkok, the northern hills and the Andaman beaches at Phuket and Krabi all at once. It is also high season, so flights, hotels and island transfers should be booked well ahead.

What is the cheapest time to visit Thailand?

The rainy months from roughly May to October are the cheapest, with September and October the lowest of all on the Andaman side. Hotels discount heavily, airfares soften and the countryside is green and uncrowded. Rain in Thailand usually arrives as heavy afternoon downpours rather than all-day drizzle, so a wet-season trip is far more workable than the word monsoon suggests, though boat crossings to the smaller islands can be cancelled in rough weather.

Which Thai coast should I choose in each season?

The two Thai coasts have opposite rain patterns, which is the single most useful thing to know when planning. The Andaman coast (Phuket, Krabi, Khao Lak and the Phi Phi islands) is at its best from December to March and wettest around September and October. The Gulf coast (Koh Samui, Koh Phangan and Koh Tao) stays comparatively dry through the middle of the year and takes its heaviest rain in October and November, sometimes running into December. If you are travelling in June, July or August, lean toward the Gulf; from December to March, lean toward the Andaman.

When is Songkran, and should I plan around it?

Songkran, the Thai New Year, is a public holiday on 13 to 15 April every year, and the celebrations often spill over a few extra days in Chiang Mai and other cities. It is joyous, extremely wet and very crowded, with water fights in the street, and you will be soaked whether or not you join in. Domestic flights, trains and hotels sell out well in advance and many businesses close for several days, so book early and either commit to it wholeheartedly or avoid mid-April entirely.

When is Loy Krathong, and what about the haze in northern Thailand?

Loy Krathong falls on the full moon of the twelfth Thai lunar month, so the date moves each year, and in 2026 it lands on 24 November, with the Yi Peng lantern festival in Chiang Mai on the same nights. Separately, northern Thailand has a smoke haze season caused largely by agricultural burning, running roughly from January or February into April and typically at its worst in March. If clear mountain air matters to you, favour November to January for Chiang Mai and the north.
