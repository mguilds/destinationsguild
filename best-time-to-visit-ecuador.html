Ecuador — Two Seasons in the Mountains, Two More in the Islands

Best Time to Visit Ecuador — Month by Month

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

On the equator the year does not work the way it does at home. Quito’s temperature is almost the same in January as in July, because at 2,850 metres the altitude decides it and the calendar only decides the rain. Meanwhile the Galápagos, two hours west, run two quite different seasons of their own, and the Amazon runs none at all. Here is the whole country, month by month, and which window suits which trip.

The short answer

Ecuador straddles the equator, so it has no summer and no winter. What it has is a dry season from June to September in the Andes, a shorter dry spell around December and January, and an afternoon-rain season from February to May. The temperature in Quito is set by its altitude and barely moves all year. The Galápagos run on their own calendar entirely: warm and wet from December to May, with calmer seas and better snorkelling, and cool and dry from June to November, with rougher crossings and more wildlife activity. The Amazon is wet throughout and wettest from March to July.

For a trip that combines the mountains and the islands, late June to early September is the single safest window. For warm water and calm boats, go February to April and accept a wet afternoon in Quito.

Month by month

January $$

A short dry window in the highlands, and often a bright, clear month in Quito between two wetter ones. The Galápagos are into their warm season: hot sun, short heavy showers, flat seas and the best water temperatures of the year. Prices spike hard over New Year and then fall away. Green hills, warm islands, and the Amazon rivers rising.

February $$$

Carnival pushes mainland prices and fills hotels for its week, so check the dates before you book. In the mountains the rains have settled into their pattern — a clear morning and a solid afternoon downpour. In the islands this is the warmest, calmest stretch and the pick of the year for snorkelling, with green landscapes and nesting marine iguanas.

March $$

The wettest month in much of the highlands, and the one to avoid if your trip depends on volcano views. The compensation is that everything is green and the cities are quiet. Galápagos conditions stay excellent in the water. The Amazon is entering its highest-water months, which is good for canoe access to flooded forest and bad for walking trails.

April $

Wet in the Andes, with Easter week the one expensive exception — a serious religious occasion in Quito and Cuenca, and worth seeing if you are there for it. Outside those days, late April is one of the best-value stretches of the year on the mainland. The islands are still warm and calm, with the green season at its lushest.

May $

The rains thin out through the month and the dry season is visible by the end of it. A quiet, cheap, pleasant month in Quito and Cuenca with a reasonable chance of clear mornings. In the Galápagos the water starts to cool and the seas to pick up; it is the handover month between the two island seasons and a good compromise.

June $$

The highland dry season arrives properly. Clear mornings, views of Cotopaxi from the road, and comfortable walking weather. Inti Raymi, the Andean solstice festival, falls late in the month and is a real event in the Otavalo valley. In the islands the cool season begins: cooler air, cooler water, rougher crossings and the first of the big seabird activity.

July $$$

Peak mainland month and priced for it. The driest, clearest weather of the year in the Andes, with everything open and the mountain roads at their best. The Galápagos are cool and productive — strong currents, plenty of feeding, blue-footed boobies displaying — and a wetsuit is no longer optional for most people. Book early for both.

August $$$

As good as July and just as busy, with the added note that August is the windiest month in the islands and the crossings show it. On the mainland this is the best stretch for the high passes and the Quilotoa and Cotopaxi days. If you are prone to seasickness, this is the month to choose a land-based island trip over a small boat.

September $$

The last of the dry season and, for our money, the best value in the good half of the year. Mainland crowds thin from mid-month while the weather holds. In the islands it is cool, green-poor and wildlife-rich, with sea lion pups about and the water at its coldest. One note for this year specifically: the 60-day state of emergency recorded by the Foreign Office was declared on 15 September 2026 and brought overnight curfews to four coastal provinces for a fortnight — none of which covered the Andes, the Amazon or the islands.

October $$

A transition month, and an underrated one. Rain returns to the highlands gradually rather than all at once, so mornings are often still clear and the afternoon showers are brief. The islands stay cool and busy with wildlife. Prices are moderate, hotels have room, and the light in the mountains is at its best.

November $

Quiet and good value, with more afternoon rain in the mountains as the month goes on. The islands begin to warm towards the end of it. The first days of November carry two public holidays back to back — the Day of the Dead and Cuenca’s own independence day on the third — which fills Cuenca completely and is either the reason to be there or the reason not to be.

December $$

Clear spells return to the highlands in the first half and the islands move into their warm, calm season. Then the last ten days of the month become the most expensive and most heavily booked of the year, all over the country. Early December is one of the best-kept secrets in the Ecuadorian calendar; late December is the one time we would tell you to book months out.

By region

The Andes — Quito, Cuenca, the volcanoes

Altitude, not season, sets the temperature. Quito sits at about 2,850 metres and Cuenca at about 2,560, and both run mild days and genuinely cold nights all year round — you want a warm layer in every month, and the sun at this altitude burns faster than the air temperature suggests. What the calendar changes is rain and visibility. June to September is the dry, clear window and the one to pick if the volcanoes matter to you. February to May delivers bright mornings and heavy afternoons. Nothing in the mountains is washed out by rain; plans just move to the morning.

The Galápagos

Two seasons, both worth having. December to May is the warm, wet season: sea temperatures at their highest, seas at their calmest, the best snorkelling of the year, green islands and nesting turtles and iguanas. June to November is the cool, dry season, when the Humboldt current brings cold water and with it food — more feeding frenzies, more seabird courtship, better big-animal diving — at the cost of rougher crossings and water that wants a wetsuit. Choose by what you want to see and by how you feel about small boats in a swell.

The Amazon

Wet, warm and humid every month, with the rivers highest from roughly March to July. High water means motor canoes can get further into flooded forest and the birding from the boat is excellent; low water, roughly August to February, means better walking trails and easier wildlife spotting along the banks. There is no dry season in any meaningful sense, so pack for rain and stop worrying about it.

The coast

January to April is the warm, wet, sunny season on the Pacific coast and May to December the cooler, grey one. We are leaving it at that, because the Foreign Office currently advises against all but essential travel to the whole Coastal Region and we are not writing a holiday calendar for it. The honest position, and the one exception for airside transit at Guayaquil airport, is set out on our Ecuador guide.

Dates that fill the country

Christmas and New Year — the busiest and most expensive stretch of the year, nationwide. Carnival, in February, moves with the calendar and books out highland hotels for its long weekend. Easter week fills Quito and Cuenca, and the processions are a genuine reason to come rather than an inconvenience. Inti Raymi, around the June solstice, is the Andean festival to plan for in the Otavalo valley. The first three days of November — the Day of the Dead and Cuenca’s independence day — fill Cuenca entirely. And 10 August, Quito’s own national date, is a busy weekend in the capital.

The Galápagos barely follow any of this. Island availability is set by how many boats are running and how far ahead the good ones sell, which is further than most people expect in July, August and the last two weeks of December.

Ecuador seasons — questions

What is the best month to visit Ecuador?

If you are doing the Andes and the Galapagos in one trip, late June through early September is the safest single answer: the mountains are at their driest and clearest, and the islands are in their cool, dry, wildlife-heavy season. If you care most about warm water and calm crossings in the Galapagos, go between February and April instead and accept afternoon rain in Quito and Cuenca. There is no month that is bad everywhere, which is the useful thing about a country on the equator.

Is there a rainy season in Quito?

Yes, though not the kind that cancels a day. February to May is the wet stretch and the pattern is typically a bright morning and a heavy afternoon shower rather than all-day rain, so plans get rearranged rather than lost. June to September is the dry season, with clearer views of the volcanoes. Quito's temperature barely moves across the year - days in the high teens Celsius, nights close to single figures - because at 2,850 metres on the equator, altitude sets the temperature and the calendar only sets the rain.

When is the best time for the Galapagos?

Both seasons are good and they are good for different reasons. December to May is the warm, wet season: air and sea are warmer, the sea is calmer, snorkelling is at its best, and the islands are green. June to November is the cool, dry season: the Humboldt current brings cold, nutrient-rich water, so there is more feeding activity, more seabird nesting and better big-animal diving, but the crossings are rougher and the water wants a wetsuit. If seasickness is a consideration, go in the warm season.

Does the weather change the price?

Less than in most countries, and mostly around holidays rather than seasons. The expensive weeks are Christmas and New Year, Carnival in February, Easter week, and the July-August stretch when North American and European school holidays overlap with the Andean dry season. The Galapagos operate their own market, where the boat matters far more than the month. The quietest and cheapest mainland weeks are in late April, May and early November.

What about El Nino?

It is worth knowing about this year. The UK Foreign Office records that the Ecuadorian authorities have issued a red alert ahead of the forecast 2026-2027 El Nino event, which may bring heavy rain, flooding and landslides. In Ecuador an El Nino year typically means a wetter, warmer coast, more landslides on mountain roads, and warmer, less productive water in the Galapagos, which can make the diving quieter even as the swimming gets pleasanter. None of that is a reason not to come. It is a reason to leave slack in a driving itinerary, to avoid mountain roads after dark, and to buy insurance that covers disruption.
