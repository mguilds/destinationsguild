Best Time to Visit Romania

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Romania is a continental country with mountains down the middle of it, a hot plain at either end and a short, intense season on the Black Sea, so the right month depends a good deal on which Romania you are coming for. This page sets out the year as it actually behaves — what each month gives you and takes away, when the high mountain roads open, when the sea is warm, and the handful of dates that will leave you without a room.

The short answer

May, June and September are the best months in Romania, and September is the one we would pick if we could only go once: warm days, cool nights, the Carpathians turning, the Transylvanian squares full and the crowds of August gone. Late June, July and August are the only weeks the Black Sea really works and the only reliable window for the high mountain roads, at the cost of heat on the plains and the year's biggest crowds at Bran and in Brasov. December to March is ski season, the cheapest time for the cities, and the best time to see Sibiu and Brasov under snow. April and October are the gambles — often lovely, sometimes wet, and the months when the high passes are shut at one end or closing at the other.

Romania month by month

January and February

Winter proper. Bucharest and the southern plain go below freezing with grey days and intermittent snow; the Transylvanian plateau is colder and prettier, and Sibiu and Brasov under snow are worth a cold trip on their own. This is the heart of the ski season at Poiana Brasov and Sinaia, and it is the cheapest time of year to sleep in any Romanian city. The high mountain roads are shut, the Danube Delta is bleak and largely closed to visitors, and the Black Sea is out of the question. A good time for cities, museums, markets and snow; a poor one for the countryside.

March

The turn, and not always a graceful one. Early March is still winter and late March can be genuinely spring, with the plains greening and the first warm afternoons on a terrace. The mountains are still in snow and the villages are at their muddiest, which is the real cost of this month in the countryside. Prices are at their lowest. Martisor on 1 March, when little red-and-white tokens are given to women and girls, is the first proper sign of the year and is charming to be present for.

April

A lovely month in the lowlands and an unreliable one higher up. The Transylvanian villages and the Wallachian foothills are at their greenest, the monasteries and citadels are quiet, and Bucharest's parks come into leaf. Expect rain, and expect the mountains to be out of bounds above the treeline. Orthodox Easter often falls in April and sometimes in early May; it is the biggest family holiday of the year, it moves, and it fills hotels and empties city restaurants, so check the date before fixing anything around it.

May

The month the year opens properly, and our pick for a first trip that is not about the beach. Warm without heat, long days, the countryside at its best, wildflowers in the Carpathian meadows and the Saxon villages as good as they get. Everything is open, nothing is crowded, and prices are still moderate. The high sections of the Transfagarasan and the Transalpina are normally still closed, which is the one thing May cannot give you.

June

The best all-round month in the country. Hot but not fierce on the plains, perfect on the Transylvanian plateau, the mountain walking open and the Black Sea warming up by the end of it. The high passes usually open somewhere in late June, though the date is set each year by the snow and is announced rather than scheduled. Sibiu's international theatre festival takes over the town for about ten days and is excellent, so book early or avoid those dates deliberately.

July

Hot, busy and the start of real summer. Bucharest and Timisoara run well into the thirties and the middle of the day on the plains is for shade rather than sightseeing — worth knowing before planning a citadel climb at noon. The Black Sea season is in full swing at Mamaia, the mountain roads are open and at their most spectacular, and Bran, Brasov and Peles are at their most crowded. The mountains and the plateau are the comfortable places to be.

August

The hottest month and the most crowded, and the one we would steer around if the choice is free. The coast is at capacity, the castle circuit is at its busiest, and the big summer music festivals in Cluj-Napoca sell the city out months ahead and move prices sharply — check their dates before you book anything there. Against all that: the delta is warm and alive, the high roads are reliably open, and the mountain villages are in the middle of haymaking, which is the Maramures everyone wants to see. Book ahead and start early.

September

The best month in Romania, and by a clear margin if you are not swimming. The heat breaks on the plains, the plateau is warm by day and cool at night, the crowds go home with the school term, the sea stays swimmable through the first half, the high roads are still open and the light on the Saxon squares is at its best. Prices ease. The George Enescu classical festival fills Bucharest when it runs, which is every second year, so check whether your dates collide with it or coincide happily.

October

Beautiful and increasingly chancy. The first half is often the loveliest fortnight of the autumn — the Carpathian beech forests turning, the vineyards harvesting, the monasteries and villages empty — and the second half can be cold and wet. The high sections of the Transfagarasan and the Transalpina close during the month, usually toward the end but sometimes earlier after an early snowfall. Cheap, quiet and well worth it if you can take the weather as it comes.

November and December

November is the bleakest month of the Romanian year: grey, damp, the trees bare, the mountains not yet in proper snow and the cheapest rooms of the twelve. December turns it round. The Christmas markets in Sibiu, Brasov, Bucharest and Timisoara run through the month and the first two are genuinely good; 1 December is the national day and fills hotels; the ski resorts open as the snow arrives; and the Transylvanian towns under their first snow are the picture people come back for. Book December well ahead and leave November to the bargain-hunters.

The weather, and what it does to a trip

Three climates in one country

Romania is continental, not Mediterranean, and the difference between its regions is larger than visitors expect. The southern plain around Bucharest and the western plain around Timisoara are the hot and cold extremes — well above thirty degrees in July and August, below freezing in January, with Timisoara regularly the hottest place in the country. The Transylvanian plateau, at four to six hundred metres inside the mountains, runs several degrees cooler all year and is the comfortable place to be in high summer. The Carpathians are alpine: snow on the tops into June, and weather that changes within an hour.

The Black Sea season is short

Romania's coast is about two hundred and forty kilometres of it, and its beach season runs roughly from the middle of June to the middle of September, peaking hard in late July and August. Outside that window the resorts at Mamaia largely shut, and Constanta becomes a perfectly good off-season city for its archaeology rather than a seaside one. The sea is shallow, calm and warms quickly, which is why the season is so concentrated.

The mountain roads set your calendar

If the Transfagarasan or the Transalpina is the reason for your trip, the trip has to be a summer one. The high sections are closed by snow for a large part of the year and typically open somewhere around late June or July and close again in October, with the dates decided each year by conditions and announced by the roads authority rather than fixed in advance. Check the current status in the days before you drive, not from a blog post, and keep a valley alternative in your pocket.

Rain, snow and what it does to a village road

There is no dry season as such; rainfall is spread through the year with a late-spring maximum, and thunderstorms are common on summer afternoons in the mountains. What matters more to a traveller is the surface: unpaved village roads in Maramures, the Apuseni and the deep Transylvanian countryside turn to mud after rain and in the spring thaw, and a small hire car will struggle. In winter the mountain passes and some village roads simply close. Plan countryside driving for June to October.

Dates worth planning around

Martisor — 1 March

The start of spring, marked all over the country by giving small red-and-white woven tokens, worn pinned to a coat through the first weeks of March. It is not a public holiday and it does not affect availability, but it is one of the pleasantest things to be in Romania for and the markets selling the trinkets appear in every town.

Orthodox Easter — a moving date

The largest family holiday of the Romanian year, usually falling in April and sometimes in early May, and on a different date from Western Easter in most years. Expect packed trains and intercity buses in the days either side, many city restaurants closed on the Sunday, hotels full in the countryside and a genuinely special atmosphere at the midnight services. Look up the date for your year before you plan anything around it.

1 December — the national day

Great Union Day, marking the 1918 union, with the main military parade in Bucharest and the largest celebrations at Alba Iulia, where the union was proclaimed. It is a public holiday, it falls at the start of the Christmas market season, and it is the single date most likely to leave you without a room in Alba Iulia or central Bucharest. 24 January, Little Union Day, is its quieter cousin.

The Sibiu and Cluj festival weeks

Sibiu's international theatre festival takes over the whole town for about ten days in June and is one of the largest performing-arts events in Europe. Cluj-Napoca hosts two very large summer music festivals that sell the city out months in advance and move room prices a long way. Both towns are small relative to their festivals. Look the dates up early and then decide whether you are going for them or around them.

The George Enescu Festival, every second year

Bucharest's classical music festival is one of the serious ones, bringing major international orchestras to the Romanian Athenaeum and the Palace Hall across several weeks of late summer and early autumn. It runs every two years rather than annually, so check whether it falls in your year; if it does, it is a reason to put Bucharest at the end of the trip rather than the beginning.

Timisoara in mid-December

Between 16 and 22 December the city marks the days in 1989 when the Romanian revolution began here, with commemorations around Piata Victoriei and the Memorial Museum. It is a sober, well-attended anniversary rather than a festival, and being in Timisoara for it is a different experience from visiting in June.

What it costs, and when

Romania is among the better-value countries in the European Union, and the seasonal pattern is clear enough to plan around. November, and January to March outside the ski resorts, are the cheapest weeks of the year in the cities — Bucharest in February is remarkably inexpensive for a European capital. May, early June and late September into October are moderate and are the best value for what you actually get. Mid-July and August are the dearest on the Black Sea coast and in the castle towns, and December is dear in Sibiu and Brasov for the markets and in the ski resorts. The sharpest spikes are not seasonal at all but event-driven: the Sibiu theatre festival in June, the Cluj music festivals in summer, 1 December in Alba Iulia and Bucharest, and Orthodox Easter in the countryside. Those weeks can double a room rate in a town that is otherwise cheap, which is the single most useful thing to check before booking. We do not print figures, because they move; the pattern does not.

Fishing here

The fishing calendar is not the tourist one. Carpathian trout and grayling run to a short, strictly dated season on rivers managed by local angling associations, and the best water is high and cold in late spring and summer. The Danube Delta fishes through the warm months for pike, perch, zander, carp and catfish, and sits inside a biosphere reserve where permits come from the reserve authority rather than over a counter. On the Danube itself, one thing to be plain about: sturgeon are protected and sturgeon fishing in the Romanian Danube has been under moratorium for years, so anyone offering it is offering something they should not. Licences, seasons and delta permits are arranged locally and all change, so set a fishing trip up with a guide on the ground rather than from abroad. Getting in: what applies, and what does not

Romania is a member of the European Union, which it joined in 2007, and since 1 January 2025 it has been a full member of the Schengen area — air and sea borders opened in March 2024 and the land borders followed on New Year’s Day 2025. That is a genuine change from the advice in older guidebooks: arriving from another Schengen country is now an internal journey with no routine passport check, and the rules below are the ones that apply when you first enter the area.

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen external border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave. United States, Canadian, British and Australian citizens may visit as tourists without a visa for up to ninety days in any one hundred and eighty, counted across the whole Schengen area rather than against Romania alone — so a longer European trip uses the same allowance.

One thing worth knowing: Romania is in the European Union but not in the euro area. The currency is the Romanian leu, and adopting the euro remains a stated intention with no firm date.

Romania’s seasons — questions

What is the single best month to visit Romania?

September, for most trips. The heat has broken on the plains, the Transylvanian plateau is warm by day and cool at night, the summer crowds have gone with the school term, the sea is still swimmable in the first half and the high mountain roads are still open. May and June are very close behind, with June the better month if you want the passes and the walking and May the better one for the countryside and the prices.

When is the Transfagarasan open?

It is a summer road. The high central section over the Fagaras range is closed by snow for much of the year and usually opens somewhere around late June or July, closing again in October, with the exact dates decided each year by conditions and announced by the roads authority. The lower approaches stay open longer. Never plan a date around a figure from an old blog post: check the current status in the days before you drive, and keep the Transalpina or a valley route as a fallback.

When can I swim in the Black Sea?

Roughly mid-June to mid-September, with late July and August the reliable weeks. The Romanian coast is shallow and calm, so it warms up quickly and cools down quickly, and the season is short and intense. Outside it the resorts at Mamaia largely close down, though Constanta itself remains a worthwhile city for its Greek and Roman history whatever the month.

Is winter a bad time to come?

Not at all, as long as you come for the right things. Sibiu, Brasov and Bucharest work very well in winter, they are at their cheapest, the Christmas markets in December are good and the Transylvanian towns under snow are memorably beautiful. Poiana Brasov and Sinaia have the skiing. What winter rules out is the countryside, the mountain roads, the delta and the coast, so build the trip around towns rather than driving.

Which month should I avoid?

November, if you want the country to look its best — it is grey, bare, muddy and between seasons, with neither autumn colour nor snow. August is the other one to think twice about, not because it is unpleasant but because it is hot on the plains and the castle towns and the coast are at their most crowded and their dearest. If August is your only option, base yourself on the plateau or in the mountains rather than at the seaside.

Do I need to plan around Orthodox Easter?

You need to know when it is. It usually falls in April, sometimes early May, and in most years it is on a different date from Western Easter. Trains and buses fill in the days either side, many city restaurants shut on the Sunday, and village accommodation books out as families come home. It is a wonderful time to be in rural Romania if you have arranged it, and an awkward one if you have not.

How long does a Romanian road journey actually take?

Longer than the map suggests. The motorway network is incomplete, so a good deal of cross-country driving is on two-lane national roads behind lorries, and the mountain crossings are slow by their nature. Allow roughly half again on any estimate your phone gives for a long leg, more in the mountains, and prefer the train for Bucharest to Brasov or Sibiu. It is the single commonest planning mistake visitors make here.
