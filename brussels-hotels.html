Brussels

Where to Stay in Brussels

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Brussels is two cities stacked on a hill. The Lower Town, around the Grand-Place, is the old mercantile heart — narrow, gilded, crowded and on foot. The Upper Town above it holds the palace, the courts and most of the museums, and behind that again sits the European Quarter, which fills on weekday mornings and empties on Friday afternoons. Add the nineteenth-century districts to the south, where most of the Art Nouveau and most of the good eating are, and you have a capital that rewards choosing your neighbourhood carefully. It is also the one Belgian city where hotel prices often fall at the weekend, because its hotels are built for officials and not for tourists.

The parts of Brussels a visitor actually chooses between, and who each one suits.

Grand-Place and the Îlot Sacré — the old centre

Most centralEverything on footBusiest at night

The Grand-Place is one of the great enclosed squares in Europe and the streets around it are the postcard Brussels: guild houses, chocolate windows, waffle stands and the Manneken Pis two minutes away. Staying inside this ring means you walk to almost everything in the Lower Town and you are ten minutes from Brussels-Central station. The trade-off is noise and tourist pricing. The lanes of the Îlot Sacré, particularly Rue des Bouchers, are lined with restaurants whose staff work the pavement, and they are not where locals eat. Hotels here run from grand old names on the square to small modern places in the side streets; ask for a room off the street if you are a light sleeper.

The Sablon and the Upper Town — museums and antiques

QuieterMuseum quarterGood for walking

Climb the hill and the city changes register. The Sablon is antique shops, chocolatiers and a Saturday and Sunday market beside a fifteenth-century church; a few minutes along are the Royal Museums of Fine Arts and the Magritte Museum, the Musical Instruments Museum in its Art Nouveau department store, and the view from the Place Poelaert. This is the choice if you are coming for the museums and want to sleep somewhere calmer than the Lower Town without being far from it. It is a genuine hill — there are lifts and a funicular-style escalator in places, but allow for the climb if stairs are hard work.

The European Quarter and Schuman — weekday Brussels

Business hotelsWeekend bargainsQuiet after Friday

The quarter around Rond-point Schuman and Place du Luxembourg is where the Commission, the Council and the Parliament sit, and its hotels are built for people on expenses. That has one real advantage for a visitor: from Friday afternoon to Sunday the area empties and the same rooms are often markedly cheaper than an equivalent room in the old centre. The Parliament’s visitor centre and the Parc du Cinquantenaire are on the doorstep and the metro puts you in the Lower Town in ten minutes. Midweek it is the opposite trade — busy, expensive and short of places to eat in the evening.

Saint-Gilles and Ixelles — Art Nouveau and the good eating

Where locals eatArt NouveauShort tram ride in

South of the centre, Saint-Gilles and Ixelles are where a great many residents would tell you to stay. This is Horta’s Brussels — his own house and studio are here, and four of his townhouses are on the UNESCO list — and the streets around Parvis de Saint-Gilles, Châtelain and Flagey hold the cafes, markets and restaurants that the centre does not. You give up being able to walk home from the Grand-Place in five minutes and gain a neighbourhood that is alive on a Tuesday evening. Trams and the metro run in constantly; it is a ten to twenty minute ride depending where you land.

Around Brussels-Midi — the Eurostar end

Best for trainsCheapest roomsPlainest streets

Brussels-Midi, or Zuid, is the international station: Eurostar to London and Paris, trains to Amsterdam and Cologne, and the airport link. If you are arriving late or leaving at dawn on a Eurostar, a room here saves a fiddly transfer and the rooms are usually the cheapest in the city. The honest caveat is that the immediate surroundings are the plainest part of central Brussels and feel bleak after dark, though the station area has been steadily rebuilt. It is one metro stop from the Grand-Place, so the location costs you very little in sightseeing time. The Sunday market around the station is one of the biggest in Europe and worth getting up for.

Getting around

Brussels is small and the middle of it is walkable, but the hill between the Lower and Upper Towns is real. STIB runs the metro, trams and buses on one ticket, and a contactless bank card tapped at the gate or on board is now the simplest way to pay. The three main railway stations — Midi, Central and Nord — are minutes apart on the same line, which is why a train from the airport or from Bruges will often stop at all three; check which one is closest to your hotel before you book, because it is a free choice. Brussels Airport is about twenty minutes by train to the centre. Charleroi, which many low-cost airlines call Brussels South, is about fifty kilometres away and needs a coach; allow an hour.

When to come, and what it costs

Late April to June and September are the best weeks: mild, long evenings, and the terraces out. July and August are warm and quieter than you might expect, because the officials are away — a good time for a city that is otherwise busy midweek. From late November the Winter Pleasures market and the lights around the Grand-Place and the Bourse bring the crowds back. The weather is Atlantic and changeable in every month, so a jacket that keeps rain off is worth more than a forecast. On price, the pattern to remember is the reverse of most capitals: weekday nights cost more than weekends, and a Friday or Saturday arrival is usually the cheaper booking.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS — the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years — is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today — nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Belgium is about as straightforward as Europe gets. It is a founding member of both the European Union and the Schengen area, it uses the euro, and its land borders with the Netherlands, Germany, Luxembourg and France are internal Schengen crossings that you will cross on a train or a motorway without noticing. Arriving by air from another Schengen country means no passport control at all. The one border that is still a proper frontier is the Channel: the Eurostar to and from London is checked at the station before you board, so allow the time the operator asks for rather than turning up as you would for a domestic train.

Staying in Brussels — questions

Which Brussels station should I book my hotel near?

It matters less than it looks. Midi, Central and Nord are all on the same underground line, a few minutes apart, and most trains call at all three, so you can pick whichever is nearest your hotel when you buy the ticket. Midi is the one that matters for Eurostar and the fast trains to Paris, London, Amsterdam and Cologne. Central is the one to want for the Grand-Place. Nord is mainly for commuters and is the least useful of the three for a visitor.

Is Brussels worth more than a day?

Yes, although a great many people give it an afternoon between trains. Two full days lets you do the Grand-Place and the Lower Town, the fine arts and Magritte museums, a proper look at the Art Nouveau houses in Saint-Gilles and Ixelles, and still leave time for a long lunch. Three is better if you want the Atomium or the Africa Museum at Tervuren. It is a city that improves considerably once you leave the square it is famous for.

Do I need French, or Dutch?

Brussels is officially bilingual and everything public is signed in both French and Dutch, but French is what you will hear most on the street. English is very widely spoken in hotels, restaurants and shops, more so than in many European capitals, and nobody will mind you opening in it. A bonjour on the way in is enough courtesy. Outside Brussels the picture changes: Flanders, which includes Bruges, Ghent and Antwerp, is Dutch-speaking, and Wallonia to the south is French-speaking.

Is the European Quarter a bad place to stay?

Not at all, but know what you are buying. Midweek it is busy and pricey and the streets are quiet in the evening once the offices empty. At weekends it is calm, the hotels discount hard, and you are a ten-minute metro ride from the old centre with a large park on your doorstep. For a Friday-to-Sunday visit it is often the best value in the city. For a Tuesday-to-Thursday stay, the southern neighbourhoods will serve you better.

How easy is it to see Bruges, Ghent or Antwerp from here?

Very easy, and this is one of the real arguments for basing yourself in Brussels. Ghent is about half an hour by train, Antwerp about forty-five minutes, Bruges about an hour. Trains run several times an hour on the main lines, you can buy at the machine on the day, and all three are small enough to see on foot once you arrive. That said, Bruges in particular is a different place after the day-trippers leave, so if you only have one night to spare outside the capital, spend it there.
