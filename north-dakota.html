A short history

North Dakota's plains were long home to the Mandan, Hidatsa, Arikara and Lakota nations, whose earthlodge villages along the Missouri River were later visited by Lewis and Clark during their 1804-05 winter at Fort Mandan. Fur trading gave way to railroad expansion and homesteading in the late 19th century, drawing waves of Scandinavian and German-Russian settlers to farm the northern plains.

North Dakota entered the Union in 1889 alongside South Dakota, the two admitted on the same day. Wheat farming and, later, oil from the Bakken formation became the backbone of the state's economy, while a young Theodore Roosevelt ranched in the Badlands in the 1880s, an experience that shaped his lifelong conservation ethic and led to the national park named for him.

Today North Dakota remains one of the least-visited but most distinctive American states: a place of enormous skies, free-roaming bison, and the colourful, eroded Badlands of Theodore Roosevelt National Park. Agriculture and energy still anchor the economy, and the International Peace Garden straddling the Manitoba border is a quiet, symbolic monument to a long peaceful frontier.

Go to Theodore Roosevelt National Park at dawn rather than midday — the bison herds are more active, the light on the badlands turns rose-gold, and you'll beat both the heat and the tour buses out of Medora.

When to go

Late spring through early autumn.

Winter (Dec–Feb)

Bitterly cold with heavy snow across the open plains; a quiet season best suited to hardy travellers.

Spring (Mar–May)

Cool and often muddy as the prairie thaws, with migrating birds returning to the wetlands.

Summer (Jun–Aug)

Warm, sunny and the peak season for the Badlands, bison viewing and the Peace Garden.

Autumn (Sep–Nov)

Crisp and golden, with fewer visitors and pleasant conditions for hiking Theodore Roosevelt National Park.
