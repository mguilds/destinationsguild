Best Time to Visit Nepal

The Best Time to Visit Nepal

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Nepal is easy to time once you know the one thing that governs it: the mountains are a weather event, not a fixture. They are reliably visible in autumn, often in winter and early spring, and hardly at all through the monsoon. That makes October and November the best weeks of the year — clear air, dry trails, warm days, cool nights and the two great festivals — with March and April a warmer, hazier second season full of rhododendrons. Winter is cold, cheap and often brilliantly clear at low altitude. The monsoon hides the Himalaya, empties the country and is the only sensible time for Upper Mustang.

Four Seasons, One Country

Nepal runs on a clear calendar. Here is what each stretch of the year is actually like.

The autumn season — October and November

The best weeks of the Nepali year, and the reason the country is busiest now. The monsoon has washed the dust out of the air, the Himalaya stand out day after day, the trails have dried, the Terai has cooled and both of the great national festivals fall in this stretch. Days are warm, nights are crisp, and the light is extraordinary. It is also when rooms and teahouse beds are fullest and dearest, and the Everest and Annapurna trails carry real traffic. Book well ahead.

The spring season — March to April

The second season, and some people’s first. It is warmer than autumn, the rhododendron forests of the middle hills come into flower, and the trekking is excellent. The trade is visibility: haze builds through the spring, and the mountain views that are reliable in October can be washed out by mid-morning in late April. The climbing expeditions are on Everest now, which gives Namche and the Khumbu a particular atmosphere.

Winter — December to February

Cold, quiet, cheap, and often brilliantly clear. Low-altitude trekking in the middle hills is very pleasant in the day, Chitwan is at its most comfortable, and the valley cities are empty of crowds. Against that: high passes are shut or dangerous, teahouses at altitude close or run unheated, the Kathmandu Valley fills with a cold winter haze that can hide the mountains for days, and the Terai gets morning fog thick enough to delay flights.

The monsoon — June to September

Heavy rain, usually in the afternoon and overnight, with mountains hidden for days at a time, landslides on mountain roads, leeches on wet trails and unreliable domestic flying. It is the cheapest and emptiest season and the countryside is spectacularly green. It is also the one time to go to Upper Mustang and the other valleys behind the Himalaya, which sit in the rain shadow and stay dry while everywhere else is soaked.

Month by Month

What to expect, and what it costs you, in each month of the year.

January

Cold and often crystal clear, with the cheapest rooms of the year. Fine for Chitwan, the valley cities and low-altitude walking; hard at altitude, where passes are shut and lodges unheated. Mornings in the Terai can be foggy enough to hold up flights.

February

Still cold, still cheap, and beginning to soften toward the end. Clear days are common. A good month for the Kathmandu Valley and the lower hills, and for having Pokhara’s lakeside to yourself.

March

Spring arrives and the trekking season reopens. Warm days, cool nights, rhododendrons starting in the middle hills, and the mountains still reasonably clear. One of the best all-round months of the year.

April

Warm, in flower, and busy — the spring peak, with the Everest expeditions on the mountain. Haze thickens as the month goes on, so the dawn views are less certain than in October. Nepali New Year falls in mid-April, and Bhaktapur’s Bisket Jatra with it.

May

Hot in the lowlands and hazy in the hills, with the first pre-monsoon storms. The last of the spring trekking. In Chitwan it is uncomfortable but excellent for wildlife, as the grass is low and the animals come to water.

June

The monsoon arrives, usually in the second half of the month. Mountain views go. Prices fall away, the hills turn green, and the rain is mostly an afternoon and night affair rather than all day.

July

Wet, warm and empty of visitors. Landslides close mountain roads from time to time and domestic flights are unreliable. The best month for Upper Mustang and the trans-Himalayan valleys behind the main range, which stay dry.

August

The wettest stretch in much of the country. Leeches on the trails, cloud on the mountains and the lowest prices of the year. Fine for the valley cities if you accept being rained on daily.

September

The monsoon withdraws through the month, and by the last week the air has cleared and the trails are drying. Dashain, the longest holiday of the Nepali year, usually begins around now and the country travels home. A gamble at the start of the month, excellent by the end.

October

The best month of the year, and priced like it. Clear mountains, dry trails, warm days, cool nights, and the festivals. Everything is open and everything is full. Book months rather than weeks ahead for trekking lodges and valley hotels.

November

October’s equal, and often a touch clearer. Slightly colder at altitude as the month goes on, with the first high-pass closures at the very end. Tihar, the festival of lights, falls in this stretch.

December

Cold, clear and quietening down. Fine weather for the valley, Pokhara and Chitwan, with genuinely cold nights. Prices soften except over Christmas and New Year, when Kathmandu and Pokhara fill again.

The Cheapest Time to Go

Where the money is, and the two stretches to avoid if you are watching it.

The monsoon — June to September

The cheapest months by a wide margin, with hotel rates well below the autumn peak and almost no other visitors anywhere. You are buying daily rain, hidden mountains and uncertain internal flights. For a trip built around the valley cities, or around Upper Mustang, it can be a very good trade. For a trip built around Himalayan views, it is not.

Winter — January and February

The best value of the dry months. Rooms are cheap, the cities are quiet, the skies are often clearer than in spring, and low-altitude trekking is perfectly comfortable. You give up the high routes and you need warm clothes for unheated rooms.

The two expensive stretches

Mid-October to late November is the high season proper and the one time Nepal genuinely runs short of beds on the popular trails. Christmas and New Year fill Kathmandu and Pokhara again. If your dates are fixed inside either window, book months rather than weeks ahead, trekking lodges included.

Views, Flights & Altitude

The four things that actually shape a Nepali itinerary.

Mountain views are a weather event, not a fixture

This is the thing most first-time visitors get wrong. The Himalaya are not reliably visible from Kathmandu or Pokhara; they are visible when the air is clear, which in practice means October and November, much of the winter, and the early spring. Through the monsoon and the late-spring haze you can spend a week in Pokhara and never see Machhapuchhre at all. If the mountains are the reason for the trip, come in autumn and give yourself several mornings.

Flying in the hills, and building in slack

Domestic flights — above all into Lukla for the Everest trails — are weather-dependent and cancel regularly, sometimes for days at a stretch in poor conditions. Never book an international flight home for the day after you are due to fly out of the mountains. Two days of slack is the sensible minimum, and three in the monsoon or the depths of winter.

Altitude, in any month

Altitude sickness is the one health matter worth taking seriously in Nepal, and the calendar does not change it. It begins to matter above roughly 2,500 metres, it has nothing to do with being fit or young, and the only reliable answers are to climb slowly, to build in rest days and to descend if symptoms appear. Judge a trekking itinerary by its acclimatisation days rather than by how quickly it gets you there, and take medical advice before you go.

Festivals worth planning around

Dashain, usually in September or October, is the longest holiday of the year: shops shut, buses fill, and much of the country goes home to family. Tihar, the festival of lights, follows a fortnight or so later. Holi falls in March and Nepali New Year in mid-April, when Bhaktapur holds Bisket Jatra. All follow lunar or local calendars and move from year to year, so confirm the dates before you fix flights.

Best Time by Region

The country runs from hot plain to high Himalaya, and the right month differs along the way.

The Kathmandu Valley

October to November and February to April are the pleasantest. Winter is cold and often hazy in the valley bowl; the monsoon is wet but perfectly workable for temples and museums, and the fewest visitors of the year are here then.

Pokhara and the Annapurnas

October to early December for the clearest mountain views and the best trekking, March to April for warmth and rhododendrons. Through the monsoon the range is often invisible for days, which takes away most of the reason to be there.

The Everest side and the high trails

October to November first, late March to May second. Winter brings closed passes, shut lodges and serious cold at altitude; the monsoon brings cloud, leeches and cancelled flights into Lukla. Build spare days into either season.

Chitwan and the Terai

October to March for comfort, with December and January mornings cold and foggy. April and May are very hot but the best wildlife months, as the grass is low and the animals gather at water. Lumbini follows the same calendar.

Frequently Asked Questions

The timing questions travellers ask most before booking Nepal.

What is the best time to visit Nepal?

October and November. The monsoon has cleared the air, the Himalaya are out day after day, the trails are dry, the lowlands have cooled and both great festivals fall in this stretch. It is the busiest and dearest time of year and worth it. If you want the same quality of walking with flowers instead of crowds, take March.

What is the worst time to visit Nepal?

For most trips, the height of the monsoon in July and August: mountains hidden, trails leeched, mountain roads liable to landslides and domestic flights unreliable. It is the cheapest and emptiest season, and it is the right time for Upper Mustang behind the main range — but if you are coming for Himalayan views, these are the months to avoid.

When is the best time to trek in Nepal?

October and November for the clearest weather and driest trails, then late March to April for warmth and the rhododendrons. Winter closes the high passes and the monsoon makes the trails hard going. Whatever the season, judge the itinerary by its rest days: altitude is the real constraint, not the calendar.

Can you see the Himalaya all year round?

No, and this is the most useful thing to know about timing Nepal. Clear mountain views are seasonal — reliable in autumn, common in winter and early spring, uncommon in the late-spring haze and rare through the monsoon. Visitors who come in July and expect the view from their hotel terrace are routinely disappointed.

Is the monsoon worth considering at all?

For the right trip, yes. The countryside is at its greenest, the valley cities are empty, prices are at their lowest, and the rain mostly comes in the afternoon and overnight rather than all day. It is also the only sensible season for Upper Mustang and the trans-Himalayan valleys, which sit in the rain shadow. Build in spare days for flights and expect no mountain views from the southern side.
