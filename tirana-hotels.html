Tirana

Where to Stay in Tirana

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Tirana was a small Ottoman town of a few thousand people when it was made capital in 1920, and it has been catching up ever since — most visibly in the last twenty years, which have given it painted apartment blocks, a pedestrianised centre, a skyline of new towers and more building sites than any other capital in the region. It is not a beautiful city and it is a thoroughly likeable one: compact, flat, green at the edges, cheap to eat and drink in, and with the best coffee culture in the Balkans. Almost every Albanian trip starts or ends here, and two nights is enough to see it properly.

Where to stay in Tirana, area by area

The parts of Tirana a visitor actually chooses between, and who each one suits.

The centre: Skanderbeg Square and the boulevard

Most centralLevel walkingBig hotels

Skanderbeg Square is one of the largest pedestrian squares in Europe since it was cleared of traffic, with the National History Museum along one side, the clock tower and the eighteenth-century Et’hem Bey mosque on another, and the opera behind. Running south from it, the boulevard carries the ministries, the university and the Pyramid — built as a museum to Enver Hoxha and reopened in 2023 as a tech and culture centre you can walk up the outside of. Staying here means level pavement, most of the larger international hotels, and everything in the city within twenty minutes on foot. It is the obvious choice for a short first visit.

Blloku

Restaurants and barsLiveliestWalk to everything

Until 1991 this quarter was sealed off and reserved for the party leadership — Hoxha’s own villa still stands in it — and the joke in Tirana is that it has been making up for lost time ever since. It is now the city’s restaurant, bar and coffee district, a grid of low streets immediately west of the boulevard, full in the evenings and genuinely good for eating. Rooms here are mostly small hotels and apartments rather than big houses. It is five minutes from the square, so the only cost is noise: ask for a room off the main streets if you are a light sleeper.

The Grand Park and the embassy streets

QuietestGreenBest for a longer stay

South and east of the centre the city climbs slightly into leafy streets of embassies and older villas, running down to the artificial lake and the Grand Park — a genuinely large wooded park with a lake, paths and the city’s best running and walking. This is the quietest central part of Tirana and the nicest for anything longer than a couple of nights, with a fifteen- to twenty-minute walk or a short taxi into the middle. Expect apartments and smaller hotels.

Pazari i Ri: the New Bazaar

Best for foodAtmosphericFive minutes in

The market quarter north-east of the clock tower was rebuilt about ten years ago and is now the best place in the city to eat: a covered produce market with grill restaurants, bakeries and raki bars in the streets around it, busy from early morning and again all evening. The lanes behind hold small guesthouses and apartments at lower prices than the boulevard. It is a few minutes’ walk from Skanderbeg Square and has more character than anywhere else central.

Rruga e Kavajes and the west

Best valueTransportMixed streets

West of the centre along Rruga e Kavajes and out towards the stadium and the Durres road is where the value is: plenty of mid-range hotels and apartments at prices well below the boulevard, the main bus departure points for Durres and the coast, and the airport road. It is an ordinary working part of the city rather than a pretty one, with a twenty-minute walk or a short bus into the middle. Pick by the individual street rather than the area, and check how far from the square you actually are before booking.

Getting around Tirana

On foot

Central Tirana is flat and small — you can cross the whole of the area a visitor cares about in half an hour — and walking is the way to see it. The pedestrianised square and boulevard make the spine of it easy. Away from the centre pavements get patchy and parked-on, and crossing a main road takes assertiveness: drivers here expect pedestrians to commit.

Buses and the furgons

City buses are frequent and very cheap, with the fare paid to a conductor on board. Intercity coaches and the shared furgon minibuses leave from several different points around the city depending on where they are going rather than from one central station, and those points move from time to time. Ask your hotel desk on the day which departure point serves your destination rather than relying on an address from an older guide.

Taxis and apps

Ride-hailing apps work well in Tirana and are the simplest way to get a fair price. For street taxis use a marked rank and agree the fare or ask for the meter before you set off. Journeys within the city are short and inexpensive.

To and from the airport

Tirana International Airport Nene Tereza is at Rinas, about seventeen kilometres north-west of the centre. A scheduled bus runs into the city, and taxis and apps are available at the terminal. Allow more time than the distance suggests at rush hour: the airport road is the main route north and it backs up.

The Dajti cable car, and getting out of town

The Dajti Ekspres cable car climbs Mount Dajti from the eastern edge of the city and is the quickest escape from it, with a long view back over the plain. Durres and its beach are about forty minutes by road, Kruje about the same, and Berat around two hours. The timetable and the running days for the cable car change by season, so check on the day rather than taking a figure from a website.

When to come

Tirana is the one place in Albania with barely a season. It is a working capital rather than a resort, it does not empty out, and its rooms cost much the same in February as in July — which makes it unusually good value for a winter city break. April to June and September to October are the pleasantest, with warm days and the cafe tables out. July and August are hot on the plain, often uncomfortably so in the middle of the day, and the city is a little quieter because Tirana itself has gone to the coast. November to March is mild and wet rather than cold, and the city carries on entirely as normal. The date that fills the hotels is 28 and 29 November, Independence and Flag Day followed by Liberation Day.

Getting in: what applies, and what does not

Albania is not in the European Union and not in the Schengen area, so it runs its own border. United States citizens may enter as tourists without a visa and may stay up to one year without a residence permit; your passport must be valid for at least three months from arrival and carry a blank page for the stamp. Most other Western nationalities get ninety days visa-free — check your own. The United States rates Albania Level 2, exercise increased caution, in an advisory issued on 26 August 2026 on account of crime; read the current text at travel.state.gov, because the level and wording change.

If your trip also crosses into Greece, Italy or anywhere else in the Schengen area, the following applies to that crossing and not to Albania:

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Tirana — questions

Which part of Tirana should I stay in?

For one or two nights and no strong feelings, stay on or just off Skanderbeg Square and the boulevard: it is flat, central, and walkable to everything. For eating and evenings, stay in Blloku and accept some noise. For a longer stay or a quieter one, take the embassy streets near the Grand Park. For character and food at a lower price, the lanes around the New Bazaar. For value, the streets west along Rruga e Kavajes, chosen street by street.

How long do I need in Tirana?

Two nights is right for most people. That covers Skanderbeg Square and the museum, the Pyramid and the boulevard, Blloku and the Bazaar for eating, and one of the Hoxha-era sites — Bunk’Art 1 or 2, or the House of Leaves — which are what most visitors remember. A third night lets you add the Dajti cable car or a day trip to Kruje or Durres without rushing. Tirana is not a city that needs a week.

Is Tirana walkable?

The centre, very much so: it is flat and compact, and the square and boulevard are pedestrianised. Outside the middle it gets harder — pavements are uneven and often parked across, and main roads take some nerve to cross, because drivers expect pedestrians to keep moving. Taxis and ride-hailing are cheap enough that there is no need to walk a leg you would rather not.

Is Tirana safe?

Yes, by ordinary European standards, and visitors routinely remark on how comfortable the centre feels late in the evening. The United States rates Albania Level 2 on account of crime, which is about organised crime rather than risk to tourists. The practical cautions are pickpocketing in crowds and at the bazaar, agreeing taxi fares in advance, and the fact that demonstrations in the centre can start at short notice and block the boulevard.

Can I use Tirana as a base for the country?

For the central third of it, yes. Durres, Kruje, Mount Dajti and Apollonia are all inside an easy day, and Berat is about two hours each way, which works as a long day trip though it is better as a night. What does not work from Tirana is the south: Gjirokaster, Saranda and Ksamil are four to five hours away on mountain roads, and the Riviera deserves its own nights rather than a dash.

Do I need a car in Tirana?

No, and you are better off without one. The centre is walkable, taxis are cheap, and city parking is genuinely difficult while the traffic has a reputation it has earned. If you are hiring a car for the rest of the country, the usual advice is to collect it on the day you leave the capital rather than on arrival.

What should I not miss?

Skanderbeg Square with the museum, the mosque and the clock tower; walking up the outside of the Pyramid; one of the three sites on the dictatorship — Bunk’Art 1 out by the cable car, Bunk’Art 2 in the centre, or the House of Leaves surveillance museum; and an evening eating around Pazari i Ri or in Blloku. If you have a spare afternoon, the cable car up Mount Dajti.
