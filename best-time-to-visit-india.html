The Best Time to Visit India

The Best Time to Visit India

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated August 2026

Short answer: February for most first trips, with late October the best-value alternative. The classic advice of ‘October to March’ is right as far as it goes, but it hides three things worth knowing: Delhi’s air is at its worst from November to January, prices roughly double over the Christmas fortnight, and Tamil Nadu takes its heaviest rain in October and November while the rest of the country is drying out. India is a subcontinent, and its weather does not agree with itself.

India runs from the Himalaya to eight degrees north of the equator, and asking when to visit it is a little like asking when to visit Europe and North Africa together. Ladakh is open for four months of the year; Kerala is warm in all twelve; Rajasthan is glorious in January and lethal in May; and Chennai has its own private monsoon that arrives when everyone else’s has gone. The good news is the same as it is for Europe: somewhere in India is lovely in every month. This guide sets out where, and when, and what it will cost you.

Four Seasons, and One That Catches People Out

Read your region first — a national average tells you nothing at all here.

The cool season — October to March

Best Oct–MarThe main seasonCold nights in the north

This is when almost everybody comes, and rightly. Days in the northern plains run from the mid-seventies down to the sixties Fahrenheit, Rajasthan is warm and dry, and the south is at its most comfortable. Two caveats belong to it, though, and both are usually left out of the brochures: December and January bring dense morning fog across the northern plains that delays flights and trains, sometimes for hours, and the same still air holds Delhi’s winter smog in place. Nights are colder than visitors expect — Jaisalmer and Bikaner can approach freezing in January — so pack a fleece for Rajasthan.

The hot season — April to June

Avoid in the plainsBest in the HimalayaPeak tiger sightings

The plains become punishing: Delhi routinely passes 104°F and Rajasthan 113°F, with Phalodi, Churu and Jaisalmer regularly the hottest places in the country. We would not send an older traveller to the Golden Triangle in May or June. But this is exactly when the mountains come into their own — the Srinagar road into Ladakh reopens, Himachal and Sikkim are lovely, and the hill stations are the reason they exist. It is also the peak of tiger sightings, because the heat concentrates every animal at the waterholes shortly before the parks close for the rains.

The southwest monsoon — June to September

Rain moves northLadakh at its bestCheapest rates

The rains usually reach Kerala around the first of June, Mumbai about the tenth and Delhi by the end of the month, covering the country by the second week of July and retreating from mid-September. Mumbai, the Konkan and Goa take the brunt of it; Goa largely closes, and the beach shacks are dismantled. Elsewhere the picture is better than its reputation: Rajasthan sees comparatively little rain, Ladakh sits in the rain shadow and is at its finest, and Kerala keeps its traditional Ayurvedic season with luxury resorts at half price. Himalayan roads, however, become genuinely unreliable, with landslides in Himachal and Uttarakhand.

The retreating monsoon — October to December

Tamil Nadu’s wet seasonCatches people outChennai rains

Here is the fact that undoes more Indian itineraries than any other: Tamil Nadu’s heaviest rain falls in October, November and December, not in July. A second, northeast monsoon comes down over Chennai, coastal Andhra, southeastern Karnataka and southern Kerala just as the rest of India is drying out and opening for the season. If your trip takes in Chennai, Madurai or Puducherry, either come in January and February or accept that it will rain. The rest of the country, meanwhile, is entering its best months.

Month by Month

What each month actually feels like, and where to be in it.

January

Beautiful days and cold mornings across Rajasthan and the Golden Triangle, with the desert dropping close to freezing at night. Peak season in Goa and Kerala. Two things to plan around: dense fog on the northern plains, which delays flights and trains and can hide the Taj Mahal at sunrise, and Delhi’s air, still poor. Prices stay high in the first week.

February

The sweet spot, and our pick of the year for a first trip. The fog is easing, the air is improving, Rajasthan is at its best, Kerala and the south are dry, the wildlife parks are comfortable, and the Christmas premium has gone. If you can travel only once and want one recommendation without conditions attached, this is it.

March

Very good through the first half, with heat building in Rajasthan by the end of the month as the thermometer passes 95°F. Holi falls somewhere in March and is worth seeing, though it is boisterous and best watched from a hotel courtyard rather than a public street. Rates soften noticeably after the first week.

April

The plains grow hot — Delhi in the high nineties and rising — but the mountains wake up. The rhododendrons come out in Sikkim and Darjeeling, the Srinagar road into Ladakh usually reopens, and the tiger reserves enter their best weeks as the animals gather at what water is left. There is also a seasonal discount on the thirty-day e-Visa from April to June.

May

The hardest month in the north, with Delhi above 104°F and Rajasthan above 113°F. Mumbai is at its hottest too. Go to the Himalaya instead: Ladakh, Himachal and the hill stations are at their loveliest, and the wildlife parks are at their most productive before they close. For most readers of this guide, not a month for the Golden Triangle.

June

The monsoon reaches Kerala around the first, Mumbai about the tenth and Delhi at the end of the month, breaking the heat as it goes. Ladakh is now fully open and superb. Kerala’s Ayurvedic season begins, with the backwater resorts at their lowest rates of the year. The wildlife parks close for the rains at the end of the month.

July

Full monsoon over most of the country, and Mumbai’s wettest month by some distance — twenty-eight inches is an ordinary July, and the city averages two and a half hours of sunshine a day. Rajasthan, by contrast, is atmospheric rather than wet, and Ladakh is at its peak. Himalayan roads elsewhere are unreliable.

August

Much like July, with Kerala green and cheap and Onam falling somewhere in the month, which is the finest festival in the south. The Udaipur lakes are filling, which is why late summer is when they look their best. Landslide risk continues in Himachal and Uttarakhand. Ladakh remains excellent until early September.

September

The best-value month of the year, and an underrated one. The monsoon retreats through the month, the whole country is green, the crowds have not arrived and the rates are still low. Delhi’s air is clean. Some risk of late rain remains, and the wildlife parks are only beginning to reopen, but for a traveller weighing value against weather this is the insider’s choice.

October

The season opens. The north is comfortable, the country is post-monsoon green, and the light is lovely. The high Himalayan passes close during the month, and Tamil Nadu begins its own rainy season. The first half is excellent value; the second half brings the beginning of Delhi’s smog, as the stubble burning starts in the states around it.

November

Arguably the finest weather of the year for the classic itineraries, with Rajasthan and the Golden Triangle in the mid-seventies to low eighties Fahrenheit. It is also when Delhi’s air is at its very worst, and when peak-season rates begin. Diwali falls in October or November and is beautiful, but it fills flights, closes businesses for several days and sharply worsens the air. The Pushkar Camel Fair, in November, distorts prices across Ajmer and Jaipur for its ten days.

December

Excellent in Rajasthan, Kerala, Goa and the south; cold, foggy mornings in Delhi and Agra, at forty to forty-six degrees Fahrenheit, with flight and train delays common. From about the twentieth, prices spike hard and gala dinners become compulsory in the resorts. Before that date, the first fortnight of December is one of the pleasantest stretches of the year.

The Cheapest Time to Go

Where the value sits, and what you give up for it.

The green season — June to September

Lowest ratesGreen landscapesParks closed

Rates fall sharply once the rains arrive, and September is the best single month of the year for value: the monsoon is retreating, the country is green, the crowds have not come and pre-season prices still apply. Kerala’s luxury backwater resorts run their traditional Ayurvedic season from June to August at roughly half their winter rates. What you give up: the wildlife parks are closed, Goa is largely shut, Himalayan roads are unreliable, and Mumbai is genuinely wet.

The two expensive fortnights

20 Dec–5 JanDiwali weekGala dinners

From about 20 December to 5 January hotels in Goa, Rajasthan, Kerala and the hill stations charge one and a half to three times their usual rates, and — the part that surprises Americans — add a compulsory gala dinner on Christmas Eve and New Year’s Eve, often eighty to two hundred and fifty dollars a head, which appears on the final bill whether you attend or not. Diwali week, in October or November, fills every flight and closes many businesses for several days. The Indian wedding season from November to February books out the Rajasthani palace hotels six to nine months ahead.

Heat, Smog & Crowds

The three practical risks, and how to plan around each.

Heat — the one that matters most

From April to June the northern plains are not merely hot but hazardous: Delhi routinely passes 104°F and Rajasthan 113°F, and the sun at Amber Fort or Agra Fort is unrelieved by shade. For a traveller over sixty this is a medical risk rather than an inconvenience. Whenever you travel, keep the same rhythm — out from about half past six in the morning until eleven, indoors through the middle of the day, out again after four — carry more water than feels necessary, take oral rehydration salts, and plan two sights a day rather than four. A hotel with a working pool and a car with reliable air-conditioning matter more here than a star rating.

Delhi’s winter air

From late October to January, and worst in November, the still, cold air over the northern plain traps crop-stubble smoke from the surrounding states, festival fireworks, exhaust and construction dust. Readings regularly reach the range the American index calls hazardous, and the authorities restrict construction and traffic when they do. Peak sightseeing weather and peak smog overlap almost exactly, which is the awkward truth at the centre of any honest India calendar. If you have asthma, emphysema or a heart condition, keep Delhi short in those months, book a hotel that filters its air, carry fitted N95 masks and check the daily reading. This is a plains problem — Rajasthan, Kerala, Goa, the south and the Himalaya are largely unaffected.

Fog, festivals and crowds

December and January bring dense morning fog across the north that delays flights and trains, sometimes for hours, and can hide the Taj Mahal at the very hour you have got up to see it. Never book a same-day international connection out of a northern morning flight in those months. Festivals are a pleasure and a complication in equal measure: Diwali is beautiful and fills every aeroplane; Holi is exuberant and best watched from a hotel courtyard rather than a public street; the Pushkar Camel Fair distorts prices across Ajmer and Jaipur for its ten days. Indian festival dates move by a fortnight or more from year to year, so check them against your dates rather than against last year’s article.

Best Time by Region

Seven Indias, seven calendars.

The northern plains — Delhi, Agra, Jaipur

Best Oct–MarBest of all Feb–MarAvoid Apr–Jun

October to March, and within that, late February and March give you the weather without the fog, the smog or the Christmas prices. Mid-October is the other sweet spot. April to June is genuinely dangerous heat for older travellers, and December and January mornings can be cold enough to need a coat and foggy enough to hide the Taj Mahal at dawn.

Rajasthan — Udaipur, Jodhpur, Jaisalmer

Best Nov–FebCold desert nightsLakes fullest Aug–Oct

November to February, without argument. Desert nights are cold — Jaisalmer and Bikaner can approach freezing in January — so pack layers, which surprises people. March is fine but warming fast; April to June is brutal. One point specific to Udaipur: the lakes are fullest from late August through October, straight after the monsoon, and can be low at the end of a dry year.

Kerala & the deep south

Best Dec–FebAyurveda Jun–AugGhats cool all year

December to February is the season, with October and March good either side of it. The southwest monsoon soaks Kerala from June to August, and the retreating monsoon adds more in October and November — but June to August is the traditional Ayurvedic treatment season, when the backwater resorts are half price and the tea hills of Munnar and the Western Ghats are cool and green.

Tamil Nadu & the temple south

Best Jan–FebWettest Oct–DecHot Apr–Jun

January and February, and this is the region that catches people out: Chennai’s heaviest rain falls in October, November and December, under a second monsoon that arrives from the northeast as the rest of India dries out. If you are combining the temple south with the Golden Triangle in November, expect two very different sorts of weather.

Goa & the west coast

Best mid-Nov–mid-FebShacks Oct–MayClosed Jun–Sep

Mid-November to mid-February. The beach shacks operate roughly from October or November to May and are dismantled for the monsoon; from June to September much of coastal Goa simply closes, water sports stop and the sea is unswimmable. March to May is hot and humid but still open.

The Himalaya — Ladakh, Himachal, Sikkim

Ladakh Jun–SepSikkim Mar–MayAltitude needs care

Ladakh is a summer destination: June to mid-September, when the Manali road is open, though Leh is reachable by air year round. Himachal runs March to June and September to November. Sikkim and Darjeeling are best in March to May for the rhododendrons and the clearest views of Kanchenjunga, and again from October to early December. Leh sits at eleven and a half thousand feet, so rest completely for two days on arrival before going higher, and take medical advice first if you have heart or lung trouble.

Wildlife parks — Ranthambore, Bandhavgarh, Kanha

Open Oct–JunClosed for the monsoonBest sightings Apr–Jun

The core zones generally open in October and close at the end of June for the rains. The most comfortable months are November to February; the best odds of a tiger are April to June, when the heat drives every animal to the water — a real trade between comfort and sightings. Book core-zone permits months ahead either way.

Frequently Asked Questions

The timing questions travellers ask us most about India.

What is the single best month to visit India?

February, for most first trips. The northern plains are comfortable and the winter fog and smog have largely lifted; Rajasthan is at its loveliest; Kerala and the south are still dry; the wildlife parks are open and pleasant; and the Christmas peak pricing has ended. Late October is the close second, with the country green after the monsoon, thin crowds and pre-season rates. November and December have marginally better weather in Rajasthan but bring Delhi’s worst air and the year’s highest prices.

How bad is Delhi’s winter smog, and should it change my plans?

It should at least inform them. From late October to January, and worst in November, Delhi and the wider northern plain sit under a lid of still, cold air that traps crop-stubble smoke from the surrounding states, festival fireworks, vehicle exhaust and construction dust. Readings regularly reach the range the American index calls hazardous, and the authorities restrict construction and traffic when they do. For a healthy traveller it is unpleasant; for anyone with asthma, emphysema or heart disease it is a genuine medical consideration. The sensible responses are to keep Delhi short in those months, choose a hotel that filters its air, carry fitted N95 masks, plan indoor mornings, and check the daily reading. Better still, travel in February, March or early October. And do not extend the worry to the whole country — Rajasthan, Kerala, Goa, the south and the Himalaya are largely unaffected.

Is the monsoon a reason not to go?

Not by itself. The southwest monsoon reaches Kerala around the first of June, Mumbai about ten days later and Delhi by the end of the month, and withdraws through September and early October. It genuinely disrupts Mumbai, the Konkan coast, Goa and Himalayan road travel. But Rajasthan gets comparatively little rain and looks wonderful under cloud; Ladakh sits in the rain shadow and is at its best; and Kerala keeps its traditional Ayurvedic treatment season in these months, with the luxury backwater resorts at half price. Rain in India usually means a spectacular downpour rather than a grey week. What it does mean is that the wildlife parks close, the Elephanta ferry stops, and mountain roads become unreliable.

When is India cheapest, and when is it most expensive?

Cheapest: the monsoon and the shoulder that follows it — June to September, with September the best value of the whole year, since the rain is retreating, the landscape is green and pre-season rates still apply. Most expensive: roughly 20 December to 5 January, when hotels in Goa, Rajasthan, Kerala and the hill stations charge one and a half to three times their usual rates and add compulsory gala dinners on Christmas Eve and New Year’s Eve — often eighty to two hundred and fifty dollars a head, non-negotiable, and an unwelcome surprise on the final bill. Diwali week and the Indian wedding season, roughly November to February, also fill the Rajasthani palace hotels; book those six to nine months ahead.

What is the best time for an older traveller specifically?

Late February into March, and it is not a close call. You get the good winter weather without the December fog that delays flights and trains across the north, without the worst of Delhi’s air, without the Christmas prices and before the April heat. Heat is the risk that matters most: from April to June, Delhi routinely passes 104°F and Rajasthan 113°F, which for anyone over sixty is a hazard rather than a discomfort. Whenever you travel, keep to the same daily rhythm — out from about half past six until eleven, indoors through the middle of the day, out again after four — and plan two sights a day rather than four.
