Barbados

Barbados Travel Guide

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Barbados is the easternmost island in the Caribbean — a coral island of about 166 square miles and some 280,000 people, sitting out in the Atlantic on its own, a hundred miles clear of the island chain that runs down to Trinidad. Being coral rather than volcanic, it is gently rolling instead of mountainous, which is why it has so much more sand than its neighbours. And being out on its own in the wind, it has three quite different coasts: a calm Caribbean west, a breezy south with surf in it, and a wild Atlantic east where nobody swims. Independent since 1966 and a republic since November 2021, it is the easiest island in the region to travel around without a car, and the one where you will hear the most cricket talked.

What your money is worth in Barbados
US $1 = 2.00 Bds$
Bds$ 1 = US 50¢
The Barbados dollar has been pegged to the US dollar at two to one for decades, so this is one of the few places where the conversion needs no app: halve the Barbados price and you have the dollar price. US dollars are accepted almost everywhere, but change comes back in Barbados dollars and you will usually do a shade better paying in local money. Cards are fine in hotels, supermarkets and most restaurants; keep cash for the buses, the rum shops and the fish fry. Expect a nightly government room levy and a service charge to be added to hotel and restaurant bills, so the final total runs above the headline rate — ask what is included when you book rather than at check-out.

Why visit Barbados

More sand than its neighbours, a country that works, and an island small enough to see properly in a week.

Sand, because it is coral and not a volcano

Most of the eastern Caribbean is volcanic, which means dark sand and steep shores. Barbados is an uplifted coral island, so the beaches are pale, wide and soft, and there are a great many of them. Every beach on the island is public to the high-water mark, including the ones in front of the grandest hotels — a quietly important fact, and one worth knowing before anyone tells you otherwise.

Three coasts, three different seas

You can swim in flat, clear water on the west coast in the morning, watch Atlantic rollers break over rock on the east coast after lunch, and eat fish on the breezy south coast in the evening. Few islands this size give you three such different days, and the drive between any two of them is under an hour.

You do not need to hire a car, or a guide

Blue government buses, yellow minibuses and white route taxis cover the island on a flat fare, the same whether you ride one stop or the length of the island. English is the language. The water is safe to drink. Nothing about getting around here requires an organised tour, which is unusual in the region and is the single biggest reason independent travellers love the place.

Flying fish, cou-cou and Friday night

The national dish is flying fish with cou-cou, a cornmeal and okra polenta, and the national Friday night is the fish fry at Oistins, where half the island eats grilled marlin and mahi-mahi at plastic tables by the water. Add macaroni pie, fish cakes, a rum shop on every corner and a rum industry that dates itself to the seventeenth century, and you have a food island that costs a fraction of its hotels.

Barbados at a glance

Money

The Barbados dollar, pegged at two to the US dollar. The island is not cheap — hotels on the west coast are priced like Europe in summer — but buses, local restaurants and the south coast are reasonable, and the shoulder months knock a good deal off a room.

Language

English, officially and in every shop and office. Among themselves Bajans speak Bajan, an English-based creole with its own grammar and a great deal of wit in it. You will understand everything said to you and perhaps half of what is said around you.

Entry

Visitors from the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom and the European Union do not need a visa for a holiday; you need a passport and the immigration officer sets the length of stay on arrival. Barbados has for some time asked arriving passengers to fill in an online immigration and customs form shortly before travel, and the requirement has changed more than once — check the Barbados government’s own travel form page a week before you fly rather than trusting an older guide, ours included.

Getting around

Buses and taxis. The blue Transport Board buses and the yellow minibuses and ZR vans run a flat fare, keep long hours and go almost everywhere; Bridgetown is the hub for both. Taxis are unmetered, so agree the fare before you get in. Hire cars make sense only for the east coast and the interior.

Driving

On the left, as in Britain. Visitors need a Barbados visitor’s driving permit, which the hire companies issue over the counter. The ABC Highway moves traffic around Bridgetown; everything else is a narrow parish lane with a bus coming the other way.

Distances

Small. The island is roughly twenty-one miles north to south and fourteen across. Grantley Adams airport, in Christ Church, is about fifteen minutes from the south coast hotels and forty to fifty from the west coast in traffic. Nothing on Barbados is more than an hour and a quarter from anything else.

The west — the Platinum Coast

Holetown, Paynes Bay, Porters and Speightstown, strung along flat Caribbean water on the sheltered side of the island. Calm swimming, sunsets, the grand hotels, the turtles — and the highest prices in the eastern Caribbean.

Top things to do in Barbados

The short list, honestly ranked, with what each one actually asks of you.

The Friday fish fry at Oistins

The best evening on the island and one of the best in the Caribbean, and it costs the price of a plate of fish. Grilled marlin, mahi-mahi or flying fish with macaroni pie and breadfruit, at trestle tables in the Bay Garden, with music and half the island there. It is busiest and best on a Friday, it is entirely level ground, and you do not need to book anything.

Swim with the turtles in Carlisle Bay

Green and hawksbill turtles feed in the shallow water off Bridgetown and Paynes Bay, and there are two accessible shipwrecks in Carlisle Bay for snorkellers. Go by catamaran or swim out from Pebbles Beach. Choose an operator who does not hand-feed or chase the animals; the good ones will tell you so before you ask.

Walk Bridgetown and the Garrison

Parliament, the synagogue and its eighteenth-century burial ground, the careenage, the old garrison and its parade ground, now a racecourse. It is flat, compact and shaded in places, and reading the inscriptions is a sobering education in what built this island. Go in the morning; the town is a furnace by two.

Harrison’s Cave

A limestone cavern in the middle of the island, visited by electric tram, which makes it one of the few natural attractions in the Caribbean that is genuinely easy for anyone with difficulty walking. Cool, dripping, theatrically lit. Book ahead in high season and take a light layer.

Stand above the Soup Bowl at Bathsheba

Great mushroom-shaped rocks, Atlantic swell breaking white across a mile of shore, and a surf break good enough that professionals come for it. Eat lunch on a terrace above it, walk the sand at low tide, and stay out of the water. Combine it with Hunte’s Gardens or St Nicholas Abbey and you have the perfect inland day.

Crop Over, if you come in summer

The old sugar harvest festival, now the island’s carnival: weeks of calypso, markets and fetes building from midsummer to Grand Kadooment on the first Monday in August, when the costumed bands cross the island. If you want a quiet beach holiday, that is the fortnight to avoid; if you want the island at full volume, it is the only fortnight that will do.

Best time to visit & getting there

Barbados has a dry season and a wet one rather than a hot season and a cold one. From about the middle of December to the end of May it hardly rains, the humidity is low, the trade wind blows steadily and the sun is reliable; that is the high season, and from February to the middle of April it is both full and expensive. From June to November it is warmer and wetter, usually in the form of short, heavy afternoon showers rather than days of rain, and the hotels cost a third to a half less. The sea is warm all year — it does not drop out of the high seventies.

Our own pick is the first half of December or the second half of April into May: dry-season weather, high-season sea, and shoulder-season prices. The Atlantic hurricane season runs from 1 June to 30 November; Barbados sits far enough east and south that most storms miss it, but Hurricane Beryl in July 2024 was a reminder that “most” is not “all”. Travel in those months is perfectly sensible with insurance that covers weather disruption.

Getting there is easier than for most of the eastern Caribbean. Grantley Adams International Airport, in Christ Church on the south coast, takes nonstop flights from the north-eastern United States and Florida, from Toronto, and from London, and it is the regional hub for the rest of the Lesser Antilles. Our month-by-month guide to the best time to visit Barbados goes through it season by season, Crop Over and cruise season included.

Arriving by ship

Bridgetown is one of the eastern Caribbean’s turnaround ports, which means a great many Southern Caribbean cruises begin or end here rather than merely calling in. If yours does, the single most useful thing we can tell you is to add a night at the front of the trip: flights from North America and Europe land in the late afternoon and evening, the ship sails early, and a night ashore turns a tight connection into a calm one — and gives you an evening at the fish fry or on the Garrison into the bargain.

The cruise terminal is in the Deep Water Harbour, a little north-west of the town centre; it is a long, hot walk to Broad Street and a very short taxi. On a one-day call, Carlisle Bay, the Garrison and Harrison’s Cave are the three things that repay the time. Barbados in your later years

One of the kindest islands in the region for an unhurried traveller, with two honest cautions: the afternoon heat and the steps down to the sand.

What makes it easy

English everywhere, left-hand driving that British visitors find familiar, drinkable tap water, good pharmacies, a well-regarded hospital in Bridgetown, and an island so small that no outing is a long haul. Bajans are courteous to older visitors in a way that is noticeable even in the Caribbean.

Ask about the steps to the beach

A good many hotels, especially the older small ones, sit above the shore with a flight of steps or a steep path down to the sand, and the listing will not mention it. Ask two questions before you book: is there a lift in the building, and is the beach reached on the level? The answers vary enormously between hotels a hundred yards apart.

Respect the middle of the day

It is eighty-five degrees with high humidity by late morning for much of the year, and the trade wind makes it feel cooler than it is, which is how people get sunburnt and dehydrated. Do your walking and your sightseeing early, keep the afternoon for shade or water, and drink much more than you think you need.

Let the buses and taxis do the work

The buses are cheap, frequent and go everywhere, though they are not gentle rides and the minibuses are driven with spirit. Taxis are plentiful and unmetered — agree the price before you set off, and it is entirely normal to hire a driver for a half-day island tour at a fixed rate.

The west coast for calm water

If getting in and out of the sea is the question, the west coast answers it: the water there is usually as flat as a pool and the entry gentle. The south coast is breezier with more shore break, which is fun on a boogie board and harder work on uncertain footing.

One base is plenty

The island is too small to need two hotels. Pick the coast that suits you, stay put, and take the bus or a driver to the rest. The commonest mistake we see here is a packed itinerary on an island whose whole point is sitting still in it.

Getting in: what you need, and what you do not

You need a passport. Visitors from the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, the European Union and most of the Commonwealth need no visa for a holiday, and the immigration officer sets the length of stay when you land; have your address on the island and your return booking to hand, because both are routinely asked for.

Barbados has for some time asked arriving passengers to complete an online immigration and customs form in the days before travel, and that requirement has been changed and re-issued more than once. We are not going to print a web address that may be out of date by the time you read this: check the Barbados government’s own travel form page, or ask your airline, about a week before you fly. It takes a few minutes and there is nothing to pay for the form itself.

Departure tax is included in the price of an airline ticket, so there is nothing to pay at the airport on the way home. If you intend to drive, you will need a visitor’s driving permit, which the car hire companies issue when you collect the car — bring your home licence.

Fishing here

Barbados is a blue-water island rather than a flats island: the shelf drops away quickly, and what the boats out of Bridgetown go looking for is wahoo, dorado, barracuda, kingfish and the occasional marlin, mostly from January to May. If it is bonefish on a shallow sand flat you are after, that is the other end of the Caribbean — our guide to fishing the flats covers where those are and what they ask of you. Barbados travel FAQ

When is the best time to visit Barbados?

Mid-December to May is the dry season and the reliable half of the year: sunshine, low humidity, a steady trade wind and almost no rain. It is also the expensive half, and February and March are the busiest weeks of all. Our own favourite months are the edges of that season — early December, and the second half of April into May — when the weather is still dry, the sea is warm and rooms cost noticeably less. June to November is warmer, wetter and cheaper, with short heavy showers rather than all-day rain, and it is also the Atlantic hurricane season, which runs from 1 June to 30 November.

Does Barbados get hurricanes?

It can, and the island's reputation for being out of the way is only partly earned. Barbados sits far enough east and far enough south that most Atlantic storms pass to the north of it, which is why insurers and cruise lines treat it kindly. But it is not immune: Hurricane Beryl struck in July 2024 and did serious damage to the fishing fleet at Bridgetown. If you travel between June and November, buy travel insurance that covers weather disruption and keep an eye on the forecast in the week before you fly.

Do I need a car in Barbados?

Most visitors do not. The island is about twenty-one miles by fourteen, the bus network covers it properly, and taxis are everywhere. Driving is on the left, the lanes outside the main roads are narrow and signposting is thin, and visitors need a local driving permit, which the car hire companies arrange when you collect the car. Hire one for two or three days if you want to explore the east coast and the interior at your own pace, and leave it alone if you are staying on the west or south coast.

What money do they use, and are US dollars accepted?

The Barbados dollar, which has been fixed at two Barbados dollars to one US dollar for decades. That makes the arithmetic easy. US dollars are widely accepted in hotels, restaurants and taxis, but your change will usually come back in Barbados dollars, and you will do slightly better paying in local currency. Cards are accepted nearly everywhere; carry some cash for buses, rum shops and the fish fry.

Which coast should I stay on?

The west coast — the Platinum Coast, around Holetown — has the calm, clear, swimming-pool water and the grander hotels, and it is the quietest and the priciest. The south coast, from Hastings through St Lawrence Gap to Oistins, has more surf, more restaurants, more nightlife and far better value. Bridgetown and the Garrison suit anyone who wants the town, the history and Carlisle Bay on the doorstep. The east coast is magnificent and is not for swimming — the Atlantic there is dangerous.

Can you swim on the east coast?

No, and this is the one piece of advice on the island we would give most firmly. The Atlantic side around Bathsheba has heavy surf, strong currents and rocks, and the Soup Bowl is a serious surf break rather than a beach. Go there for the scenery, the rock formations and lunch, watch the surfers, and do your swimming on the west or south coast.

Is Barbados a good island for older travellers?

It is one of the best in the Caribbean. English is the language, the medical care is good by regional standards, the island is small so nothing is a long drive, and the buses and taxis mean you need not drive at all. The things to watch are the heat and humidity in the afternoon, beach access that sometimes involves steps down from the road, and the fact that many of the older small hotels have no lift. Ask about the lift and the walk to the sand before you book.

How long do you need?

A week is comfortable: enough for one base, a day on the east coast, a day in Bridgetown, the Friday fish fry at Oistins and several days doing nothing at all. Ten days lets you split the trip between two coasts. If you are arriving on a cruise and have one day, spend it on Carlisle Bay or at Harrison's Cave and the east coast, not trying to see everything.
