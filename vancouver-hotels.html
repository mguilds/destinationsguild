Vancouver

Where to Stay in Vancouver

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Vancouver is wedged between the sea and the mountains, which makes it the most good-looking big city in Canada and a very easy one to choose a district in: the downtown peninsula holds most of what a visitor wants, it is flat, it is walkable end to end, and the seawall runs the whole way round it. The decisions are really about how close you want to be to Stanley Park, the water and the restaurants, and how much you mind paying downtown prices.

Downtown and Coal Harbour — the obvious choice

Most hotelsCruise terminalPriciest

The centre of the peninsula, between Robson Street and the harbour, is where the hotels, the art gallery, the shops and the convention centre are, and where the cruise ships dock at Canada Place. Coal Harbour, along the north shore, gives you the seaplanes, the marinas, the mountain view across the inlet and the start of the Stanley Park seawall. Everything is walkable, the SkyTrain runs underneath and the airport line comes straight in. It is also the most expensive part of the city to sleep in, and the stretch around Granville and Hastings can feel rough at night in a way that surprises first-time visitors.

The West End and Stanley Park — the best value on the peninsula

Near the parkEnglish Bay beachMid-range rooms

West of downtown, between Robson Street and the beach, the West End is a dense residential neighbourhood of apartment towers and old wooden houses, with Davie Street’s cafes and restaurants through the middle of it, English Bay beach at the end and Stanley Park — a thousand acres of rainforest, seawall and totem poles — at the top. The hotels are older and plainer than Coal Harbour’s and cost noticeably less, and this is where we would stay with the whole peninsula to choose from. It is ten to twenty minutes on foot into the middle of downtown and there is no SkyTrain station in it.

Yaletown and False Creek — restaurants and the little ferries

Best restaurantsAquabusQuiet streets

The old warehouse district on the south-east side of the peninsula was converted twenty-odd years ago and is now brick loading bays, patios and some of the best restaurants in the city, with the False Creek waterfront and the small Aquabus and False Creek ferries at the bottom of the street — which are the pleasant way to reach Granville Island and its public market. It is on the Canada Line, so the airport is a straight run. There is less hotel stock here than downtown and almost nothing cheap, and it is a little further from Stanley Park.

Kitsilano and the west side — beaches and a local morning

BeachesGranville IslandBuses, no SkyTrain

Across the Burrard Bridge, Kitsilano is a low-rise neighbourhood of wooden houses, beaches, yoga studios and good coffee, with Kits Beach and the enormous outdoor saltwater pool, Vanier Park and its museums, and Granville Island a short walk or ferry ride east. It is where Vancouver feels least like a downtown and most like a place people live, and the rooms are mostly small hotels and guest houses at fair prices. The trade-offs are real: there is no SkyTrain, so you are on buses or the ferries, and it is fifteen to twenty-five minutes from downtown depending on the bridge traffic.

Getting around

The SkyTrain is driverless, frequent and the fastest way around: three lines, with the Canada Line running from the airport through downtown and the Expo and Millennium lines heading east. A Compass card or a contactless bank card works on all of it, on the buses and on the SeaBus, the passenger ferry across to North Vancouver, which is twelve minutes and the cheapest harbour cruise in the city.

From YVR, take the Canada Line: about twenty-five minutes into downtown, every few minutes, with a small extra charge added to the fare when you start at the airport. It is quicker and far cheaper than a taxi in the afternoon. Note the one quirk of the fare system: journeys that cross between zones cost more at peak times, but everything inside the downtown peninsula is one zone.

The real point of Vancouver, though, is that it is flat and built for walking and cycling. The seawall runs unbroken from Coal Harbour round Stanley Park, down English Bay and along False Creek — about twenty-eight kilometres in all — with separated bike lanes and a public bike-hire scheme feeding into it. Doing some of it on foot or on two wheels is the best free thing in the city.

When to come

June to September is the season, and it is a dry one: Vancouver summers are warm, bright and far less humid than the eastern cities, with long evenings, the beaches in use and every boat and patio running. July and August are the busiest and dearest and also the most reliable. September holds the weather and loses the crowds, which makes it our pick.

The thing to know is the rain. From November to March this is one of the wettest cities in the country — mild rather than cold, rarely much snow at sea level, but grey and dripping for days at a time. It is not a reason to stay away: the skiing on the three local mountains is twenty to forty minutes from downtown, Whistler is two hours up the coast road, and the city is at its coziest. Just bring a proper coat and do not count on views. April and May bring the cherry blossom and the gardens and are lovely between the showers.

Two trips worth the extra nights rather than a rushed day: Victoria and Vancouver Island, reached by ferry or seaplane, and Whistler up the Sea-to-Sky highway. Our month-by-month guide to the best time to visit Canada goes through the year properly.

Getting in

Most visitors from visa-exempt countries arriving in Canada by air need an Electronic Travel Authorization, an eTA, applied for online before they fly and tied to the passport they travel on. Citizens of the United States do not need one but do need proof of citizenship. Nationals of visa-required countries need a visitor visa instead. An eTA is not required if you arrive by land or by sea. We do not print the fees here because they are revised from time to time — read them off the official pages at canada.ca when you book, and apply there rather than through an agent charging a mark-up for the same form.

Two Canada-specific points worth checking well before you travel: past criminal convictions, including an old drink-driving conviction, can make a traveller inadmissible, and there is a formal process for it that is not quick; and taking cannabis across the border in either direction is a federal offence whatever is legal at home. Vancouver hotels FAQ

Which part of Vancouver should I stay in?

The West End if you want the best balance of price, position and quiet: Stanley Park and English Bay at one end, downtown at the other, and rooms that cost less than the harbour hotels. Downtown or Coal Harbour for the widest choice and the mountain views, especially if you are joining a cruise. Yaletown if dinner matters most, and Kitsilano if you would rather wake up in a neighbourhood than a city centre.

How do I get in from the airport?

The Canada Line SkyTrain, which runs from under the terminal into downtown in about twenty-five minutes, every few minutes, for a few dollars plus a small airport surcharge. It is quicker than a taxi at most times of day and a great deal cheaper. Trains have luggage space and the downtown stations are close to most hotels.

Does it really rain that much?

From November to March, yes: this is one of the wettest big cities in Canada, though mild with it and rarely snowy at sea level. From June to September it is the opposite — reliably dry and bright, which is why the summer here fills up. If you are coming in winter, bring a waterproof coat rather than an umbrella, because the wind off the water defeats umbrellas.

How many days does Vancouver need?

Three days is right for the city itself: Stanley Park and the seawall, Granville Island, the museums, Gastown and an evening or two out. Add two more if you want Victoria and a night on Vancouver Island, and another two for Whistler. Trying to do the city, the island and Whistler in three days is the commonest mistake here.

Is it a good base for the Rockies?

Less than people assume. Banff and Lake Louise are about ten hours away by road, across two mountain ranges, so the Rockies are a separate trip rather than a day out — fly or take the train to Calgary, or drive it over several days. What Vancouver is a superb base for is the coast: Whistler, Victoria, the Sunshine Coast and the inside-passage ferries.
