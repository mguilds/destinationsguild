Nida

Where to Stay in Nida and the Curonian Spit

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Nida is the main village on the Lithuanian half of the Curonian Spit, a sandbar about ninety-eight kilometres long and in places only a few hundred metres wide, lying between the open Baltic and the shallow Curonian Lagoon. The whole of it is a UNESCO World Heritage site, listed in 2000 and shared with Russia’s Kaliningrad region, and the Lithuanian part is a national park. What that protects is odd and specific: pine forest planted to stop the sand, bare migrating dunes that have buried villages within historical memory, and four small settlements of wooden fishermen’s cottages painted blue and oxblood. Nida is the largest of them and the one with the beds.

Nida village and the harbour

Most centralLagoon sideWalk to everything

Nida faces east, away from the sea, onto the flat brown water of the Curonian Lagoon, and the village is arranged along it: a small yacht harbour, a promenade, the amber gallery, a church up the slope and a handful of restaurants. Everything a visitor needs is inside a ten-minute walk, the beach on the open Baltic side is another fifteen minutes through the pines, and the Parnidis dune rises at the southern end of the village. This is where most of the beds are and where to stay on a first visit. It is also the only part of the Lithuanian spit with anything resembling an evening.

Purvyne and the fishermen’s cottages

PrettiestQuietShort walk in

The old quarter on the slope north of the harbour, where the wooden cottages stand in their original blue and oxblood with carved finials on the gables, is the loveliest corner of the spit and now almost entirely given over to guesthouses and holiday lets. Thomas Mann built a summer house up here in the early nineteen-thirties and it is open as a museum. The ethnographic cemetery with its carved wooden grave markers is a few minutes away. It is five to ten minutes from the harbour on foot, and it is silent at night.

The Urbo kalnas and Parnidis side

Nearest the big duneLighthousePine and sand

South and west of the village the ground rises to the lighthouse on Urbo kalnas and then to the Parnidis dune, a bare sand hill above the strait with a granite sundial on top and the Russian border visible down the spit from it. Staying on this side puts you in the pines, a few minutes from both the dune and the sea beach, and a little further from the restaurants. Marked paths and boardwalks keep walkers off the sand itself, which is actively moving and protected.

Preila and Pervalka, up the spit

SmallestNothing to do, deliberatelyCar or bus

Two tiny settlements between Nida and Juodkrante, eight and fourteen kilometres up the road, each a scatter of houses on the lagoon with a jetty, a bay and a great deal of silence. There is no shopping and barely any eating out; people come here to walk, cycle, fish and read. Buses along the spit road serve both, and the cycle path from Nida is flat and beautiful. Juodkrante, halfway to Klaipeda

Closest to the ferryBird colonyAmber bay

Thirty-odd kilometres up the spit and about twenty minutes from the vehicle ferry, Juodkrante is the other village with a proper street: lagoon-side promenade, wooden villas, the Hill of Witches with its carved wooden figures in the woods above, and — just outside it — one of the largest cormorant and grey heron colonies in Europe, which you can smell before you see. It suits a shorter stay or a stopover on the way down. The same caution applies: the booking button searches Nida, so check the address.

Getting around the spit

On foot and by bicycle

The whole Lithuanian spit has a paved cycle path running its length through the pines, flat, well-signed and one of the best rides in the Baltic. Bicycles are for hire in Nida and Juodkrante. On foot, Nida itself and the dune and beach around it are all walkable; the villages up the spit are a ride or a bus rather than a walk.

Getting here from Klaipeda

Two ways, and they are different trips. The passenger ferry from Klaipeda’s Old Town quay crosses to Smiltyne in a few minutes, and buses run down the spit from there. With a car you want the vehicle ferry from the separate terminal south of Klaipeda, then roughly fifty kilometres of single road down the spit. Allow well over an hour door to door either way, and much more on a July weekend when the vehicle queues build.

The national park fee and the rules

The spit is a national park and a UNESCO World Heritage site, shared with Russia’s Kaliningrad region, and vehicles pay an environmental fee at the barrier. The amount changes, so check it on the day rather than taking a figure from a website. Walking on the protected dunes off the boardwalks is forbidden and enforced, fires are restricted, and the sand really does move: the villages that used to stand here were buried.

Buses along the spit

Local buses run the spit road between Smiltyne, Juodkrante, Pervalka, Preila and Nida through the day, more often in summer. They are the sensible way to reach the smaller villages without a car and the only way to do the spit cheaply. Check the last departure before planning dinner somewhere other than where you sleep.

Boats on the lagoon

In season, boats cross the lagoon from Nida to the Nemunas delta and run shorter trips along the spit, and there are fishing boats that will take passengers. None of it runs to a timetable you can rely on from abroad, so arrange it at the harbour when you arrive.

When to come

The spit has the shortest season in the country and the sharpest difference between its halves of the year. June is the best single month: long light, warm pines, the paths empty and the village still half-asleep. July and August are the only weeks the Baltic is swimmable and the only weeks Nida is full, dear and booked out months ahead. September is our own pick — warm sand, cool air, nobody on the cycle path. October to April is wind, rain, a grey sea and much of the village shut, and it is also when the spit is at its strangest and most beautiful, and when amber comes up on the sand after a storm. Whenever you come, book before you travel: the number of rooms inside a national park does not change.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Lithuania is in the European Union, the Schengen area and the euro, so arriving from anywhere else in Schengen there is no check at all. Month-by-month weather and the dates worth planning around are in Best Time to Visit Lithuania, and the country guide is The Lithuania Travel Guide.

Nida and the Curonian Spit — questions

Is Nida worth the journey?

Yes, and it is the one place in Lithuania we would tell a visitor not to skip for time. It takes a ferry and an hour or more of road from Klaipeda, and what you get is a ninety-eight-kilometre sandbar between a lagoon and the open Baltic, pine forest, a bare moving dune the height of a tower block, wooden fishing villages that have been painted the same two colours for two centuries, and almost no noise. It is a UNESCO World Heritage site and has been since 2000.

How many nights should I stay?

Two or three. One night means you spend most of your time travelling. Two gives you the dune, the beach, the village and Thomas Mann’s house without hurrying. Three lets you cycle up the spit to Preila and Juodkrante, which is the best thing to do here and the thing day-trippers never get to.

Which side is the sea and which is the lagoon?

The lagoon is on the east, facing Lithuania, and it is where the villages, the harbours and the promenades are: shallow, brown, calm and good for small boats. The Baltic is on the west, over the dune ridge and through the pines, and that is where the swimming beaches are. The spit is only a few hundred metres to a couple of kilometres wide, so you are never more than a walk from either.

Can I see the Russian border?

You can see down the spit towards it from the top of the Parnidis dune, and the frontier is a few kilometres south of Nida. It is a closed external border of the European Union, there is no crossing here for a visitor, and the area immediately around it is restricted. Look, photograph the view north, and do not wander south off the marked paths.

Is there anything to do in bad weather?

Less than you would like, and that is part of the point. There is the Thomas Mann house, the amber gallery, the ethnographic museums in Nida and Juodkrante, the Sea Museum back at Smiltyne, and a short list of good restaurants. Beyond that, a wild day on the spit is for walking the beach in waterproofs, which is genuinely one of the better things to do here. If you need a programme, stay in Klaipeda.

When should I come?

June for light and quiet, July and August if you want to swim and do not mind company, and September for the best combination of warm sand and empty paths — that is our pick. The spit in winter is beautiful, almost deserted, and the time local people walk the beaches after a storm looking for amber; accept that much of the eating and renting is shut.

Is it expensive?

It is the dearest place to sleep in Lithuania in July and August, by a clear margin, because the supply of beds inside a national park cannot grow. It is reasonable in June and September and cheap in winter. If high summer is fixed for you, book a long way ahead, and consider sleeping in Klaipeda and coming down for two long days instead.
