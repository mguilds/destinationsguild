Best Time to Visit Laos

The Best Time to Visit Laos

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Laos has two seasons and one complication. The seasons are straightforward — a cool dry winter, a hot dry spring, a green summer monsoon. The complication is the burning haze in March and April, which most guides mention in passing and which is in fact the single most likely thing to spoil a first trip. Get the month right and Laos is one of the most rewarding countries in Southeast Asia. Get it wrong and you can spend a fortnight looking at mountains you cannot see.

Two Seasons and a Haze

What each stretch of the Lao year is actually like on the ground.

The cool dry season — November to February

The high season, and it earns it. Little or no rain, warm days, and mornings cool enough for a jacket in Luang Prabang and genuinely cold in the uplands around Phonsavan. The air is at its clearest, which matters in a country whose best views are long ones. This is also when rooms are fullest and dearest, especially in Luang Prabang between Christmas and New Year.

The hot dry season — March to May

Dry and seriously hot, with April the peak. The heat is manageable with an early start and a slow afternoon. The haze is the real issue — see below. By May the first storms arrive, the air clears and the country turns green almost overnight.

The green season — June to October

The southwest monsoon, and much better than its reputation. It seldom rains all day: expect a bright morning, a heavy afternoon downpour and a washed-clean evening. Waterfalls run hard, the hills are brilliant, the haze is gone, there are few other visitors and prices are at their lowest of the year. Unsealed roads in the north get difficult and August and September are genuinely wet.

North and south are not the same trip

Laos is long. The northern mountains around Luang Prabang, Nong Khiaw and Phonsavan are markedly cooler than the Mekong lowlands, and in December and January the nights up there are cold enough that unheated guest houses are uncomfortable. The far south around Pakse and the Four Thousand Islands stays warm all year, and the Bolaven Plateau above Pakse is the one place in the south that is reliably cool.

Month by Month

What to expect, and what it costs you, in each month of the year.

January

The coolest month and one of the best. Dry, clear, warm by day and genuinely cold after dark in the northern hills — Luang Prabang mornings want a jacket and Phonsavan can drop near freezing. Peak season prices and full rooms.

February

As good as January with the chill easing. Dry, clear and comfortable everywhere, and the last reliable month for mountain views before the haze builds. A very good month for the north.

March

Hot, dry, and the month the burning haze usually begins in the northern valleys. Early March is often still clear; by the end of the month the hills around Luang Prabang and Vang Vieng can be lost in smoke. Prices ease as the crowds thin.

April

The hottest month, and the haze is typically at its worst in the first half. Mid-month brings Pi Mai, Lao New Year: three days of water-throwing and the warmest welcome of the year, but the country effectively stops, buses and flights fill and many businesses close. Wonderful to witness, awkward to travel through.

May

The first storms break, the smoke washes out of the air and the country turns green again. Still hot, but the relief is immediate and the light comes back. Boun Bang Fai, the rocket festival, falls this month. Rates drop from mid-month.

June

Green season proper, and excellent value. Bright mornings, an afternoon downpour, a clean evening. The waterfalls start to run hard and there is almost nobody about.

July

Warm and wet in the afternoons, and one of the loveliest times to see the country — full rivers, deep green hills and Kuang Si at its best. Pack a light rain shell and footwear you do not mind soaking.

August

The wettest stretch along with September. Fine for the towns, the temples and the main roads; less reliable for unsealed mountain roads in the north and for some river trips. Prices are at their lowest.

September

Still wet and still cheap, with the countryside at its most dramatic. Leave slack in the itinerary if you are going anywhere remote, and do not plan a tight connection off a mountain road.

October

The rains taper off and the country is green, washed and quiet before the season starts. Boun Ok Phansa ends the Buddhist rains retreat with candle-lit floats on the Mekong and boat races at Vientiane and Luang Prabang. An underrated month.

November

The best month of the Lao year. Dry, clear, still green from the monsoon, and before the spring haze. Boun That Luang fills Vientiane around the full moon. Book ahead — everyone who knows, knows.

December

Dry, clear and cool, with cold nights in the north. Christmas and New Year are the busiest and dearest fortnight of the year in Luang Prabang; book months out or come in the first half of the month.

The Burning Haze — the thing most guides underplay

The one seasonal problem worth rearranging a trip around.

What it is

Across the northern uplands, fields and hillsides are burned off before the rains. The smoke has nowhere to go: it pools in the river valleys and sits there. From roughly March into April, in a bad year, the hills behind Luang Prabang and the karst towers at Vang Vieng can be a grey outline or invisible altogether, and the balloon flights that make Vang Vieng famous lose the view they exist for.

What it means for your trip

The temples, the food, the markets and the river are all unaffected, and plenty of people have a fine time in Laos in March. But if your reason for coming is mountain scenery or photography, this is the wrong month, and no amount of optimism will clear the air. Shift to November to February if the views matter, or to the green season if value matters more than crowds.

It varies, so check rather than assume

Severity and timing move with the weather from one year to the next, and some years are much milder than others. The Guild does not print a forecast it cannot stand behind: check current air quality and conditions close to your dates rather than taking a date range — this page included — as a guarantee.

Laos Weather FAQ

The questions travellers ask most when they are choosing a month.

What is the best month to visit Laos?

November, and it is not a close call. The rain has stopped, the countryside is still green from the monsoon, the air is clear before the spring burning starts, the days are warm and the mornings in Luang Prabang are cool. December and January are nearly as good and bring the coolest nights of the year. The trade-off is company and price: this is the busiest stretch, and rooms in Luang Prabang over Christmas and New Year need booking months ahead.

What is the burning season in Laos, and does it matter?

It matters more than any other seasonal fact about Laos, and most itineraries ignore it. From roughly March into April, farmers across the northern uplands burn fields before the rains, and the smoke settles in the valleys. In a bad year the haze can sit over Luang Prabang, Vang Vieng and the northern mountains for weeks, flattening the views, greying the photographs and irritating eyes and chests. It does not make travel impossible and the temples and food are unaffected, but if the mountain scenery is the reason you are coming, do not come in late March. The timing and severity vary from year to year with the weather, so check conditions close to your dates rather than assuming.

Is the green season a bad time to visit Laos?

No, and it is the best value of the year. From June to October it rarely rains all day; the usual pattern is a bright morning, a heavy afternoon downpour and a clear evening. The waterfalls are at full volume, Kuang Si is at its most spectacular, the air is washed clean of haze, the country is brilliantly green and prices fall. The real caveats are narrow: unsealed roads in the north get difficult, some river trips and remote treks are affected, and August and September are the wettest months.

When are the big festivals in Laos?

Pi Mai, Lao New Year, falls in mid-April and is the biggest: three days of water-throwing, temple visits and family travel, when much of the country closes and transport fills up. Boun Bang Fai, the rocket festival, comes in May to call the rains. Boun Ok Phansa marks the end of the Buddhist rains retreat in October, with candle-lit floats on the Mekong and boat racing at Vientiane and Luang Prabang. Boun That Luang fills Vientiane around the November full moon. All of these follow the lunar calendar and move from year to year, so confirm the dates for your year before booking.
