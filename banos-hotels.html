Baños — Hot Springs, a Live Volcano and the Road to the Jungle

Where to Stay in Baños — the Best Areas

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Baños de Agua Santa sits at about 1,800 metres in a gorge on the eastern slope of the Andes, with a live volcano above it and the Amazon basin starting a few bends down the road. It is two towns at once: an adventure capital of canyoning and ziplines, and an old-fashioned spa town where hot mineral water comes straight out of the mountain into public baths. It is also small — you can walk across it in a quarter of an hour — so the choice of where to sleep is less about districts than about whether you want the baths, the view or the waterfalls on your doorstep.

The town centre — walk to the baths and the buses

Baños is a grid of about twelve streets by eight, wedged between the river gorge and the mountain, and the centre is where to be. From it you can walk to the Termas de la Virgen baths beneath the waterfall at the eastern end, to the market, to the bus terminal and to every restaurant in town. Hotels here run from hostels to comfortable mid-range places with hot tubs on the roof, and the whole area is flat. Ask for a room away from the main through-street if you are a light sleeper, because the night buses are real.

By the thermal baths — the eastern end of town

The streets closest to the Termas de la Virgen, under the cliff and the waterfall at the eastern edge of the grid. The advantage is simple and considerable: you can be in hot mineral water five minutes after leaving your room, which is the whole point of coming here, and the baths open very early and run late. It is a slightly steeper corner of town than the middle, and busier in the evenings when day visitors arrive for the water.

The hillside above town — for the view

The slopes above Baños hold a scattering of small lodges and guesthouses looking down on the town, the gorge and, when the cloud lifts, the volcano. They are quiet, often beautiful, and usually have a hot tub pointed at the view. The trade-off is that you are a taxi ride from everything, the road up is steep and narrow, and an evening out means arranging the way back. Worth it for a night or two if the view is what you came for.

Out towards the waterfalls — Río Verde and the gorge road

East of town the road follows the Pastaza gorge down towards the Amazon basin, past a succession of waterfalls, and a few places to stay sit along it — most notably around Río Verde, which is where the walk down to the Pailón del Diablo starts. Staying out here is greener, warmer, much quieter and genuinely remote-feeling, and it puts the best of the waterfall road on your doorstep. You will need transport for everything else.

What to know before you book

Ask whether the hot water in the hotel is thermal or ordinary. Plenty of Baños hotels advertise a hot tub or a pool; some are fed from the mineral springs and some are simply heated. Both are pleasant and they are not the same thing, so ask if it matters to you.

The public baths get busy and they open early. The Termas de la Virgen are at their best not long after dawn and again late in the evening; the middle of the day belongs to the crowds. Take a swimming cap — they are required and sold at the door.

It rains here, and it is the reason everything is green. Baños sits on the eastern slope where Amazon air meets the mountains, so a wet afternoon is normal in any month. Pack a waterproof rather than planning around it.

Volcano and road conditions change. Ashfall can close roads and airports at short notice, and the mountain roads in and out are vulnerable to landslides in heavy rain — and the Foreign Office records that the authorities have issued a red alert ahead of the forecast 2026–2027 El Niño. Leave slack in a tight itinerary, and ask your hotel what the road is doing before a long transfer day.

Nothing here needs booking through us except the bed. Canyoning, rafting, ziplines and the chiva buses are all arranged in town, in person, on the day. We carry no links for them and we take nothing from them.

Baños — questions

Which part of Banos should I stay in?

The town centre, for almost everyone. Banos is small enough to cross on foot in fifteen minutes, and staying in the grid means you can walk to the thermal baths, to dinner and to the bus for the waterfall road. The streets nearest the Termas de la Virgen put you closest to the water; the quieter blocks on the western side of town trade two minutes' walk for a better night's sleep. Stay on the hillside above town only if the view is the point and you are happy to taxi in and out.

Is it safe to stay under an active volcano?

Tungurahua is an active volcano and the town lives with that openly rather than hiding it. Evacuation routes are signposted in the streets, the volcano is monitored continuously by Ecuador's geophysical institute, and the town has been evacuated and returned more than once in living memory. The UK Foreign Office warns generally that Ecuador's highlands carry a high risk from lahars - mixed flows of water, mud, lava and debris - and that ashfall can disrupt flights and close airports at short notice, naming Latacunga, Salcedo and the low-lying areas east of Quito as particularly vulnerable. The honest summary is that Banos is a normal, busy tourist town on a volcano that is watched very closely. Check the current advice before you travel, note the signs when you arrive, and do what the town does if it is ever asked to move.

What is there to do in Banos?

Two quite different things. It is the adventure capital of Ecuador - canyoning in the gorges, rafting on the Pastaza, ziplines, bridge swings, quad bikes and mountain bikes - and it is a spa town, with hot mineral water piped straight out of the mountain into public baths that have been in use for generations. Add the Ruta de las Cascadas, the road east that passes half a dozen waterfalls and ends at the Pailon del Diablo, and a day is easily filled twice over. We do not sell tours or tickets; book those locally.

Is Banos good for older travellers?

Better than its adventure-town reputation suggests. At about 1,800 metres it is noticeably lower and warmer than Quito or Cuenca, which makes it an easier place to spend a few days, and the town centre is largely flat. The thermal baths are the obvious draw and are used by Ecuadorians of every age. The waterfall road can be done by road and by the open-sided chiva buses rather than on a bicycle. What to avoid, unless you want it, is the stair-and-platform end of the Pailon del Diablo walk, which is steep, wet and narrow.

How do I get to Banos?

By road, from Quito in roughly three and a half hours or from Cuenca in rather more, and by frequent long-distance bus into the town's own terminal. There is no airport and no railway. The UK Foreign Office advises against road travel after dark in Ecuador and warns that the country has one of the highest rates of road accidents in Latin America, so travel this route in daylight. Banos is in Tungurahua province, outside the areas the Foreign Office advises against travelling to.
