St John’s

Where to Stay in St John’s

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

St John’s is the capital, the port and the only real town in the country — a working place of markets, minibuses and cruise ships rather than hotels. Almost nobody sleeps in the middle of it. What people mean by staying in St John’s is the coast immediately around it: the shopping quays and the berths in the harbour itself, then the bays running north — Fort James, Runaway, Soldier’s Bay — and the Five Islands peninsula to the west, where two or three resorts each have a beach to themselves. You get the shortest taxi rides on the island, a town five minutes away, and in several cases a far quieter evening than the beach strips.

The areas, and which one suits you

Several distinct places go under this name, and they are not interchangeable.

Downtown and Heritage Quay

The cathedral, the public market, the government offices and the cruise berths, with the duty-free shops of Heritage Quay built right beside the piers. Busy by day, shut up by evening, and with very few places for a visitor to stay. Come for the market on a Saturday morning, not for a bed.

Redcliffe Quay

Two streets back from the water: restored brick-and-timber warehouses from the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, now small shops, bars and restaurants. The prettiest corner of town and the best place in it to eat lunch.

Fort James and Runaway Bay

A few minutes north of town, where the first beaches begin — calm, shallow water, a ruined fort on the point, beach bars at the weekend and a local crowd. Good value, close in, and not glossy.

Soldier’s Bay and the north-west

Sheltered bays a short drive out of town with one or two long-established resorts tucked into them, each with its own stretch of sand and gardens. Quiet, self-contained, and still only ten or fifteen minutes from the capital.

The Five Islands peninsula

West of town across the harbour: Galley Bay, Hawksbill and the bays around them, reached by a single road. Several resorts here face their own beach with nothing else on it. You are close to St John’s as the crow flies and about twenty minutes by car.

Getting about

Minibuses leave the West Bus Station for the whole island and cost very little. Taxi fares are set by zone and drivers will quote the official figure — agree it before you set off. The airport is about fifteen minutes, Dickenson Bay ten, English Harbour forty-five.

What your money is worth in Antigua
US $1 = 2.70 EC$
EC$ 10 = about US $3.70
Antigua and Barbuda uses the Eastern Caribbean dollar, pegged to the US dollar at EC$2.70 — a rate that has held for many years, so a price of EC$27 is about ten US dollars and no app is needed. US dollars are taken almost everywhere, but change often comes back in Eastern Caribbean dollars and paying in local money is usually very slightly better. Cards are fine in hotels, supermarkets and the larger restaurants; keep cash for minibuses, beach bars and the market. A government tax and a service charge are normally added to hotel and restaurant bills, so the final total sits above the headline rate — ask what is included when you book, and at an all-inclusive ask exactly what the word covers here, because it varies a great deal from one resort to the next.

Blue Waters describes itself as a family-owned resort on its own private bay on Antigua’s north-western coast, with a published address at Soldier’s Bay, St John’s. Its own site gives 85 rooms, villas and suites set in seventeen acres of mature tropical gardens, four bars and lounges across the property, and a range of room types from deluxe beachfront rooms up to a group of cove suites and three named houses and cottages. Meal plans include an all-inclusive option rather than all-inclusive being the only way to stay, so check which plan a quoted rate refers to.

The site does not state an age threshold on its home page, so confirm it directly if you are travelling with anyone under eighteen.

Travelling in your later years

An honest assessment of this corner of the island.

St John’s and the bays around it are among the easier places to base yourself on an easy island. The ground is flat or gently sloping, the resorts here are generally laid out along a beach rather than up a hillside, and the airport transfer is about fifteen minutes — which after a long flight is worth more than a view.

The practical advantages of being near the capital are real: the hospital is just outside town, there are pharmacies and supermarkets rather than one hotel shop, and if you want a doctor, a bank or a chemist you are minutes from all three rather than half an hour. Taxi fares being posted by zone also removes the one negotiation most people dislike.

Two things to ask before booking. First, how far the room is from the restaurant and the sand, because the resorts on the peninsula are spread out over large gardens and “beachfront” can still mean a long walk. Second, whether the beach shelves gently — the bays on this coast mostly do, which is one of the reasons we suggest them.

Questions about staying in St John’s

Is it worth staying in St John’s itself?

Only if you particularly want to be in a town. The centre has the market, the cathedral, the shopping quays and the cruise berths, but very little accommodation and almost nothing open in the evening. The sensible version of “staying in St John’s” is one of the bays just outside it — Fort James, Soldier’s Bay or the Five Islands peninsula — which give you a beach and keep the town five to twenty minutes away.

How far is the airport?

About fifteen minutes to St John’s and the bays immediately north of it, and about twenty to twenty-five to the Five Islands peninsula. V. C. Bird International sits on the north-eastern side of the island, so every main hotel coast is a short run — one of the real practical advantages Antigua has over the islands further south.

Is the beach any good this close to town?

Yes, and that surprises people. Fort James and Runaway are proper beaches with calm shallow water a few minutes from the capital, and the resort bays at Soldier’s Bay, Galley Bay and Hawksbill are as good as anything on the island, each with its own stretch of sand. What you do not get here is a long continuous strip — for that, go to Dickenson Bay.

Will the cruise ships spoil it?

In town, on a busy day, Heritage Quay is crowded and the shops are aimed squarely at passengers — Redcliffe Quay a couple of streets away is far pleasanter. Outside town you will barely know the ships are there. If you want to avoid the crush altogether, do your shopping early or on a day when nothing is berthed.

Do I need a car if I stay here?

No. This is the best-connected part of the island: minibuses leave the West Bus Station for everywhere, taxi fares are posted by zone, and most of what you would want to see is within half an hour. Hire a car for a day or two if you want the east coast beaches, and leave it at that.

What about eating out?

Better than the town’s sleepy evenings suggest, but you need to know where to go. Redcliffe Quay has the pick of the central restaurants, and the resorts on this coast all have their own dining rooms. For a wider choice of independent restaurants, English Harbour and Falmouth in the south are the island’s real eating quarter, forty-five minutes away.
