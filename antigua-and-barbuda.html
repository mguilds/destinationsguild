Antigua and Barbuda

Antigua and Barbuda Travel Guide

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Antigua and Barbuda is a two-island country at the top of the Leeward chain — Antigua, about 108 square miles of low rolling hills and sheltered bays, and Barbuda, about 62 square miles of flat coral and sand some 30 miles to the north, with the uninhabited rock of Redonda making up the third name on the coat of arms. Around 93,000 people live here, the great majority of them on Antigua. It has been independent since 1 November 1981 — forty-five years ago this month — and it is English-speaking, with a cricketing tradition out of all proportion to its size. What sets it apart from the green volcanic islands further south is that it is dry and low rather than steep and wet: no rainforest, no long climbs, and instead an indented coastline of calm sandy bays, and at English Harbour on the south coast the Georgian naval dockyard the Royal Navy built to shelter its fleet, inscribed by UNESCO in 2016 and still a working yard. It is the island for people who want a beach, a harbour and an easy week rather than a mountain.

What your money is worth in Antigua
US $1 = 2.70 EC$
EC$ 10 = about US $3.70
Antigua and Barbuda uses the Eastern Caribbean dollar, shared with Saint Lucia, Grenada, Dominica, Saint Kitts and Nevis, Saint Vincent and Anguilla, and pegged to the US dollar at EC$2.70 — a rate that has held for many years, so no app is needed: a price of EC$27 is about ten US dollars. US dollars are taken almost everywhere, but change often comes back in Eastern Caribbean dollars and paying in local money is usually very slightly better. Cards are fine in hotels, supermarkets and the larger restaurants; keep cash for minibuses, beach bars and the market. A government tax and a service charge are normally added to hotel and restaurant bills, so the final total sits above the headline rate — ask what is included when you book.

Why go

Four reasons, and they are not the usual four.

The coastline

Antigua is almost all bays. The coast folds in and out the whole way round, which means sheltered water, calm sand and a great many small beaches rather than one long strip — and because the island is low, you can get to most of them without a climb.

A real harbour town

English Harbour is not a resort pretending to be a port. It is a working sailing harbour with a Georgian dockyard at the head of it, chandlers and sail lofts around it, and crews in and out all winter. Few places in the Caribbean have anything like it.

Easy going

No rainforest road, no ninety-minute transfer, no stairs down a cliff. The airport is fifteen to twenty minutes from the main hotel coasts, the island is small, English is the language and the taxi fares are posted.

Barbuda next door

An hour and a half by boat and you are on an island with almost nobody on it: miles of empty sand, some of it faintly pink, and one of the largest frigate bird colonies in the Caribbean in the lagoon behind it.

At a glance

Where it is

The top of the Leeward Islands, east of Saint Kitts and north of Guadeloupe. About four hours from Miami and eight from London.

Getting there

V. C. Bird International Airport, north-east of St John’s, with direct flights from North America and Europe. Fifteen to twenty-five minutes to most hotel coasts.

Language and money

English. The Eastern Caribbean dollar, pegged at EC$2.70 to the US dollar; US dollars widely accepted.

Size

Antigua about 108 square miles, Barbuda about 62. Nowhere on Antigua is more than an hour from anywhere else.

High ground

Mount Obama, about 1,319 feet, in the south-west. The rest is hills and flat coastal plain.

Driving

On the left, on a temporary local licence the hire company arranges. Taxis are set by zone and minibuses are very cheap.

The regions, and which coast suits you

Six parts of the country, and they want quite different holidays.

St John’s and the north-west

The capital, the market, the cathedral and the cruise berths, with the hotel bays beginning immediately outside town at Fort James, Soldier’s Bay and Dickenson Bay. The most convenient base on the island and the shortest taxi rides.

Dickenson Bay and Runaway Bay

The classic beach strip: a long curve of calm, shallow sand with hotels, beach bars and water sports along it. Busier than most of Antigua and the easiest place to spend a week without a car.

The west coast

Jolly Harbour’s marina village of villas and apartments, with a supermarket, restaurants and a golf course, and either side of it the quiet luxury bays — Hermitage Bay, Galley Bay, the Five Islands peninsula — each with a beach of its own.

English and Falmouth Harbours

The south coast and the sailing end. Nelson’s Dockyard, the superyacht quays at Falmouth, Shirley Heights above both, and the best eating on the island. Fewer long beaches; much more going on.

The east and the Atlantic side

Half Moon Bay, Long Bay and Devil’s Bridge: wilder water, stiffer breeze, far fewer people. Half Moon Bay in particular is one of the finest beaches in the Caribbean and has almost nothing built on it.

Barbuda

Flat, dry, scarcely populated, reached by passenger ferry or a short flight. Miles of untouched sand, the frigate bird colony in Codrington Lagoon, and a village still rebuilding from Hurricane Irma in 2017. Go for the emptiness, not for the facilities.

Things to do

Nelson’s Dockyard

The restored Georgian naval yard at English Harbour, a UNESCO World Heritage site since 2016 and still working. The museum, the Admiral’s House, the stone pillars of the old sail loft, and yachts tied up where men-of-war once were.

Shirley Heights

The old gun battery on the ridge above English Harbour, with the finest view in Antigua — both harbours, the south coast and Montserrat and Guadeloupe on a clear day. There is a long-running barbecue with live music up there on Sunday evenings; check the current days and the entry charge before setting out, as both have changed over the years.

Beaches, plural

Spend a day going round them rather than staying on one. Half Moon Bay and Long Bay on the east, Darkwood and Ffryes on the west, Pigeon Point near Falmouth, Dickenson Bay in the north — all public, all free to walk on.

Going sailing

This is a sailing island, and you do not need to be a sailor. Day charters, catamaran trips round the island and crewed yachts all run out of Jolly Harbour, Falmouth and St John’s, and a snorkelling sail is the single best way to see the coast.

Betty’s Hope

The remains of a sugar estate inland, with one of its twin windmill towers restored and turning. It is the plainest and most honest account of where the island’s wealth came from, and it takes less than an hour.

Barbuda for a day

The ferry from St John’s takes about an hour and a half each way, which makes a day trip long but possible; a light aircraft turns it into a short hop. Schedules change, so confirm the crossing before you commit to a date.

The dry season

Mid-December to April: the driest, breeziest, most reliable weather and the highest prices. February and the weeks around Christmas and Easter are the peak.

The wet season

June to November: hot, humid, with heavy showers rather than all-day rain, and the Atlantic hurricane season from 1 June to 30 November. Prices fall a long way and some hotels close for part of September and October.

Arriving by ship

Antigua is a regular call on eastern Caribbean itineraries, and St John’s is one of the easier ports to walk out of.

Ships tie up at St John’s, a few minutes on foot from the middle of town. The berths sit immediately beside the Heritage Quay shopping complex, and the restored brick-and-timber warehouses of Redcliffe Quay are a couple of streets away, so you can be having a cold drink in the shade ten minutes after you step off. The port opened a new cruise terminal in January 2026, alongside a fifth berth — a 700-foot pier designed to take the largest ships afloat — so expect more company in the harbour than Antigua used to have.

With one day, pick one of three. South to English Harbour and Shirley Heights is the most rewarding and takes about forty-five minutes each way by road: the dockyard, the museum and the view from the ridge. A beach day is the simplest — Dickenson Bay in the north is about fifteen minutes, or Fort James closer still. A boat trip is the third, and on an island shaped like this one it is a good answer: catamarans run snorkelling and coastal sails timed around ship calls.

Agree the taxi fare before you get in, and give yourself more margin getting back than the driver suggests. If you are flying in the night before a sailing, our Caribbean cruise guide covers which lines call here and when, and the Guild cruise shelf has the rest of the fleet.

Travelling in your later years

An honest assessment, and this is one of the islands we recommend most readily.

The terrain is the reason. Antigua is low and gently rolling, so the hotels are generally built along a beach rather than down a cliff, and the walk from the room to the water is usually short and level. That is not true of the famous hillside resorts on the volcanic islands further south, and it is the single most useful thing to know about this one.

The rest helps too: English is the language, the airport is fifteen to twenty-five minutes from the main hotel coasts rather than an hour and a half, taxi fares are posted by zone so there is nothing to haggle over, and hotels here are thoroughly used to older guests and will arrange transfers and excursions. Medical care is adequate for ordinary needs, with the hospital outside St John’s; anything serious means an air evacuation, so travel insurance that covers it is not optional.

Three things to plan around. The heat and humidity from June to October are real, and a room with reliable air conditioning matters more than a view. Several of the best beaches on the east coast have no shade and no facilities at all — take water. And if steps are a problem, ask before booking how many there are between the room, the restaurant and the sand, because a handful of properties here are built up a slope even on a flat island.

Getting in

Straightforward for most visitors, with two things worth doing before you fly.

You need a passport and a return or onward ticket. Many authorities ask that the passport still have six months to run on the day you arrive, so check yours before you book. Visitors from the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom and the European Union do not need a visa for an ordinary holiday; British passport holders are admitted for stays of up to 90 days. Other nationalities should check with an Antigua and Barbuda mission before travelling.

Arrival and customs declarations have moved online in recent years and the arrangements have changed more than once, so confirm the current requirement with your airline or the Antigua and Barbuda authorities when you book rather than trusting an address printed in a guide, including this one. Departure tax is normally included in the price of an air ticket.

If you intend to drive, you must buy a temporary local driving licence on production of your home licence; the car hire companies issue them when you collect the car. Traffic keeps to the left. Arriving on your own boat is different: the port authority expects to hear from you on VHF channel 16 well before you enter, and clearance is done at a designated port of entry.

Fishing here

Antigua and Barbuda gives you both kinds of saltwater fishing, which few places this size do. Off Antigua the shelf drops away and the charter boats out of Falmouth, Jolly Harbour and St John’s go after wahoo, mahi-mahi, tuna, kingfish and marlin. Barbuda is the other thing entirely: flat, shallow, wadeable sand and turtle grass, and a genuine bonefish fishery in and around Codrington Lagoon that serious anglers travel a long way for. Our guide to fishing the flats covers what that kind of fishing asks of you, and The Guild Fishing Library holds the whole shelf: lakes, rivers and salt.

Questions travellers ask about Antigua and Barbuda

When is the best time to visit Antigua?

Mid-December to April is the dry season and the reliable half of the year: steady sunshine, lower humidity, a dependable trade wind — and the highest room rates. June to November is hotter and wetter and is the Atlantic hurricane season, which runs from 1 June to 30 November. Our own picks are the shoulders: the first half of December, and the back half of April into May, when the weather is still dry, Sailing Week has the harbours alive and rooms cost noticeably less than in February.

Is Antigua really flat, and does that matter?

Mostly, and yes it matters — in Antigua's favour. Unlike the volcanic Windward islands to the south, Antigua is low and dry: rolling hills rather than mountains, with the highest ground at Mount Obama at about 1,319 feet. There is no rainforest and no hour-long climb between your room and the beach. For anyone who would rather not negotiate tiers of steps on a hillside, Antigua is one of the easier islands in the eastern Caribbean.

How many beaches does Antigua have?

The tourist board's line is 365, one for every day of the year, and nobody has ever produced the list. Treat it as a good slogan rather than a count. What is true is that the island is ringed with sheltered sandy bays on every coast, that a great many of them are small and quiet, and that all beaches in Antigua are public below the high-water mark even where a resort sits behind them.

Should I stay on Antigua or Barbuda?

Antigua, for almost everyone, on almost every trip. Antigua has the airport, the hotels, the restaurants, the harbours and the history. Barbuda is a day trip or a deliberate retreat: flat, empty, barely inhabited, with extraordinary beaches, a vast frigate bird colony in Codrington Lagoon and very little else — which is the whole point of it. If Barbuda is what you have come for, go for several nights rather than one, and book a long way ahead.

Do I need to hire a car?

Not necessarily. Taxi fares are set by zone and drivers will quote the official figure, minibuses run cheaply along the main roads, and if you are staying put at a resort you may never need a vehicle. If you do drive, you must buy a temporary local driving licence, which the hire companies arrange when you collect the car, and traffic keeps to the left. The roads are generally good on the main routes and rough and unlit off them.

What is Nelson's Dockyard, and is it worth a morning?

It is the reason Antigua has a place in naval history, and yes. English Harbour is a deep, hurricane-proof inlet the Royal Navy used as its Caribbean base, and the Georgian dockyard built around it is still a working yard — the only one of its kind anywhere. UNESCO inscribed it in 2016 as the Antigua Naval Dockyard and Related Archaeological Sites. Give it a couple of hours with the museum, then take the road up to Shirley Heights for the view down over both harbours.

What does a week in Antigua cost?

It spans a very wide range. The island carries some of the Caribbean's best-known luxury resorts, and in February a beachfront room at one of them is a serious sum; the same room in September can be a different proposition entirely. Below that there are mid-range hotels, apartments and villas, particularly around Jolly Harbour, at a fraction of the price. Budget for a government tax and a service charge on top of hotel and restaurant bills, and ask what is included before you book rather than at check-out.

Is Antigua safe, and what about the water?

Ordinary sense covers it: Antigua is a settled, friendly place where most visitors have no trouble at all, and the usual advice about valuables on a beach and unlit roads at night applies as it would anywhere. Water is the more practical point. Antigua is one of the drier islands and much of its supply is desalinated, so be sparing with it — if a hotel asks you to go easy on the shower and the towels, it is not a gesture.
