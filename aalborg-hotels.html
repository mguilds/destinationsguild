Where to Stay in Aalborg

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Aalborg is north Jutland’s city and the gateway to the top of Denmark, and it is a far better place to spend a night than it was twenty years ago — the town took the traffic off its fjord-side and put a park there instead. The centre is small and the choice is simple: the waterfront, the quieter streets by the station, or across the bridge for less money.

The centre and the waterfront

Most convenientWalk everywhereThe fjord at the door

Aalborg pulled down the dual carriageway along its fjord and put a long waterfront park in its place, and the difference to the town is hard to overstate. Staying in the centre now means the water, the Utzon Centre, the old half-timbered merchant houses on Jomfru Ane Gade and the pedestrian streets are all within a few minutes. This is where the hotel rooms are, and the right answer for almost everyone.

Around the station and Vestbyen

Near the trainsQuieterBest value

The station is five minutes south of the centre, and the streets west of it — Vestbyen — are an older residential quarter of low houses and small gardens running down to the water. It is quieter than the middle, the rooms cost less, and you are still within a ten-minute walk of everything. The practical choice if you are arriving by train or moving on north to Skagen.

Nørresundby, across the fjord

Across the bridgeCheaperNeeds the bus or a walk

The far bank of the Limfjord is a separate town joined to Aalborg by a bridge and a tunnel, with the Lindholm Høje Viking burial ground on the hill above it — several hundred stone ship settings in a field, and one of the best Viking-age sites in Denmark. There are rooms over here and they are cheaper, but you will cross the bridge every time you want dinner.

Getting around

The centre is flat and walkable end to end in about twenty minutes, and city buses cover the rest on a ticket bought in the app or at a machine. The airport is a short bus or taxi ride north-west. Regional trains run north from the main station to Frederikshavn and Skagen, which is how most visitors go on to the dunes.

Bicycles are easy to hire and the ground is flat. A car is useful here in a way it is not in Copenhagen, because the coast, the Rubjerg Knude dune and the west-facing beaches are spread out and thinly served by bus.

When to come, and what it costs

North Jutland is a summer destination and Aalborg follows its coast. June to August is when the waterfront is in use, the beaches are open and the trains to Skagen are full; late May and early September give the same long light with far fewer people. The town is a university city, so it is livelier in term than in the holidays.

Winter here is dark, wet and windy, and a good deal of the coastal trade shuts. Aalborg itself stays open and is cheap in those months, but the dunes and Skagen are for people who positively want an empty beach in a gale. Our month-by-month guide to Denmark sets out the year.

Denmark uses the Danish krone (DKK, written kr), not the euro. Denmark is a member of the European Union but voted to keep its own currency and has set no date for changing it, so euro notes are not legal tender — though the krone is held in a fixed band against the euro, which is why the rate barely moves from one year to the next. Pay in kroner. Denmark is one of the most nearly cashless countries in the world: cards and phones are taken everywhere, including on buses, at museum desks and at market stalls, and a good many places no longer handle notes at all, so you need very little cash and may need none. Prices in shops include the twenty-five per cent sales tax, which is among the highest in Europe and is already in the figure on the shelf. Tipping is not expected and never chased — service is in the bill, and rounding up is plenty. Decline any card machine that offers to charge you in dollars; pay in kroner and let your own bank convert.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS — the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years — is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today — nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Denmark has been in the European Union since 1973 and in the Schengen area since 2001, so everything above applies here in full and an arrival from anywhere else in Schengen involves no passport control at all. Denmark did not join the euro, so the currency is the krone. If you plan to cross to Malmö in Sweden, carry your passport even though the bridge is inside Schengen: Sweden has run identity checks on that crossing on and off for years.

Aalborg hotels FAQ

Is Aalborg worth visiting?

More than its reputation suggests. The waterfront rebuild turned a working industrial town into a pleasant one to walk in, the old streets around Jomfru Ane Gade are genuinely handsome, and Lindholm Høje across the fjord is a first-rate Viking site. It is also the natural base for the top of Jutland, which is the real reason most people come this far north.

Where should I stay in Aalborg?

The centre, near the waterfront, for almost every visitor — it is where the rooms, the restaurants and the water all are. The station side and Vestbyen for a quieter street and a lower rate within a ten-minute walk. Nørresundby across the bridge only if the price is worth the crossing to you.

How do I get to Aalborg?

By train from Copenhagen in about four and a half hours, with direct departures from the airport, or by air to Aalborg’s own airport, which takes domestic flights and a handful of European ones and sits about twenty minutes from the centre. By road it is the end of the motorway north from Aarhus.

Can I get to Skagen from Aalborg?

Yes — it is the usual way. A regional train runs north from Aalborg to Frederikshavn and on to Skagen in about two hours in total, and the line is the easiest approach to the top of Denmark without a car. In high summer the trains are busy and worth boarding early.
