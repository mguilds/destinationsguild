Where to Stay in Salzburg

Where to Stay in Salzburg — Best Hotels & Neighbourhoods

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated August 2026

Salzburg is a small city that punches far above its size, and the whole of it that a visitor wants is squeezed into a narrow strip between a cliff and a river. That geography is the single most useful thing to understand before you book. The old town on the left bank — Getreidegasse, the cathedral square, Mozart’s birthplace — is beautiful, pedestrianised and entirely cobbled, with a fortress on the rock above it and buildings so old that a lift is a genuine question rather than an assumption. The right bank, around Linzer Gasse and the Mirabell Gardens, is five flat minutes away over a bridge, a little cheaper, easier underfoot, and where we send most first-time visitors who are not twenty-five any more. Everything below is arranged by style, with an honest note on stairs, noise and distance, and a Book Me link straight to Trip.com.

Which side of the river to sleep on

The left bank is the postcard: the cathedral, Getreidegasse, Mozart’s birthplace, the fortress rock. It is also cobbled from end to end, closed to cars, and full of protected buildings where the staircase is original and the lift is not. The right bank — Linzer Gasse, Mirabell Gardens, the Kapuzinerberg foot — is a five-minute walk across a flat bridge from all of that, is easier on the feet, has a broader range of prices, and is where the larger modern hotels are. Unless you have your heart set on waking up inside the old town, the right bank is the kinder choice.

Lifts are the question, not the stars

This matters more in Salzburg than in almost any city we cover. Old-town houses are four and five storeys of protected fabric, and a hotel can be excellent, expensive and still ask you to climb a flight to reach the lift — or have no lift at all. Do not assume, and do not rely on a booking site’s tick box. Write to the hotel and ask three plain questions: is there a step at the front door, does the lift reach the ground floor, and does it reach my room floor. Every good Salzburg hotel answers that cheerfully.

The Festival, and what it does to prices

The summer Salzburg Festival takes over the city for roughly six weeks from the second half of July through August: the finest music you will hear anywhere, and the worst time to arrive without a plan. Rooms are booked a year ahead, rates rise steeply, and restaurants need reserving. If you are coming for the music, book early and accept the price. If you are not, come in late May, June or September instead and have the same city at half the fuss. The Easter Festival is a smaller, shorter affair — 20 to 29 March in 2027.

Getting here, and the hill you do not have to climb

Salzburg’s own airport is small; many Americans fly into Munich and take the train, which runs to Salzburg in about an hour and a half. From Vienna the Railjet takes about two hours and twenty-five minutes, and from Innsbruck about one hour fifty. All of them arrive at the main station, a flat fifteen-minute walk or a short bus ride from the centre. And the fortress on the rock does not have to be climbed: a funicular railway runs up from the old town, and a separate lift inside the Mönchsberg cliff carries you to the terrace and the modern art museum for the price of a bus ticket.

Luxury

Two grand houses, one inside the old town and one facing it across the water — and the river-bank one is the easier stay.

Hotel Sacher Salzburg

$$$$ Grand luxuryOn the right bankFacing the old townFamily-ownedDogs genuinely welcomed

The Salzburg sister of the famous Vienna house, standing on the right bank with its best rooms looking straight across the Salzach at the old town and the fortress — which is, we would argue, a better view than being inside the picture. It is a proper grand hotel: doormen, lifts, a café that serves the Sacher-Torte, and a level of service that makes travelling at any age easier. The old town is a flat five-minute walk over the Staatsbrücke bridge. Dogs are genuinely welcomed here rather than tolerated, with beds and bowls provided.

Boutique

Two independent houses of real character — one in the oldest inn building in the city, one with a garden on the quiet side of the river.

ArtHotel Blaue Gans

$$$ BoutiqueGetreidegasse, old townInnkeeping since 1350Contemporary art throughoutSteps from the Festival halls

The oldest inn building in Salzburg — the records go back to about 1350 — run today as a quietly confident art hotel, with a serious contemporary collection hung along the corridors and vaulted rooms that are white and modern rather than folksy. The position is excellent for music: the Festival halls are around the corner, and the Mönchsberg lift is a minute away. Rooms vary a great deal in size and shape, as they must in a building this old, so describe what you need when you book. Cobbles outside, and a pedestrian lane, so arrive by taxi to the end of the street.

Budget & Value

Two modern, lift-served hotels on the right bank, both a flat walk from the old town — the sensible way to afford Salzburg.

Motel One Salzburg-Mirabell

The most reliable inexpensive bed in central Salzburg. Motel One trades room size for everything else: good beds, a handsome lobby-bar to sit in, a proper lift, and a position by the Mirabell Gardens that puts the old town about ten level minutes away over the bridge. Rooms are small and have showers rather than baths, and breakfast is charged separately and worth taking. If your budget is the reason you have been putting Salzburg off, this is the answer — and the money saved covers a Festival ticket.

Family-friendly

Modern hotels with room to spread out and lifts that take a pushchair — the practical choice when three generations travel together.

The easiest big hotel in Salzburg to actually stay in: a modern building next to the Mirabell Gardens with wide corridors, large lifts, level access and rooms that an American will find a normal size. There is a spa and an indoor pool, which earns its keep on a wet afternoon with grandchildren, and the gardens next door are free, flat and full of fountains. Adults-only & quiet

Salzburg has one genuinely adults-only hotel, and we say so plainly rather than pad the list. The second here is a quiet, romantic house — not adults-only.

Hotel Stein – Adults Only

$$$ Adults-onlyGenuinely adults-onlyOn the river at the bridgeRooftop bar with fortress viewsOld town two minutes away

The one hotel in Salzburg that really is adults-only, and happily it is also one of the best positioned: on the right bank at the foot of the Staatsbrücke, so the old town is a two-minute walk over the bridge and the Linzer Gasse restaurants are behind you. The rooftop bar looks straight at the fortress and is one of the finest sunset seats in Austria — it is popular, so go early. Rooms are contemporary and vary in size; the building is historic, so ask about the lift if that matters. Quiet inside, lively outside.

Hotel Schloss Mönchstein

$$$$ Castle hotelPrivate woodland above the cityVery quiet and romanticNot adults-onlyYou are up a hill

A small castle in private woods on the Mönchsberg, directly above the old town but a world away from it: gardens, long views, a fine restaurant and about as much peace as you can buy within a mile of a famous city centre. It is romantic in a way few hotels manage honestly. Two plain cautions. It is not adults-only, whatever the atmosphere suggests. And you are on top of a cliff — the way down is the Mönchsberg lift or the hotel’s car, not a stroll, so this suits a restful two or three nights rather than a sightseeing dash.

Salzburg Hotels — common questions

Should I stay in the old town or across the river in Salzburg?

Across the river, for most older travellers. The old town on the left bank is the beautiful half and the one everybody photographs, but it is cobbled throughout, closed to cars, and full of protected buildings where lifts are small or absent. The right bank around Linzer Gasse and the Mirabell Gardens is a flat five-minute walk from all of it, has easier pavements, a wider range of prices and the larger modern hotels. Stay in the old town if the address matters to you more than the stairs do — and if it does, ask about the lift before you book.

Is Salzburg walkable for older travellers?

Yes, with one warning. The city is small and mostly flat along both riverbanks, and everything a visitor wants is within about twenty minutes on foot — you will not need a car or much public transport. The warning is the surface: the old town is laid in cobbles and setts that are uneven and slippery in rain, and wheeled cases hate them. Flat, cushioned, grippy shoes matter more here than fitness does. The fortress and the Mönchsberg terrace are reached by funicular and by a lift inside the cliff, so no climbing is required.

How do I get to Salzburg from Vienna or Munich?

By train, in both cases. Railjet services run from Vienna to Salzburg in about two hours and twenty-five minutes, several times an hour at the busy end of the day, and land you at the main station a flat fifteen-minute walk from the centre. From Munich the journey is about an hour and a half. Book a few weeks ahead on the Austrian railway’s own site or app for the cheap advance fares, and take a seat reservation in summer and December. Flying between these cities makes no sense at all.

When is the best time to visit Salzburg?

Late May and June, or September, when the weather is kind, the days are long and the city is busy but not besieged. July and August bring the Salzburg Festival: glorious if you have tickets, expensive and crowded if you do not. December is the other special case — the Christmas markets in the cathedral square and by the fortress are among the loveliest in Europe, at the price of short days and thick weekend crowds. January to March is quiet and cold, and the mountains nearby are in ski season.

Do I need a car in Salzburg?

Not for the city, and it will be a nuisance if you have one: the old town is pedestrianised, parking is expensive and mostly in garages under the cliff, and everything is within walking distance anyway. A car earns its keep only if you are heading out to the Salzkammergut lakes — Hallstatt, St Wolfgang, Bad Ischl — where buses and trains exist but are slow and thin out in the evening. The sensible pattern is to see Salzburg on foot, then pick up a car for two or three days in the lake country.

How many days do you need in Salzburg?

Two nights is enough for the old town, Mozart’s houses, the fortress and a concert; three lets you add Hellbrunn or a half-day in the lakes without rushing. Salzburg is small, and after three days most visitors are ready to move on to the mountains or the lakes rather than dig deeper. It also pairs beautifully with Vienna at the far end of a two-and-a-half-hour train ride, which is why so many trips through Austria are built on exactly that pair.
