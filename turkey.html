Türkiye — Two Continents, Cave Towns & a Turquoise Coast

Turkey Travel Guide — Where to Stay, Region by Region

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Türkiye (Turkey) is a country most people visit once and then plan to return to. Istanbul stands on two continents and holds two imperial capitals’ worth of monuments. An hour inland, Cappadocia is a landscape of carved volcanic rock where you sleep in a cave and watch balloons lift at dawn. South, the Mediterranean runs along the foot of the mountains past Roman cities and very clear water, and west, the Aegean holds Ephesus and the harbour towns. Below is each region, where to sleep in it, and the honest word on distance, season and crowds that decides whether a trip works.

Why visit Türkiye

Because very few countries hand you this much variety inside one set of borders and one time zone. In a fortnight you can stand inside a sixth-century cathedral that spent five hundred years as a mosque, sleep in a room cut out of volcanic rock, walk a Roman main street with the ruts of cart wheels still in it, and swim off a coast the colour of a swimming pool. The food is genuinely one of the world’s great cuisines and is cheap at the ordinary end of it. And Türkiye is used to visitors — the tourism industry here is a century old, the transport works, and in the places in this guide you will not be the first foreigner anyone has met this week.

The honest counterweight: it is a big country and the distances are real. Istanbul to Cappadocia is a flight, not a drive. The famous sights are genuinely crowded from June to September. And the lira has moved a great deal in recent years, which is why you will not find a single price in this guide — see “at a glance” below.

Türkiye at a glance

Language

Turkish. English is widely spoken in the tourist districts of Istanbul, in Cappadocia and along the resort coast, and much less so inland and in ordinary neighbourhoods. A few words of Turkish are received very warmly.

Money

The Turkish lira (TRY). Inflation has been high for several years, so any price printed in a guidebook — ours included — dates fast. We do not print prices. Cards are accepted almost everywhere in cities; carry some cash for village cafes, markets and small museums.

Time and plugs

One time zone for the whole country, UTC+3 all year — Türkiye no longer changes its clocks seasonally. Sockets are the two-round-pin European type at 230 volts, so UK and North American travellers need an adapter.

Getting in by air

Istanbul has two airports: Istanbul Airport (IST) on the European side and Sabiha Gökçen (SAW) on the Asian side — they are a long way apart, so check which one your ticket says. Antalya (AYT) serves the coast; Cappadocia is served by Kayseri (ASR) and Nevşehir (NAV).

Getting around

Domestic flights are frequent and are how most visitors cross the country. Intercity coaches are comfortable and go everywhere. Istanbul itself is best done on foot, on the tram and on the ferries, not in a car.

Manners that matter

Türkiye is a secular republic with a mostly Muslim population. Working mosques welcome visitors outside prayer times: shoes off, shoulders and knees covered, and a scarf over the hair for women — most large mosques lend one at the door.

The four Türkiyes

Deciding the region comes before deciding the hotel. These are the four that first and second visits are usually built from.

1. Istanbul and the Marmara

The only city in the world standing on two continents, and the reason most people come. The old city of Sultanahmet holds Hagia Sophia, the Blue Mosque, Topkapı Palace and the Basilica Cistern within a few streets of one another. Across the Golden Horn, Beyoğlu, Galata and Karaköy are where the city eats and drinks in the evening. Up the Bosphorus, Beşiktaş and the villages beyond it are the water-facing, expensive, quietly grand Istanbul. Three full days is the minimum; a week is not too long. Our guide: where to stay in Istanbul.

2. Central Anatolia and Cappadocia

An hour’s flight inland, a landscape of soft volcanic rock that wind and water have cut into cones, ridges and valleys, and that people have been carving houses, churches and whole underground cities out of for two thousand years. You sleep in the rock here — cave hotels are the point, not a gimmick. Hot-air balloons lift at dawn when the wind allows. Two nights is enough to see it; three lets you lose a morning to weather and still fly. Our guide: where to stay in Cappadocia.

3. The Mediterranean coast and Lycia

The south coast, where the mountains come down to very clear water and classical ruins sit in the pine woods above the beaches. Antalya is the gateway and a real city with a walled Ottoman quarter at its heart; west of it the Lycian coast runs through Kaş, Kalkan and Fethiye, with the Lycian Way footpath above it and gulet boats below. East of Antalya the big all-inclusive resort strips at Belek, Side and Alanya are a different kind of holiday altogether — excellent at what they do, and not a touring base. Our guide: where to stay in Antalya.

4. The Aegean

The west coast and the classical sites: Ephesus, the best-preserved Roman city in the eastern Mediterranean, reached from Selçuk; the white terraces of Pamukkale; Bergama, Priene and Didyma; and the harbour towns and peninsulas around Bodrum and Çeşme. İzmir is the regional city and the usual flight in. The Guild does not yet have a book for this coast; it is next on the list for this country.

Not covered here, honestly rather than quietly: the Black Sea coast and its green mountains, and the east and southeast, which hold some of the country’s most remarkable places. Several governments publish specific travel advice about provinces on the Syrian and Iraqi borders. Read your own government’s current advice before planning anything in that part of the country — it is written for your passport, it changes, and it is more use to you than a sentence from us.

Things to do in Türkiye

The Guild does not yet sell tours, so nothing in this section is a booking link — it is simply what we would make time for.

In Istanbul: Hagia Sophia and the Blue Mosque in the same morning, early, before the queues; Topkapı Palace and its treasury; the Basilica Cistern; an ordinary commuter ferry up the Bosphorus, which is the cheapest great view in Europe; the Grand Bazaar for an hour and the Spice Bazaar for twenty minutes; and an evening walking between the meyhanes off İstiklal Caddesi.

In Cappadocia: the rock-cut churches of the Göreme Open-Air Museum, with their frescoes; the underground cities at Derinkuyu and Kaymaklı, which go down many storeys; the walk down the Red and Rose valleys at the end of the day; and the balloons at dawn if the wind lets them fly.

On the coast: Ephesus, given a whole morning; Pamukkale’s terraces; the old town and harbour at Antalya; the Düden waterfalls; the Roman theatre at Aspendos, which is still used for performances; and a day on a boat anywhere between Fethiye and Kaş.

Best time to visit & getting there

The short version: April to early June and September to October are the two good windows almost everywhere. Spring and autumn give you warm days on the coast, comfortable walking weather at Ephesus and in Istanbul, and Cappadocia without either the summer heat or the winter ice. July and August are hot — genuinely hard work on the archaeological sites in the middle of the day — and are also when the coast is fullest and dearest. Winter is quiet, cheap and often lovely in Istanbul, and Cappadocia under snow is a sight in itself, though balloon flights and some rural roads are weather-dependent.

Fishing here

Travelling in your later years

Türkiye rewards an unhurried traveller, but a few things are worth knowing before you book. Istanbul is built on hills and cobbles; Sultanahmet in particular is lovely and hard underfoot, and many small hotels in the old city are converted houses with stairs and no lift. Ask the question directly before booking — our Istanbul guide says which districts are flattest. Cappadocia is the one to think hardest about: cave rooms often mean steps cut in rock, uneven floors and low doorways, and the valley walks are proper walks. The ridge hotels at Uçhisar have the views but also the climb. Antalya is the gentlest of the three, with flat seafront promenades, level modern hotels and a compact old town.

Elsewhere: the archaeological sites are mostly unshaded and uneven — Ephesus is a kilometre of marble downhill — so go early, carry water and treat the middle of the day as a rest. Pharmacies are everywhere, well stocked and used to advising walk-in customers. And the coaches, which are comfortable and have a steward, are often an easier way between cities than an airport at either end.

Getting in: what to expect at the border

We are not going to print a visa rule for you, and here is why. Entry requirements for Türkiye are set by the Republic of Türkiye, they differ by passport, and they have changed more than once in recent years. The official channels are the Republic of Türkiye’s electronic visa service at evisa.gov.tr and your nearest Turkish embassy or consulate. When we tried to read the official pages while writing this guide, the government’s own site refused our request — so rather than repeat what a travel blog says, we are sending you to the source. Check it for your own passport, close to your travel dates.

What we can tell you is the shape of it. Most visitors arrive by air into Istanbul, Antalya or one of the coastal airports; passport control at Istanbul Airport is large, well signed and can be slow at peak hours. Your passport will generally need a decent amount of validity left on it beyond your trip — again, the exact requirement is on the official site. Keep the card or stamp you are given on arrival. If you are driving in, or arriving by sea on a cruise, the rules can differ from the air arrival rules, which is one more reason to check rather than assume.

Türkiye — questions

How long do I need in Türkiye?

Ten days is the comfortable minimum for a first visit built from Istanbul and one other region — three or four nights in Istanbul, two or three in Cappadocia, and the travelling between them. A fortnight lets you add the coast without rushing. A long weekend in Istanbul alone is a perfectly good trip and many people do exactly that; what does not work is trying to fit Istanbul, Cappadocia, Ephesus and the Mediterranean into one week, because the distances are flights rather than drives.

Is Istanbul enough on its own, or should I add Cappadocia?

Istanbul alone justifies the flight, and if you only have four or five days we would stay put and go deeper rather than add an internal flight. If you have ten days or more, Cappadocia is the addition we would make first: it is an hour in the air, it looks like nowhere else, and it is the strongest contrast to the city. The coast is the better second addition if you want a holiday rather than a tour.

Do I need a visa for Türkiye?

That depends on your passport and it has changed in recent years, so we deliberately do not print a rule here. Check the Republic of Türkiye's own electronic visa service at evisa.gov.tr, or your nearest Turkish embassy or consulate, close to your travel dates. We would rather send you to the source than have you rely on a sentence from us that may be a year out of date.

Is Türkiye expensive?

At the ordinary end — local restaurants, public transport, museums, domestic flights, simple hotels — it is inexpensive by North American and Western European standards, and the food in particular is very good value. At the top end, Bosphorus hotels in Istanbul and the better cave hotels in Cappadocia are priced like anywhere else in the world. The lira has moved a great deal in recent years, which is why you will not find a single price in this guide: anything we printed would be wrong by the time you read it.

Is it easy to get around?

Yes, more easily than most first-time visitors expect. Domestic flights between Istanbul, Kayseri or Nevşehir for Cappadocia, and Antalya or İzmir for the coast are frequent and cheap. Intercity coaches are comfortable, run everywhere and are a genuine alternative for shorter hops. Istanbul itself is best handled on the tram, the metro and the ferries with a rechargeable travel card; driving in the city is for the brave and is not necessary.
