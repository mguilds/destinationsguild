Where to Stay in Lisbon

Where to Stay in Lisbon — Best Hotels & Neighbourhoods

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated August 2026

Lisbon is one of the great walking cities of Europe and one of the most punishing, sometimes on the same street. It is built across seven hills above the Tagus, and its pavements are laid in the calçada portuguesa — small limestone blocks, beautiful to look at and polished glassy by two centuries of feet. Where you sleep therefore matters more here than in most capitals. The choice is really between five quarters: Baixa, the flat grid rebuilt after the 1755 earthquake, and the easiest ground in the city; Chiado, handsome, central and gently sloping; Príncipe Real, the most civilised address but set high on a hill; Alfama, romantic and genuinely stepped; and Belém, flat, green and six kilometres west among the monuments. Below are the hotels we would send our own family to, by style, each with an honest note and a Book Me link straight to Trip.com.

Flat Baixa, steep everything else

Lisbon divides sharply underfoot. Baixa is the exception — a level grid running from Rossio down to the river, and the single easiest quarter for anyone unsure on their feet. Avenida da Liberdade is broad and gently graded; Chiado slopes moderately. Alfama and Bairro Alto are steep, stepped and slippery in rain. Use the Glória, Bica and Lavra funiculars and the Santa Justa lift without the slightest embarrassment — locals do. Many visitors adopt a simple rule: walk down, ride up.

Take the metro in from the airport

Humberto Delgado is barely seven kilometres from the centre, one of the closest city airports in Europe. The metro runs from the terminal on the Red line, changing at Alameda to the Green line, and reaches Baixa-Chiado in roughly twenty to twenty-five minutes for under two euros. A taxi to Praça do Comércio is ten to fourteen euros by day and takes fifteen to twenty-five minutes. The old AeroBus shuttle was discontinued in 2022, so do not plan around it — some older guidebooks still list it.

Spring and autumn, not August

Late April through June, and mid-September into October, are the comfortable months: warm days in the seventies, cool evenings, and queues you can live with. July and August push into the mid-eighties and higher, the heat sits trapped in the hill streets, the tram queues are at their worst, and a good share of the best neighbourhood restaurants close while their owners take their own holidays. Winter is mild — around sixty degrees — and cheap, but it is the wet season, so plan flexibly.

Where the noise is

Bairro Alto is the loudest address in Lisbon: the bars serve until two or three and the drinkers stand in the lanes. Cais do Sodré’s Pink Street is much the same, and Praça Luís de Camões, where Chiado meets Bairro Alto, stays busy well past midnight. For real quiet, look to Lapa, the side streets of Príncipe Real, Belém or Parque das Nações. Wherever you land, ask for a courtyard or rear-facing room and ask specifically about double glazing.

Luxury

Lisbon’s grand hotels sit above the centre rather than in it — splendid, calm, and a taxi ride from dinner.

Four Seasons Hotel Ritz Lisbon

The grande dame of the city, opened in 1959 and run by Four Seasons since 1997. Broad corridors, deep armchairs, terraces on most rooms and one of the largest hotel spas in Portugal, with a heated indoor lap pool. The service is unhurried and genuinely kind to older guests, and the building itself is level and generously lifted throughout. The caveat is the position: it stands above Parque Eduardo VII, so Chiado is twenty minutes downhill and a stiff climb back. Take a taxi home.

Portugal’s licensing makes genuine adults-only hotels uncommon in Lisbon. These two have verified minimum-age policies.

A converted nineteenth-century bakery on a cobbled back lane in Alfama, with forty-two rooms over four floors and a small red infinity pool on the terrace — the view over the rooftops to the Tagus is the reason people come. Lisbon Hotels — common questions

Which Lisbon neighbourhood should I choose if hills are a problem?

Baixa, without hesitation. It is the flat grid between Rossio and Praça do Comércio, rebuilt on level ground after the 1755 earthquake, with metro stations at either end and taxi ranks throughout. Avenida da Liberdade is the next best — a wide, gently sloping boulevard with excellent pavements. Belém and Parque das Nações are flat too, though both sit away from the centre. Avoid basing yourself in Alfama or Graça if stairs trouble you, however romantic the setting. The walk home from dinner is the problem, not the sightseeing.

Do I need a car in Lisbon?

No, and it would be a hindrance. The streets are narrow, one-way and often cobbled, parking is scarce and dear, and many historic lanes are closed to traffic altogether. The metro is clean, air-conditioned and lift-served at most central stations, and taxis and ride-hailing are inexpensive by American standards — typically six to twelve euros across the centre. Hire a car only for day trips to Sintra or the Arrábida coast, and even then the train or an organised excursion is usually less trouble. Most hotels can arrange a driver for the day.

How much walking should I expect, and is it manageable?

More than the distances suggest, and more strenuous. Lisbon is built across seven hills, and the calçada paving — small limestone blocks worn glassy by centuries of feet — is unforgiving, especially when damp. Plan shorter days than you would in Paris or Amsterdam, build in café stops, and use the funiculars, the Santa Justa lift and taxis for the uphill legs without apology. A great many visitors adopt the rule of walking down and riding up. Shoes with real grip are not a luxury here; they are the single best preparation you can make.

Do Lisbon hotels have lifts and step-free access?

Not reliably, and this is worth an email before you book. Many of the city’s most charming small hotels occupy eighteenth- and nineteenth-century buildings where a lift was retrofitted into a stairwell afterwards, arriving at a half-landing with several steps on either side, or where the entrance itself has a threshold step. The purpose-built modern hotels — Corinthia, EPIC SANA, MYRIAD and the Four Seasons — are dependably step-free with generous lifts. Ask specifically about the route from the street to the bedroom door, not merely whether a lift exists.

Is Tram 28 worth it, and how do I ride it sensibly?

It is worth it, and it is also the most crowded ride in Portugal. The 28 runs from Martim Moniz through Graça, Alfama, Baixa and Estrela, and when the cruise ships are in, the queue at Martim Moniz can pass half an hour. Ride before eight in the morning or after seven in the evening, carry your bag in front of you, and hold on firmly, because the brakes are abrupt. Tram 24 covers similar ground and almost never queues, and Tram 12 is a shorter, quieter Alfama loop that most visitors overlook entirely.

Is Lisbon safe, and what should I watch for?

Lisbon is among the safer capitals in Europe for violent crime, and older visitors generally feel comfortable walking in the centre. Pickpocketing is the genuine concern, and it concentrates in predictable places: Tram 28, the queue for the Santa Justa lift, Rossio, and crowded metro carriages at the moment of boarding. Carry bags in front and keep nothing in a back pocket. You will also be offered counterfeit drugs on Rua Augusta — a firm no and continued walking ends it. Some outer districts feel bleak after dark; take a taxi rather than walking.
