Estonia

The Estonia Travel Guide

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Estonia is a small, flat, heavily forested country on the eastern shore of the Baltic, with a walled medieval capital at the top of it, a university city in the middle, a shallow warm bay on the west coast and two thousand islands scattered off it. It is the northernmost of the three Baltic states and in temperament the most Nordic: the language is a cousin of Finnish, Helsinki is two hours away by ferry, and the long summer light and the long winter dark shape the year here in the way they do in Finland rather than in Poland. For a visitor it is one of the easiest countries in Europe — short distances, good roads, near-universal card payment, widely spoken English and very few people. You can stand in a thirteenth-century merchant town in the morning and be alone on a bog boardwalk in the afternoon.

Why visit Estonia

It is the easiest country in Europe to travel through

Estonia runs on its phone. Tickets, parking, restaurant bills and museum entry are all card or app, English is spoken widely and well, the distances are small, the roads are good and empty, and the bureaucracy a visitor meets is close to none. For a first trip somewhere unfamiliar, or for anyone travelling alone, it asks remarkably little of you.

Tallinn’s old town is the real thing

Not a reconstructed quarter but a complete walled medieval merchant town — the guild halls, the churches, the town hall, long runs of wall with their towers — standing where it was built and still lived in. It is on the UNESCO list and it is small enough to walk across in a quarter of an hour, which is why so many people see it in a day and then wish they had stayed.

More than half the country is forest, and you may walk in it

Estonia is one of the most thinly populated countries in Europe and more than half of it is woodland and bog. Public access is broad, boardwalks cross the raised bogs, and berries and mushrooms are picked by anyone who wants them. You can be in genuine wilderness within an hour of the capital, which is not a claim many European capitals can make.

Two thousand islands, and two of them have towns

The west coast dissolves into islands. Saaremaa, the largest, has a castle with no rival in the Baltic and a spa town around it; Hiiumaa is quieter still; Kihnu, small and far out, kept a tradition of dress, song and women running the place that has outlasted everything around it. Ferries are cheap, frequent in summer and part of the pleasure.

The light is extraordinary at both ends of the year

At midsummer the sun is up around eighteen hours and the sky never goes fully dark. At midwinter you get six or seven hours and spend the rest in lamplight, snow and sauna heat. Neither is a compromise; they are two different and equally good holidays, and the country is organised for both.

Singing is not a tourist attraction here

The Song and Dance Celebration gathers tens of thousands of singers in Tallinn every five years, and the chain of gatherings that helped end Soviet rule is still called the Singing Revolution. It is recognised by UNESCO and it is a living part of national life rather than something performed for visitors. If your trip falls in a festival year, change your plans to be there.

Estonia at a glance

Capital

Tallinn, on the Gulf of Finland, with about a third of the country’s people in and around it. The walled old town is a UNESCO World Heritage site; the modern city, the port and the ferry terminals for Helsinki sit immediately around it, and the airport is a few kilometres out with a tram into the centre.

Money

The euro, since the beginning of 2011. Cards work almost everywhere, including on buses, in small cafes and at rural museums, and Estonia is one of the easiest countries in Europe in which to travel without cash at all. Carry a few notes anyway for island ferries, small churches and the occasional farm stall.

Language

Estonian, a Finno-Ugric language closely related to Finnish and not at all to Russian or Latvian. Russian is widely spoken, particularly in Tallinn and in the north-east. English is spoken widely by younger people and almost universally in the places a visitor goes. Nobody expects a visitor to attempt Estonian.

Borders

In the European Union since 2004, the Schengen area since the end of 2007 and the eurozone since 2011. Arrivals from other Schengen countries, including the ferry from Helsinki, have no passport control. The eastern border with Russia is an external EU frontier and its crossing arrangements have changed more than once; check officially before relying on one.

Getting about

Driving is on the right, and dipped headlights are required day and night all year. The country is small — Tallinn to Tartu is a little over two hours by train, Tallinn to Pärnu about two by bus — and intercity coaches are frequent, cheap and comfortable. A car is unnecessary in the cities and close to essential on the islands and in the countryside.

Shape of it

About forty-five thousand square kilometres and roughly 1.3 million people, which makes it one of the emptiest countries in Europe. More than half is forest. The land is almost flat; the highest point is a wooded hill in the south-east that barely passes three hundred metres. There are over two thousand islands, most of them uninhabited.

The regions

Five of them, and the country is small enough that the furthest two are about four hours apart by road.

The north: Tallinn and Lahemaa

The capital and the coast either side of it. East of Tallinn, Lahemaa was the Soviet Union’s first national park and is still the easiest piece of wild Estonia to reach — a coast of boulder fields and bays, bog boardwalks, pine forest, and a handful of restored manor houses among it. West of the city the shore runs out towards the old Soviet naval town at Paldiski and the limestone cliffs beyond.

The south-east: Tartu, Otepää and Lake Peipus

Estonia’s other half. Tartu is the university city and the cultural capital; south of it the land finally rises a little into the hills around Otepää, which is the country’s winter sports centre and its summer walking country. East of both lies Lake Peipus, one of the largest lakes in Europe, with a shore of onion fields and the villages of the Old Believers, a Russian religious community that has been there for three centuries.

The west coast: Pärnu, Haapsalu and Matsalu

The low, sandy, shallow side of the country, and the holiday side. Pärnu has the beach and the spa tradition; Haapsalu, further north, is a smaller resort of wooden villas around a ruined bishop’s castle, with its own long-standing mud-cure trade. Between and around them, Matsalu is one of the most important bird migration sites in northern Europe, with hides and towers along the reed beds.

The islands

Over two thousand of them, and a handful that matter to a traveller. Saaremaa is the largest, with Kuressaare and its castle, juniper heath, a meteorite crater and preserved windmills. Hiiumaa to the north is quieter and more thinly served. Muhu sits between Saaremaa and the mainland on the ferry route. Kihnu, small and far out in the gulf off Pärnu, holds a tradition of dress, song and community that UNESCO has recognised.

The north-east: Narva and the Ida-Virumaa coast

The least visited corner and, for some travellers, the most interesting. Narva stands on the river that forms the Russian border, its castle facing another castle across the water, and it is the most Russian-speaking city in Estonia. Along the coast west of it, Narva-Jõesuu is an old seaside resort with a long sand beach and a surviving spa trade, and the shale-mining country inland is unlike anywhere else in the Baltics.

Things to do in Estonia

Walk the walls and the lower town in Tallinn

Start at Viru Gate, work up through the lanes to Town Hall Square, and then follow the surviving stretch of city wall round the northern side where you can climb inside three linked towers and walk the covered rampart between them. It is the best hour in the country for the money, and it makes sense of the plan of the town in a way no museum does. Go early or late in summer, when the cruise crowds are not in the square.

Stand in the castle moat at Kuressaare

Saaremaa’s episcopal castle is a grey dolomite keep inside a water-filled moat and a later ring of earthworks, and it is the best-preserved medieval castle in the Baltic states — not a ruin and not a reconstruction. You can walk the vaulted halls, the chapel and the defensive passages, and the island museum is inside. It is worth the ferry on its own.

Take a bog boardwalk at dawn

Estonia’s raised bogs are landscapes with no real equivalent in western Europe: open, pale, dotted with pools and dwarf pines, and reached on plank walkways that keep you out of the moss. Viru bog in Lahemaa is the easiest from Tallinn and busy for it; several others in the south and west are emptier. Go at first light, in mist if you can, and take the insect repellent seriously in summer.

See the Estonian National Museum at Tartu

A long glass building running up a former Soviet runway on the edge of Tartu, holding the permanent exhibition of Estonian life. It is the best museum in the Baltic states and it treats a small nation’s ordinary history with a seriousness that is genuinely moving. Give it half a day and check the opening days before making the trip out.

Cross the Gulf of Finland for the day

Fast ferries run from Tallinn to Helsinki in about two hours, several times a day for most of the year. Both countries are in Schengen, so it is an ordinary journey rather than a border crossing. It is a good use of a fifth day and a poor use of a second; check the weather first, because winter sailings are cancelled often enough to matter.

Look across the river at Narva

Hermann Castle stands on the Estonian bank and Ivangorod fortress on the Russian one, a few hundred metres apart with the river and the European Union’s external frontier between them. There is nowhere else quite like it. The town is the most Russian-speaking in Estonia and repays an afternoon; our partner lists nothing to book there, so this is a day trip from Narva-Jõesuu or a stop on a drive rather than a base.

When to go, and what it costs

Estonia has a short, bright summer and a long, dark winter, and the two are different holidays rather than better and worse versions of the same one. From June to August the days are enormous, the coast and the islands fill with Estonian and Finnish holidaymakers, and the sea — properly, only at Pärnu — is warm enough to swim in. The Guild would choose late May to mid-June, when the light is already long and the crowds have not arrived, or September, when the forests fill with mushrooms and the cities get their students back. December is its own case: the Christmas market and the Old Town in the dark are a real reason to travel rather than a consolation. The two stretches we would steer a first-time visitor away from are the March thaw and November. One date to plan around above all others: Midsummer, 23 and 24 June, when the country closes and goes to the countryside.

Fishing here

Estonian fishing runs from pike and perch in the coastal shallows, Lake Peipus and the inland lakes to sea trout in the short northern rivers, and ice fishing is an ordinary winter pastime once the bays and the great lakes freeze. Licences are required for most of it and the rules differ between sea, lake and river, so it is best arranged locally rather than puzzled out from abroad. Travelling in your later years

Distances are short and that changes everything

Nothing in Estonia is far from anything else. Tallinn to Tartu is a little over two hours on a comfortable train, Tallinn to Pärnu about two by coach, and the island ferry crossing is under half an hour. You can see a great deal of the country without a long day in a vehicle, which is not true of most European itineraries.

The cobbles are the one real hazard

Tallinn’s Old Town is rounded limestone cobble, it is slippery when wet and treacherous when iced, and the two ramps up to Toompea are steep. This is the single thing most likely to spoil a trip here, and it is entirely manageable with flat boots with proper grip and a willingness to take the long way round. Tartu and Pärnu are flat and easy by comparison.

The spa hotels are built for this

Estonia’s spa tradition is a serious part of its hospitality trade rather than a luxury extra, and the hotels in Pärnu, Kuressaare and Haapsalu are set up for guests who have come for treatments and rest. Lifts, level access, pools and medically supervised programmes are normal here. They are also what stays open and staffed through the winter.

Winter is a decision, not a detail

Between December and March the days are short, the pavements ice and the island ferries thin out. None of that makes Estonia unsuitable; it makes it a different trip, indoors-shaped, with sightseeing in the middle of the day. If you would rather not deal with ice at all, come between May and September, when the light is long and the walking is easy.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Estonia itself is straightforward: in the European Union since 2004, in the Schengen area since the end of 2007 and on the euro since the beginning of 2011. An arrival from another Schengen country has no passport control at all, and an arrival from outside it is a single check at the airport you land in. The land borders with Latvia and the sea crossing from Finland are internal Schengen movements. The eastern border with Russia is an external EU frontier and the arrangements at its crossings have changed more than once in recent years, so if any part of your plan depends on one, check with the Estonian Police and Border Guard Board before you book rather than trusting anything written earlier, including this page.

One point of confusion worth clearing up: Estonian e-Residency is a digital identity for running a business online. It is not residency, it is not a visa and it gives you no right to enter or stay. If you are coming as a visitor, the Schengen rules above are the ones that apply to you.

Estonia — questions

How many days do I need in Estonia?

Three days covers Tallinn properly, which is what most people come for. Five gets you Tallinn and Tartu with the train between them, or Tallinn and a few days on Saaremaa. A week lets you do the capital, the south and either the islands or the west coast without rushing, and that is the trip we would recommend to anyone making the journey from outside Europe. The country is small and the distances are genuinely short, so each extra day buys more here than it does in a larger country.

Is Estonia expensive?

Less than the Nordic countries across the water and more than it was ten years ago. Eating out, transport and museums are reasonable by western European standards; hotel rooms in central Tallinn in summer and on the coast in July and August are the places where the price bites. The cheapest months are late autumn and the deep winter outside the Christmas weeks. We do not print figures because they move, but the shape of the year is reliable.

Do I need a car?

Not for Tallinn, Tartu or Pärnu, all of which are walkable and well connected by frequent, cheap intercity coaches and, between Tallinn and Tartu, by train. You want one for Saaremaa beyond Kuressaare, for Lahemaa and the bogs, and for the south-east. A reasonable plan is to travel between cities by coach or train and hire a car for the two or three days you are in the countryside. Driving is on the right and dipped headlights are required at all hours.

What language do people speak, and is Russian useful?

Estonian is the national language, a Finno-Ugric language related to Finnish and unrelated to Russian or Latvian. Russian is widely spoken, especially in Tallinn and very much so in the north-east around Narva. English is spoken widely by younger people and more or less universally in hotels, restaurants and museums. A visitor needs no Estonian at all, though aitäh for thank you is always well received.

Is Estonia safe?

Yes, by any ordinary measure, and it is an easy country to travel in alone. Normal city sense in Tallinn after dark is the extent of it. The practical risks here are environmental rather than human: ice underfoot in winter, ticks in long grass and forest in the warmer months, and elk and deer on country roads at dusk, which is a real hazard if you are driving. None of those should put anybody off.

What is e-Residency? Does it let me live there?

No, and the name causes real confusion. Estonian e-Residency is a government-issued digital identity that lets people run an EU-registered company online from anywhere. It is not residency, not citizenship and not a visa, and it confers no right whatever to enter or stay in Estonia. If you are coming as a visitor, the ordinary Schengen rules on this page are the ones that apply to you.

Can I visit Russia from Narva?

Narva sits directly on the border and its castle faces Ivangorod across the river, so the question comes up constantly. The crossing arrangements on Estonia’s eastern frontier have changed more than once in recent years, and anything written about them dates quickly — including this page. Do not plan any part of a trip around crossing there without checking the current position with the Estonian Police and Border Guard Board and with your own government’s travel advice. Narva itself is an ordinary Estonian city and perfectly straightforward to visit.
