Podgorica

Where to Stay in Podgorica

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Podgorica is the capital, the transport hub and the least visited city in Montenegro, and it is worth saying plainly that most people sleep here for a reason rather than for pleasure: an early flight, a hire car, a train north, or a base for Lake Skadar and Ostrog. It was bombed flat in the Second World War and rebuilt, so it is a city of boulevards, cafes and parks rather than old stone, with a small Ottoman quarter surviving by the river. The compensation is that it is cheap, unhurried and genuinely Montenegrin in a way the coast in August is not, and almost everything central is within a twenty-minute walk.

The new centre: around Hercegovačka and the Republic Square

Most centralCafes and shopsBest choice of rooms

The pedestrianised run of Hercegovačka and the streets around Trg Republike are the heart of modern Podgorica, and this is where almost all the city's hotels sit. You are a short flat walk from the restaurants, the bus and railway stations are a reasonable walk or a very short taxi, and the city parks are on the doorstep. There is nothing picturesque about the architecture, but it is comfortable, well lit and easy, and for a one-night stop on the way somewhere it is the obvious place to be.

Stara Varoš and the Ribnica

The old quarterQuietSmall and local

The old town is a modest pocket of low houses, a clock tower and a mosque near where the Ribnica joins the Morača, with the stump of a Turkish bridge beside it. It is not a sight so much as a half-hour walk and a reminder of what the city was before 1944. There are few rooms here and they are mostly apartments; the attraction is quiet streets and being able to walk to the centre in ten minutes. Take it if you like staying somewhere residential rather than somewhere convenient.

Along the Morača and the Millennium Bridge

River walksNewer buildingsParking easier

The Morača runs through the middle of the city in a surprisingly deep bed, with footpaths, swimming spots and a good deal of greenery along it, and the white cable-stayed Millennium Bridge is the city's one piece of showmanship. Staying on this side puts you among newer apartment buildings with easier parking and a pleasant walk along the water into the centre. It suits drivers and anyone who would rather look at a river than a boulevard.

Out by the airport and Golubovci

Early flightsCar-dependentCheapest beds

Podgorica's airport is about twelve kilometres south of the city near Golubovci, and there is a scattering of small guesthouses on that road. The only good reason to stay out here is a flight at an hour that makes the city centre awkward, or a very late arrival. Otherwise you are a taxi ride from anywhere, with nothing to walk to. Using the city for Lake Skadar and Ostrog

Day tripsHire carBest value base

The real argument for Podgorica is what is within an hour of it. Lake Skadar, the largest lake in southern Europe and a national park, begins half an hour to the south-west, with boat trips from the lakeside villages and a famously photogenic bend in the river above them. Ostrog, the monastery built into a cliff face and the most visited place in the country for Montenegrins themselves, is about an hour north-west. Both are far easier from here than from the coast, and a room in the capital costs a fraction of one in Budva in August.

Getting around Podgorica

On foot

The central area is flat and compact and walking is the sensible way to cover it. Summer is the exception: Podgorica sits in a basin and is one of the hottest cities in Europe in July and August, and the locals disappear into the shade in the middle of the day for good reason.

Taxis and buses

Taxis are cheap by western European standards and are the usual way to reach the airport or the stations with luggage; use a company number or an app rather than flagging one down, and check the meter is running. City buses exist and are cheap, but the network is not aimed at visitors and the central area does not really need it.

Driving

This is the easiest place in the country to pick up a hire car, and the one city where parking is not a battle. The motorway north towards Mateševo has taken the worst of the old Morača canyon road out of the journey to the mountains; the rest of the country is ordinary two-lane roads, well surfaced on the main routes and slow in the hills.

Trains

The line from Bar on the coast up through Podgorica and on towards Belgrade is one of the great scenic railways of Europe, crossing the Mala Rijeka viaduct and climbing through the mountains to Kolašin and Mojkovac. It is slow, the rolling stock is old and delays are normal, and it is worth doing anyway for the stretch north of the capital. Check current timetables on the day.

When to come, and what it costs

Podgorica has a hot inland climate with none of the sea breeze that makes the coast bearable, and midsummer here is genuinely fierce — this is regularly one of the hottest cities in Europe in July and August. Spring and autumn are the pleasant seasons, with April, May, late September and October giving warm days and comfortable evenings, and they are also when the lake and the mountains behind are at their best. Winter is mild, grey and wet by Balkan standards, and the city carries on much as usual. Room prices barely move across the year, which is part of the appeal.

Getting in: Montenegro is outside the EU and outside Schengen

Montenegro uses the euro but is not in the European Union and not in the Schengen area. That has one very useful consequence for a traveller: days spent here do not count against the ninety days in any hundred and eighty that Schengen allows, and the reverse is also true. Citizens of the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, Australia and the European Union do not need a visa for a short holiday, with ninety days in any hundred and eighty the usual allowance — confirm your own nationality’s terms with your own foreign ministry rather than with any website. Your passport should have been issued within the last ten years and be valid for at least three months beyond your departure, and you should make sure it is stamped on the way in and out, because those stamps are the record of your stay.

Every stay must be reported to the police. Montenegro’s own government says that duty falls on the accommodation provider, and hotels do it automatically at check-in; British government advice tells visitors to make sure they are registered within twenty-four hours and warns of a fine if they are not. In a hotel it is handled for you. In a private flat, ask your host to confirm it is done and keep the slip. Crossing to or from Croatia is a Schengen border with full checks and, in high summer, queues — and if you are driving a hire car across it, confirm first that the rental company permits it.

Podgorica — questions

Is Podgorica worth a night?

As a destination in its own right, honestly, not for most visitors — there is no old town to speak of and no great museum. As a base it earns its place: it is central, cheap, has the airport, the hire cars and the train, and puts Lake Skadar and Ostrog within an hour. A night here at either end of a trip usually makes the rest of the itinerary work better, and the city is pleasant enough to spend an evening in.

What is actually worth seeing in the city?

A half day covers it comfortably: the old quarter of Stara Varoš with its clock tower and the Ribnica bridge, the walk along the Morača and across the Millennium Bridge, the Cathedral of the Resurrection with its unusual modern frescoes, and the parks. After that the thing to do is what the locals do, which is sit outside with a coffee for considerably longer than seems necessary.

How do I get to Ostrog and Lake Skadar?

A hire car is far the easiest for both, and both are comfortable half-days. Ostrog is about an hour north-west, with a steep narrow approach road and a walk up to the upper monastery; dress modestly, and expect crowds on holy days. For Lake Skadar, the lakeside villages to the south-west are the usual starting point for boat trips, and the road along the lake towards the coast is one of the best drives in the country. Organised day tours run from the capital in season if you would rather not drive.

Is the train to the coast or to Belgrade worth taking?

The Bar to Podgorica stretch is short and useful. The stretch north from Podgorica towards Kolašin and Mojkovac is the spectacular one, climbing through tunnels and across high viaducts. Treat it as scenery rather than transport: it is slow, the carriages are elderly and delays are routine. Buy ahead in summer if you can, and check times on the day rather than from an old timetable.

How hot does it really get in summer?

Hot enough that the city empties into the shade for several hours in the middle of the day, and hot enough that we would not plan long sightseeing walks here in July or August. If you are travelling in those months, do the capital early or late and keep the middle of the day for the lake, the mountains or an air-conditioned room.
