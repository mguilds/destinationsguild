Mexico · The Colonial Heart

Mexico City Travel & Adventure Guide

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated July 2026

An Aztec capital under a Spanish cathedral, the greatest museum in the Americas, pyramids an hour up the road — and quite possibly the best eating on the continent.

Mexico City is the oldest capital in the Americas and one of the largest cities on earth, and it does not reveal itself quickly. It was built on a lake, on top of Tenochtitlan, the Aztec island city the Spanish tore down in 1521 — and the layers are still visible: the ruins of the Templo Mayor sit exposed beside the cathedral on the Zócalo, both of them slowly sinking into the old lakebed. Above that foundation, the city has become the cultural engine of the Spanish-speaking world: the National Museum of Anthropology alone is worth crossing an ocean for, and the food — from the Sunday markets to the taquerías to the tasting rooms — is the reason a lot of people come back. It is a big, high, loud, endlessly rewarding city, and this guide covers where to stay, what to see, when to go, and how to book it.

The Aztecs founded Tenochtitlan on an island in Lake Texcoco in 1325, and by the time Cortés arrived it was one of the largest cities in the world. The Spanish razed it and built their capital directly on top — which is why the Metropolitan Cathedral stands on the same square as the excavated Templo Mayor, and why so many colonial buildings in the centre lean at visible angles: the lake was drained, and the city has been settling into the soft ground ever since.

Two practical things shape a first visit. The first is altitude: at roughly 2,240 metres, plenty of visitors feel breathless or headachy on day one, and alcohol lands harder than it should. Go gently at first. The second is scale: this is an enormous city, but almost everything a traveller wants sits in a handful of central neighbourhoods, and the metro is cheap, extensive and fast. Take it, and take Uber at night.

Eat everywhere. The city's genius is that the finest meal of your trip might cost eight dollars and be served standing up: al pastor carved off a spit at midnight, a quesadilla from a market comal, tacos de canasta from a bicycle. The formal restaurants of Roma and Polanco are world-class, but they are not where the city keeps its soul.

Why visit Mexico City

Four reasons CDMX has become one of the great city breaks on earth.

History you can stand on

Aztec ruins, a Spanish cathedral and a modern plaza all share one square. Nowhere else layers 700 years quite so bluntly.

The Anthropology Museum

One of the greatest museums anywhere — the Aztec Sun Stone, the Maya halls, the Olmec heads. Give it a full day.

The food

From street corners to tasting menus, this is the most exciting eating city in the Americas, and by a wide margin the best value.

Pyramids up the road

Teotihuacán — the Avenue of the Dead and the vast Pyramid of the Sun — is an hour from the city centre.

Top things to do in Mexico City

The experiences worth building the trip around.

● The Zócalo & Templo Mayor

The vast main square, the Metropolitan Cathedral, and the excavated Aztec temple beside it — the founding stone of the whole country.

● Museo Nacional de Antropología

The Sun Stone, the Maya treasures, the Olmec heads. If you see one thing in Mexico, see this.

● Teotihuacán

An hour northeast: the Avenue of the Dead running between the Pyramids of the Sun and the Moon. Go at opening time.

● Frida Kahlo's Casa Azul

The blue house in Coyoacán where she was born, painted and died. Book tickets well ahead — it sells out.

● Palacio de Bellas Artes

The marble-and-glass opera house, with Rivera and Orozco murals upstairs and the Ballet Folklórico on stage.

● Xochimilco

Board a painted trajinera and drift the last surviving canals of the Aztec lake, with a mariachi boat pulling alongside.

Where to stay in Mexico City

Four neighbourhoods cover almost every trip. Roma Norte and Condesa are the default and the best first choice — leafy, walkable, art-deco streets thick with cafés, bookshops and some of the best restaurants in the country, and close enough to the centre. Polanco is the wealthy district: the luxury hotels, the designer shopping, and the Anthropology Museum on its doorstep — polished, but less characterful. The Centro Histórico puts you directly on the Zócalo and within walking distance of the great sights; it's thrilling by day and much quieter after dark. Coyoacán, further south, is the villagey, cobbled quarter where Frida Kahlo lived — lovely, but a long way from everything else.

Best time to visit & getting there

When to go

March–May is warm and dry. June–September is the rainy season — mornings are usually clear and the afternoon downpours are brief, and the city is green and cheap. November–February is cool, dry and very pleasant. Day of the Dead, around 31 October to 2 November, is extraordinary — and books out months ahead.

Getting there

Benito Juárez International (MEX) sits close to the centre and is one of the busiest airports in Latin America, with direct flights from across the US and Canada. A second airport, Felipe Ángeles (NLU), serves some routes but is considerably further out. Use authorised airport taxis or a ride app rather than street cabs.

Mexico City travel FAQ

Where should you stay in Mexico City?

Roma Norte and Condesa are the first choice for most visitors — leafy, walkable, full of restaurants and cafés, and well placed for the centre. Polanco is the upmarket district, with the luxury hotels and the Anthropology Museum on its doorstep. The Centro Histórico puts you on top of the Zócalo and the major sights but is quieter at night. Coyoacán is charming and villagey, but further out.

Do you feel the altitude in Mexico City?

Some people do. The city sits at roughly 2,240 metres (about 7,350 feet), high enough that many visitors feel short of breath or tired on the first day, and alcohol can hit harder than expected. Take the first day gently, drink plenty of water, and you'll usually adjust quickly.

How do you get to Teotihuacán?

The pyramids sit about 50 km northeast of the city, roughly an hour by road. Buses run from the Terminal del Norte, and organised tours and private drivers are easy to arrange. Go early — the site is vast, exposed and very hot by midday.

When is the best time to visit Mexico City?

March to May is warm and dry. The rainy season runs roughly June to September, when afternoon downpours are common but mornings are usually clear. November to February is cool, dry and pleasant. Late October and early November bring Day of the Dead — spectacular, and very busy.
