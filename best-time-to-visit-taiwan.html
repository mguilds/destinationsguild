Best Time to Visit Taiwan

Best Time to Visit Taiwan

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Short answer: October and November. But Taiwan is the rare country where the answer changes depending on which end of it you are standing on. A mountain range runs down the middle of a small island, and it splits the weather in two: in winter the north is damp and grey while the south is dry and warm, and in summer the pattern turns over. What follows is the year month by month, with the typhoons, the plum rains and the holiday weeks treated as seriously as the temperature.

The short answer

If you only get one window, take the autumn.

Best overall: October and November

Warm, dry, clear and comfortable from Taipei to Kenting, the typhoon season over and the mountains finally visible. Twenty to twenty-nine degrees. Book ahead, because this is the season people who have been before come back for.

Next best: late March and April

Spring, before the humidity. Twenty to twenty-seven degrees, blossom in the hills, fireflies, and a sea warm enough to swim in down south. It ends suddenly in mid-May when the plum rains arrive.

Best value: July and August

Hot and humid, and the peak of the typhoon season, but city hotels are at their cheapest, the high mountains are at their best and the island is at its liveliest. Keep a spare day and flexible bookings.

Winter, planned properly

December to February is a very good holiday if you start in the south: Kaohsiung, Tainan and Kenting stay dry and warm. The same trip planned from Taipei downwards is a disappointment. It is also the hot-spring season.

The coldest month, which in Taiwan means fourteen to nineteen degrees and persistent drizzle in Taipei, and nineteen to twenty-five degrees of dry sunshine in Kaohsiung. One island, two climates

This is the thing worth understanding before you pick your dates, and it is the thing most visitors do not know. The Central Mountain Range runs the length of Taiwan and reaches nearly four thousand metres, and it does not merely divide the island geographically — it divides the weather.

In winter, from December to February, the northeast monsoon carries cold damp air off the East China Sea onto the top of the island. Taipei, Keelung and the northeast coast sit under low cloud and drizzle at twelve to eighteen degrees, sometimes for a fortnight at a stretch. The range blocks it: on the far side, Kaohsiung, Tainan, Taitung and Kenting are dry, bright and twenty to twenty-eight. The two places are two hours apart by train and feel like different countries.

In summer, roughly June to September, the pattern turns over. The southwest monsoon drives the heavy convective rain onto the south and west, which get spectacular afternoon downpours, while the north and the east are hot and comparatively dry. The mountains themselves are the exception to everything: ten degrees cooler than the coast, at their best and safest in summer, and reliably thundery after lunch.

The practical rules that follow are simple. A winter trip should be built from the south upwards, with Taipei as a short stop rather than the base. A summer trip should lean on the north, the east coast and the high country, with the lowland south kept for mornings. And in autumn, for about eight weeks, none of this matters, because the whole island is pleasant at once — which is exactly why October and November are the answer.

Typhoons, plainly

Taiwan sits in the busiest typhoon basin on earth and is hit or brushed by several storms in an average year, nearly all of them between July and September, with the outer edges of the season reaching into June and October. This is a real thing to plan around and not a reason to stay away.

What it actually looks like as a visitor: the forecast gives two or three days notice, and then the city or county government declares a typhoon day, usually the evening before. Offices, schools, museums, theme parks, ferries and the mountain roads close; trains and flights are cancelled or thinned; the convenience stores stay open and are mobbed. Hotels keep running. The following day is often brilliantly clear, and then the clean-up is quick and impressively well organised. The genuine dangers are landslides on mountain roads and dangerous surf, both of which are why the closures exist, and the sensible response is to obey them without argument.

Three bookings worth protecting if you travel in the summer: anything that puts you on an offshore island, because the ferries and the small aircraft are the first things to stop and the last to restart; a tight onward international connection; and non-refundable rooms in the mountains. Leave one slack day in the itinerary and a typhoon costs you a day of sightseeing rather than the trip.

Crowds and holidays

Taiwan’s busy weeks are set by the lunar calendar and move every year. Check your dates against them.

Lunar New Year

Late January or February, and the one to plan around. The island travels home at once: reserved train seats sell out weeks ahead, hotel rates roughly double, major temples are packed and a great many small restaurants and family businesses shut for several days. It is a wonderful time to be here if you have booked everything; a frustrating one if you have not.

The Lantern Festival

A fortnight after New Year. The Pingxi sky lanterns outside Taipei and the Yanshui beehive fireworks near Tainan are the famous two, and both are extraordinary and extremely crowded. Trains to Pingxi are standing room only.

Tomb Sweeping, Dragon Boat, Mid-Autumn

Early April, around June and around September respectively. Each gives a long weekend, and each fills the trains, the coast roads and the hotels for three or four days. Domestic travel, not international, so it shows up as full trains rather than full flights.

Summer school holidays

July and August. City hotels are cheap and quiet; the beaches, Kenting, Penghu, Green Island and Orchid Island are the opposite. If you want an offshore island in summer, book early.

Double Ten

The national day, the tenth of October, with a parade in Taipei and a long weekend around it. It lands in the best week of the year for weather, so book October rooms well ahead.

The quiet weeks

If you want Taiwan to yourself: the second half of November, early December in the south, and mid-June once the plum rains have cleared and before the schools break up.

Getting in, whatever the season

Citizens of the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, the European Union and Schengen countries, Australia, New Zealand, Japan and South Korea, among others, are admitted visa-free for ninety days, with no fee and no application. Some other nationalities get thirty or fourteen days and some need a visa in advance, and the list is revised from time to time, so check your own passport at boca.gov.tw. There is also a free Taiwan Online Arrival Card to complete shortly before you land; use the official government site rather than an agent charging for the same form.

Most long-haul flights land at Taoyuan (TPE), forty minutes from Taipei Main Station on its own metro line. Kaohsiung (KHH) is the southern international door and worth considering for a winter trip, since it puts you straight into the good weather. Buy an EasyCard or an iPASS in the arrivals hall; it pays for the metros, buses, local trains and ferries all over the island. The full picture is in our Taiwan travel guide.

Best time to visit Taiwan FAQ

What is the single best month to visit Taiwan?

October. The typhoon season has wound down, the summer humidity has gone, and the whole island gets warm, dry, clear days of about twenty-three to twenty-nine degrees. November is almost as good and a little cooler. If you cannot come in autumn, late March and April are the next best thing.

Which months should I avoid?

No month is a write-off, but two need care. The Lunar New Year fortnight, which falls in late January or February and moves every year, is when the whole island travels at once: trains sell out, hotel rates double and many small restaurants and family shops close for days. And the plum rains, from about the middle of May to the middle of June, bring a month of grey and wet to the north. July to September carries the typhoon risk, but it is a risk rather than a certainty and plenty of people have a fine summer trip.

Why do the north and the south need different seasons?

Because the Central Mountain Range gets in the way of the weather. From December to February the northeast monsoon pushes damp cloudy air onto the top of the island, so Taipei, Keelung and the northeast coast sit under drizzle at twelve to eighteen degrees for weeks. The mountains block it from the south, which stays dry and sunny at twenty to twenty-eight. In summer the pattern reverses: the southwest monsoon brings the heavy afternoon rain to the south and west while the north and the east are merely hot. The practical rule is that a winter trip should be planned from the south upwards and a summer trip from the north and the mountains.

When is Taiwan cheapest?

The summer, roughly late June to early September, is the low season for city hotels and often for flights, because the heat and the typhoon risk put visitors off. The exception is the coast and the islands, which fill with Taiwanese families during the school holidays. The other quiet stretch is deep northern winter, January and February outside the New Year holiday, when Taipei rooms are soft. Prices spike hard for Lunar New Year and noticeably for the Tomb Sweeping, Dragon Boat and Mid-Autumn long weekends.

How likely is a typhoon to ruin my trip?

Taiwan is hit or brushed by a handful of typhoons in an average year, nearly all of them between July and September, and the practical effect on a visitor is usually one or two lost days rather than a ruined holiday. The island is extremely well drilled: a typhoon day is declared city by city, usually the evening before, and offices, schools, attractions, trains and flights stop for it. Build one slack day into a summer itinerary, keep domestic flights and ferries flexible, book accommodation that can be changed, and do not plan to be on an offshore island on a tight connection in August.

Is there a cherry blossom season?

Yes, and it is less famous and much less crowded than Japan's. Taiwan's cherries flower from late January in the warmer lowlands to late March high in the mountains, with Yangmingshan above Taipei, Wuling Farm and Alishan the best-known spots. Because the island climbs from sea level to nearly four thousand metres, the season lasts far longer than it does anywhere flat; the trade-off is that the good viewing areas are in the hills, need booking, and are busiest at weekends.

What about the high mountains?

They run on their own calendar. Summer, when the lowlands are at their most unpleasant, is the best and safest time for the high peaks and the alpine roads, and the temperature up there is a relief rather than a hardship. Winter brings ice, occasional snow above about three thousand metres and road closures. Afternoon thunderstorms are the rule rather than the exception in summer, so walk early. Permits are required for the national park high routes and for Yushan in particular, and they are applied for well in advance.
