Panama Travel Guide

A country the width of a long afternoon’s drive, with two oceans, the ship canal that made it, a capital of glass towers above a restored Spanish quarter, Caribbean islands on one side and cloud-forest coffee farms on the other — and the US dollar in your pocket the whole time.

Why visit Panama

The canal is still the most astonishing thing in the Americas

Eighty kilometres of engineering that cut a continent in half, opened in 1914 at a cost in lives that nobody should gloss over, and widened a century later with a second set of locks big enough for ships that did not exist when it was built. Standing on the terrace at Miraflores while a vessel the size of a town is walked up a staircase of water by cables is not a museum experience. It is a working waterway, and about forty ships a day still go through it.

Two coasts, two climates, one short drive

Few countries let you have breakfast on the Pacific and dinner on the Caribbean. Panama is at its narrowest here, and the two sides do not look, sound or taste alike: the Pacific has the capital, the surf and the fishing grounds, while the Caribbean side has reef, Afro-Antillean and Guna culture, and a slower, wetter, greener way of going about things.

A capital that is genuinely two cities

Panama City has a skyline of mirrored towers that would not look out of place in the Gulf, and a few minutes away, on a headland, the restored colonial quarter of Casco Viejo: balconies, plazas, ruined churches and the best bars in Central America. Further east again are the stone ruins of Panamá Viejo, the original 1519 city that Henry Morgan sacked in 1671.

Rainforest at the end of the bus route

Soberanía National Park begins within half an hour of the skyscrapers, and Pipeline Road inside it is one of the most celebrated bird walks on earth — the kind of place where serious birders come for a fortnight and where a casual visitor with a morning to spare will still see toucans. The harpy eagle, the national bird and the most powerful raptor alive, is up there somewhere.

Panama at a glance

Money

The balboa, fixed one to one with the US dollar since 1904. American notes circulate as the everyday money; Panamanian coins match US ones in size. No conversion to do and no exchange counter to find.

Language

Spanish. English is common in the capital’s hotels and businesses, widely spoken in Bocas del Toro and on the Caribbean coast, and thin once you are in the countryside.

Getting around

Drive on the right. The Pan-American Highway runs the length of the country and stops at the Darién. Panama City has a metro and a vast bus terminal at Albrook; domestic flights to the islands and the highlands are short but infrequent.

Flying in

Tocumen International (PTY), about forty minutes east of the capital, is the main hub of this part of the Americas and the reason so many people change planes in Panama. Albrook (PAC), in the city itself, handles most domestic flights.

Weather

Hot and humid at sea level all year, around thirty degrees Celsius by day. Dry season roughly mid-December to mid-April; green season the rest, with heavy afternoon rain. Boquete, up at about 1,200 metres, is cool enough for a jumper at night.

Time

Eastern Standard Time all year, with no summer clock change — so Panama is level with New York in winter and an hour behind it in summer.

The regions — which part is which

Panama City and the canal

The capital, the locks at Miraflores, the causeway out to the islands at Amador, and the rainforest parks on the city’s northern edge. Almost every trip starts here, and two or three nights is the usual share.

Bocas del Toro

An archipelago in the north-west corner of the Caribbean coast, reached by a short flight or a long drive and a boat. Wooden buildings on stilts over the water, surf on the exposed side, snorkelling on the sheltered side, and water taxis instead of roads.

Chiriquí and the highlands

The west: Boquete and Volcán on the flanks of Barú, Panama’s only volcano and its high point. Coffee farms, cloud forest, cool nights and the Geisha varietal that has broken world auction records. David, the provincial capital, is the transport hub.

The Pacific beaches and Veraguas

The arc of resort sand west of the capital at Coronado and Playa Blanca, and further on the surf village of Santa Catalina, which is the jumping-off point for Coiba National Park — a former prison island whose isolation left the reef and the big fish alone.

Guna Yala, the San Blas islands

Several hundred Caribbean islands governed by the Guna people under their own autonomous administration, not by a resort company. Accommodation is simple and community-run, visits are arranged through Guna operators, and the rules are theirs. It is one of the most distinctive places in the Americas and it is not a luxury destination.

The Darién

The roadless forest on the Colombian border. Out of bounds for travellers, and we say so plainly in the questions below.

Things to do in Panama

Watch a ship climb the Miraflores locks

The viewing terraces, the commentary and the museum beside them. Ask locally which half of the day the traffic is expected, because the convoys change direction and an empty chamber is a long wait.

Walk Casco Viejo in the evening

The old quarter after the heat goes: plazas, the ruined arch, rooftop bars looking back at the skyline across the bay, and a dinner scene that is the best in the country. Stay to the lit, busy streets.

Pipeline Road and the rainforest

An early start into Soberanía for toucans, trogons and howler monkeys within an hour of the city, or the aerial tram and tower at the Gamboa end for the canopy without the walk.

A coffee morning in Boquete

Farms above the town run tours that take you from the cherry on the branch through washing and drying to a cupping table, Geisha included. It is a genuinely good two hours even if coffee is not your subject.

Snorkel or surf at Bocas del Toro

Water taxis to the reef at Cayo Zapatilla and the sheltered bays, or the exposed breaks off Isla Colón and Bastimentos when the swell is up. Sloths are easier to see here than almost anywhere.

Dive Coiba from Santa Catalina

A national park and World Heritage site an hour and a half offshore, with big pelagic fish, white-tip reef sharks and, in season, whale sharks and humpbacks. Boats go from the village; weather decides the day.

Cross to the San Blas islands

Arranged through Guna operators, usually a four-wheel-drive over the pass and then a boat. Palm islands, Guna molas, simple huts, no resorts. Go with respect for a community that has fought to keep it that way.

When to go

Mid-December to mid-April — the dry season

The reliable half: sun on the Pacific side, calmer water, the best chance of a clear morning on Barú. It is also when rates are highest and when Panama City is busiest, and the weeks around Christmas and Carnival are in a price class of their own.

May to November — the green season

Hot, humid, and usually a hard downpour in the afternoon rather than a grey day. Everything is greener, the waterfalls run, rates drop and the crowds thin. No hurricanes reach here, which is the advantage Panama holds over the islands to the north.

The weeks we would pick

Late April, when the dry season has just broken and the prices with it; or September and October, which are the wettest weeks on the Pacific side but often the calmest and clearest in Bocas del Toro, and the months when whales are off the Pacific coast. Arriving by ship

Panama is two kinds of cruise call at once. Many ships come to transit the canal itself — a full crossing between oceans that takes most of a day on deck, or a partial transit that enters the locks and turns back — and for a great many passengers that day is the reason the cruise was booked. Others call to put people ashore: on the Pacific side at the Amador terminal on the causeway, a short drive from Casco Viejo and the city, and on the Caribbean side at Colón, which is the nearest port to the Agua Clara locks and the old Spanish forts at Portobelo.

If your ship transits, you do not need an excursion to enjoy it: find a spot on an upper deck early, take sun cover and water, and give the whole morning to it. If you are ashore for a day in Colón, Agua Clara and Portobelo are the sensible pair and the town itself is not a place to wander. From Amador, a day is enough for the old quarter and Miraflores, though not comfortably both plus the rainforest.

A night either side of a sailing is worth booking rather than trusting a same-day flight. Our Caribbean cruises guide sets out the wider routes, and the Guild’s cruise desk covers planning in general.

Travelling in your later years

Honest notes for anyone who does not want surprises. Panama City is hot and humid in a way that takes a day or two to get used to, and Casco Viejo is cobbled, uneven and short of handrails — lovely in the evening, hard going at midday. Taxis are cheap and an app is easier than negotiating a fare; the metro is modern and air-conditioned but has stairs at some stations.

The water journeys are the thing to ask about in advance. Bocas del Toro and San Blas are reached by small open boats that you step down into from a dock, often in a swell, and the San Blas road crosses a steep pass in a four-wheel-drive. Ask the operator plainly how you board before you pay, and say if you need a hand. Coiba day trips are an hour and a half each way in open water.

Boquete is the gentlest base in the country — cool, flat enough in the town itself, and with a long-established community of older residents from North America, which means pharmacies, clinics and people who will speak English with you. Hospital care in Panama City is the best in the region; it thins out quickly in the islands and the highlands. Bring prescription medicines in their boxes with a copy of the prescription, and take insurance that will move you if it has to.

Getting in

Most North American and western European travellers are admitted as tourists without a visa, in the ordinary case for ninety days. Immigration may ask to see an onward or return ticket and evidence that you can support yourself, and your passport should have a comfortable margin of validity left on it. Tocumen is a busy connecting hub and the queue at passport control can be long when several wide-bodies land together; allow for it if you have a domestic flight to catch.

Entry rules differ by nationality and are changed without notice, so check what your own government publishes now rather than relying on a figure printed in any guide, this one included. Fishing here

The country is named for its fish — one long-standing reading of “Panamá” is an abundance of them — and the claim holds up. The Pacific side is the serious water: the Gulf of Chiriquí, the seamounts offshore and the drop-offs near the Darién coast have produced a long list of record catches, and Coiba sits in the middle of it. On the Caribbean side the shallows around Bocas del Toro and the San Blas keys hold tarpon, snook and permit in skinny water. Questions travellers ask about Panama

What money does Panama use?

The balboa, which has been fixed at one to the United States dollar for more than a century. In practice the paper money you handle is US dollars — Panama prints no notes of its own — and the coins are balboa coins the same size and weight as American ones, so the two mix in your pocket. For anyone coming from the United States that removes the usual holiday arithmetic entirely. Carry small notes: taxis, fondas and the islands are often unable to break a fifty.

When is the dry season?

Roughly mid-December to mid-April on the Pacific side, which is where the capital, the canal and most visitors are. That is the stretch of reliable sun, and it is also the busy and dearer one. The rest of the year is the green season: hot, humid and usually a heavy downpour in the afternoon rather than rain all day. The Caribbean side, including Bocas del Toro, keeps its own rhythm and often has a drier, calmer spell around September and October when the Pacific side is at its wettest.

Is Panama in the hurricane belt?

No, and that is one of the quiet advantages of the country. Panama lies far enough south that hurricanes essentially do not form over it or track across it, which is why insurers and cruise lines treat it differently from the islands further north. You will still meet tropical downpours, thunderstorms and rough water on the Caribbean coast in the wet months, so weather cover on your insurance is still sensible — but not for the reason it is sensible in Jamaica or the Bahamas.

Can I see the canal without taking a cruise?

Yes, easily, and most visitors do. The Miraflores locks on the Pacific side have a visitor centre with viewing terraces a short drive from Panama City, and the Agua Clara locks on the Caribbean side look down on the newer, larger chamber. Ships are not on a timetable you can rely on, so ask at your hotel which part of the day traffic is expected before you set out. Partial transits by small boat are also sold from the city if you want to go through a lock yourself.

How long should I stay?

A week is the honest minimum if you want more than the capital. Two or three nights in Panama City covers Casco Viejo, the canal and the rainforest on its doorstep; then most people fly or drive on to one coast or the other. Ten days lets you add Bocas del Toro or Boquete without rushing. Internal flights are short but the domestic schedule is thin, so build in slack rather than planning a tight connection on the day you fly home.

Do I need a visa for Panama?

Travellers from most of North America and western Europe are admitted as tourists without a visa, typically for ninety days, and the immigration service may ask to see an onward or return ticket and proof that you can pay your way. Your passport should have a comfortable margin of validity left. Rules differ by nationality and they change without notice, so check what your own government currently publishes before you book rather than taking a figure from any guide, ours included.

Is Panama City safe for visitors?

The parts of the city that visitors use — Casco Viejo, the banking district, the Cinta Costera, Amador — are ordinarily safe in daylight and busy in the evening, and the city is noticeably easier to move around than most Central American capitals. There are districts with a serious crime problem that no visitor has reason to enter, and the edges of Casco Viejo change character within a block or two after dark. Use booked taxis or an app at night rather than walking between neighbourhoods, and read your own government’s current advice.

What about the Darién Gap?

Do not go. The roadless forest along the Colombian border is the one part of the country that every government tells its citizens to stay out of, and it is a route used by organised crime as well as by people crossing on foot in great numbers. There is no scenic drive to South America from Panama: the Pan-American Highway stops. If you are travelling onward to Colombia, you fly, or you take a licensed boat along the San Blas coast.
