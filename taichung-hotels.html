Where to Stay in Taichung

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Taichung has the kindest weather in Taiwan — sheltered from both monsoons, warm and bright when Taipei is under cloud — and it is the natural base for the central mountains. It also has the one layout trap in the country worth knowing before you book: the high speed rail station and the main railway station are in different places, six kilometres apart. Pick your district by which of them you will actually use.

The old city, around Taichung Station — cheap, central, improving

Cheapest bedsConventional railWalkable

The original centre, around the handsome old brick station, is the cheapest place to sleep in Taichung and the most interesting to wander. This is where Miyahara, the old Japanese optician’s turned extravagant ice-cream and gift hall, sits, along with the Second Market, the Taiwan Boulevard end of the night-market trail and a run of Japanese-era civic buildings. It has been slowly regenerating for a decade and parts of it still look half-finished. The practical point: this station is on the conventional railway, which is the one you want for Changhua, Lukang and the west coast towns, and it is twenty-five minutes by shuttle train from the high speed rail. Stay here if you are arriving by ordinary train or bus and want to spend as little as possible.

The Seventh District and the West District — the good part of town

Best eatingMuseumsNewer hotels

West and north-west of the old centre lies the part of Taichung that people actually recommend: the Calligraphy Greenway, a long green spine of park running past the National Taiwan Museum of Fine Arts and the Natural Science Museum, with the Seventh District’s cafes, bakeries and small restaurants either side of it. The National Taichung Theater, Toyo Ito’s cave-like concert hall, is here, and so is most of the city’s better accommodation. It is a flat twenty-minute walk or a short taxi from the old station and it is where we would stay with no particular constraint. The drawback is that Taichung’s metro does not serve it well, so you will use taxis and buses.

Fengjia — the student quarter and the biggest night market in Taiwan

Very cheapNight marketLoud

Around Feng Chia University in the north-west, Fengjia Night Market is the largest in the country and runs every night of the week, which makes the surrounding streets the cheapest and liveliest beds in Taichung and the natural choice for a younger traveller. Everything is open late, the food is excellent and relentless, and there are hundreds of small hotels and guest houses. The honest drawbacks are real: it is a long way from the station and from the museums, it is genuinely loud until one or two in the morning, and it is a student district rather than a pretty one.

Wuri and the high speed rail station — for the fast train and the mountains

Fast trainSun Moon Lake busesNot an evening

The high speed rail stops at Xinwuri, south-west of the city, where a large modern station sits beside the conventional line’s Xinwuri stop and the Taichung metro’s southern terminus. It is also where the direct buses to Sun Moon Lake and several mountain services leave from. A few big hotels have gone up around it, and they are the right choice for exactly two situations: an early fast train south, or a dawn bus to the lake. Otherwise do not stay here. There is nothing to walk to in the evening, and the city proper is twenty-five minutes away.

Getting around

Taichung has one metro line, the green line, which runs from the high speed rail station in the south-west up through the eastern suburbs to Beitun. It is modern and useful at the two ends and it does not go to most of the places a visitor wants, which is the single most common disappointment here. The city buses do, they are frequent on the main corridors, and an iPASS or EasyCard pays for all of it.

Because of that, taxis matter more in Taichung than in Taipei or Kaohsiung. They are metered, cheap and easy to find at the stations and the malls, and a ride across the middle of town costs very little. Ride-hailing works well here too. The city is flat, so bicycles are a real option in the cooler months.

For the mountains, the practical routes are the direct bus to Sun Moon Lake from the high speed rail station or the old station, and the train south to Chiayi for the Alishan forest railway. Both are long day trips rather than half days: leave early, and if the weather in the hills looks poor, swap the day rather than go anyway.

When to come

Taiwan’s weather is split in two by the mountain range down its middle, and Taichung sits on the sheltered central western side of it. October and November are the best weeks of the year here, as they are everywhere on the island: warm, dry, clear and comfortable, with the typhoon season finished. Late March and April are the next best, before the humidity arrives and before the plum rains of mid-May to mid-June.

Summer, June to September, is hot and humid with heavy afternoon rain, and it is the typhoon window; it is also the cheapest time for a room and the best time for the high mountains that Taichung is the gateway to. Winter is where Taichung earns its keep. Sheltered from the northeast monsoon by the hills to the north and east, the city stays milder, drier and brighter from December to February than Taipei does, which makes it a good place to break a winter trip up or down the island.

The one week to book around, wherever you are going, is Lunar New Year, in late January or February: the whole island travels at once, trains sell out, room rates roughly double and many small restaurants close for several days. Getting in

Citizens of the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, the European Union and Schengen countries, Australia, New Zealand, Japan and South Korea, among others, are admitted to Taiwan visa-free for ninety days, with no fee and no application. Some other nationalities get thirty or fourteen days and some need a visa in advance, and the list is revised from time to time, so check your own passport at boca.gov.tw. There is also a free Taiwan Online Arrival Card to complete shortly before you land; use the official government site and not an agent charging for the same form.

Buy an EasyCard or an iPASS in the arrivals hall and keep it in your pocket for the rest of the trip: it pays for the metros, the buses, the local trains, the ferries and the hire bicycles the length of the island, and for your coffee in the convenience store. Taichung hotels FAQ

Which Taichung station will my train use?

That depends on which train. The high speed rail uses Taichung HSR station at Xinwuri, south-west of the city; the conventional railway, which covers Changhua, Lukang, Chiayi and the local stops, uses Taichung Station in the old centre. They are about six kilometres apart and linked by a shuttle train that takes roughly twenty-five minutes, plus the metro's green line. Check which one your ticket says before you book a hotel, because guessing wrong costs you an hour on arrival and again on departure.

Where should I stay for Sun Moon Lake?

In the city, and then take the direct bus out for the day, or near the high speed rail station if you want the earliest departure. Direct coaches run to the lake from both the HSR station and the old station and take about two hours. If the lake is the main point of your trip rather than a day out, consider staying at the lake itself for a night instead, since it is at its best early and late in the day when the coaches have gone.

Is Taichung worth a stop at all?

Yes, for three reasons. It has the most reliable weather in Taiwan, which makes it the right stop in a wet northern winter; it is the gateway to the central mountains; and the stretch between the Calligraphy Greenway and the Seventh District is genuinely pleasant to spend a day and an evening in. What it does not have is a single unmissable sight, so two nights is usually the right length.

Is the metro useful?

Only partly, and this surprises people. The green line is new and good, but it runs from the high speed rail station through the eastern side of the city and misses the old centre, the Seventh District, the museums and Fengjia. Plan on buses and cheap metered taxis for most journeys, with the metro for getting to and from the fast train.

Is Fengjia too noisy to sleep in?

For some people, yes. The night market runs every night and the surrounding streets are busy until one or two in the morning, every day of the week. If you want to be in the middle of it for the food and the price, ask for a high floor away from the street and bring earplugs. If you want quiet, stay in the Seventh District instead and come to Fengjia for the evening by taxi; it is fifteen minutes.
