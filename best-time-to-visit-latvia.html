Latvia through the year

Best Time to Visit Latvia

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Latvia has four seasons that behave like four separate countries. Midsummer brings light that never quite goes out and a national holiday the whole population disappears into; autumn turns the Gauja valley gold and empties the hotels; winter brings real snow, a frozen gulf edge and the cheapest rooms of the year; and spring is late, muddy and then suddenly green. Nothing here is unusable and nothing here is interchangeable, so the month you pick decides what sort of trip you get. Here is the year as it actually runs.

The short answer

Come in June for the light, the flowers and the warm evenings, avoiding or embracing the midsummer holiday on the 23rd and 24th. Come in late September or early October for the best-looking country of the year, the autumn colour in the Gauja valley and almost no other visitors — this is our own pick. Come in July or August if you want to swim, because the shallow Baltic takes that long to warm. Come in January or February if you want snow, the Opera, an empty city and the lowest prices of the year, and accept that it will be dark by three. Think twice about March and November, which are the thaw and the dark respectively, and the only two months we would steer a first visit away from.

Latvia month by month

January and February

Deep winter, and the real thing: snow lying for weeks at a time, temperatures below freezing for most of it, and the edges of the Gulf of Riga icing over. It is dark by the middle of the afternoon. This is Riga indoors — the Art Museum, the Opera, the Corner House, the market pavilions, long lunches — and it is the cheapest time of the year to do all of it. Out of town, ice fishing is a serious pastime and the bogs and forests under snow are beautiful and very quiet. Bring boots with grip: the cobbles ice up.

March

The thaw, and the least attractive month in Latvia. The snow turns to slush and then to brown, the sky stays grey, the countryside is mud and the trees are still bare. Prices are low and the city is yours, but nothing is at its best. If you have a free choice, pick almost any other month; if you are coming anyway, make it a city trip rather than a country one.

April

Spring arrives late here and then arrives all at once. The woods flower before they leaf — anemones and then lily of the valley — the storks come back to their poles in the fields, and the Gauja can run high and brown with meltwater. Daylight lengthens fast. It is still cold enough for a proper coat and still wet enough for boots, but by the end of the month Riga's parks and the boulevard ring have turned green and the cafe tables come out.

May

One of the two best months. Bird cherry and then lilac come out across Riga, the Gauja valley is in new leaf, the evenings stretch out towards the white nights and the mosquitoes have not properly started. The sea is too cold to swim in but the beach at Jurmala is perfect for walking. Two dates to know: Restoration of Independence Day on 4 May, and the Museum Night in Riga towards the end of the month, when the museums open late and are free.

June

The prize. Light until nearly midnight and never fully dark, lindens in flower and scenting whole streets, the beach warming up, and the countryside at its greenest. The one thing to plan around is Jani, midsummer on 23 and 24 June, which is the biggest holiday in the Latvian year: the cities empty, the countryside fills with bonfires, oak wreaths, cheese with caraway and people staying up for the short night. Both days are public holidays and much of the country simply does not work. Join one if you are invited; do not expect to get business done.

July

The warmest and busiest month, and the only time Jurmala is genuinely full. The sea is warm enough to swim in, the Riga City Festival fills the Old Town in the middle of the month, and the thunderstorms arrive in the afternoons and clear by evening. Wild strawberries give way to blueberries in the forests. Book Jurmala ahead if you want the coast in July; Riga itself is easier, because a good number of its own residents have left for the sea.

August

The best swimming of the year, because the shallow Baltic takes this long to warm properly, and the start of the mushroom and berry season that sends the whole country into the woods at weekends. The light begins to go noticeably by the end of the month and the first cool nights arrive. 23 August is the anniversary of the Baltic Way of 1989, when about two million people joined hands in a chain from Tallinn through Riga to Vilnius; it is marked rather than celebrated.

September

Our pick for a first visit. The crowds have gone, the weather is still kind in the first half of the month, the light turns long and gold, and the first colour comes into the Gauja valley in the last week. The sea is swimmable at the start and not at the end. Prices drop back from the summer. It is also the mushroom month, and the markets fill with chanterelles, boletus and a great deal of honey.

October

The country at its best looking. Peak colour in the Gauja valley falls in the first fortnight, and Sigulda and Cesis are what the trains out of Riga are full of. Cranes and geese move through in long skeins. The weather is wet and windy and the first frosts arrive, and none of that matters much. By the end of the month the leaves are down, the clocks have changed and the dark part of the year has begun.

November

The darkest and wettest month, and the one to avoid if you have a choice. Bare trees, short grey days, rain and wind, and not yet any snow to brighten it. It carries the most important date in the Latvian calendar all the same: Independence Day on 18 November, the proclamation of the Republic in 1918, marked with a parade, a torchlight procession through Riga and fireworks over the Daugava, and worth being there for. The Christmas market in the Dome Square opens in the last days of the month.

December

Short days, long evenings and Riga at its most charming if the snow has come. The Christmas market runs in the Dome Square and in Livu Square with mulled wine, grey peas and craft stalls, the Opera and the concert season are in full swing, and the city makes much of its claim to have put up Europe's first decorated public Christmas tree in 1510 — a claim Tallinn also makes, and both cities are cheerfully certain. Expect it to be dark by four. Expect also that it may be brown rather than white: a green Christmas is no longer unusual here.

The weather, and what it does to a trip

The coast and the inland are two climates

The Baltic moderates everything near it: the coast at Liepaja and Ventspils is milder in winter and cooler in summer than Riga, and Latgale in the south-east is the other way round — colder winters, warmer summers, more snow. A forecast for Riga is a fair guide for Jurmala and Sigulda and a poor one for Daugavpils.

The light matters more than the temperature

Latvians will tell you that the cold is manageable and the dark is the thing. In late June the sky never goes properly black; in late December you get around six and a half hours of grey daylight. Plan outdoor sightseeing for the middle of a winter day and let the evenings be museums, concerts and dinner, which is how the country itself does it.

Snow is normal, and so is a brown winter

A Latvian winter usually brings weeks of lying snow, and when it does the forests and the frozen gulf are the reason to come. But mild winters have become common enough that a December visit may find rain and brown grass instead. If snow is the point of your trip, come in late January or February rather than at Christmas, and keep your plans loose.

Ticks and mosquitoes, honestly

If you walk in forest, long grass or near the bogs between about May and September, you will meet mosquitoes, and ticks are present across the Baltic region. Cover your legs, use repellent and check yourself after a walk. Latvians treat this as ordinary rather than alarming, and so should you — but if you plan a lot of time in the woods, ask your own doctor well before you travel what they advise for the region.

Dates worth planning around

Some of these fill every room in Riga. One of them closes the country for two days.

Jani — 23 and 24 June

Midsummer, and the biggest holiday of the Latvian year by a long way. Bonfires, oak and flower wreaths, caraway cheese, beer and a night nobody sleeps through. Both days are public holidays, the cities empty into the countryside and a great many businesses close. Marvellous if you are invited to a country celebration, and bewildering if you turn up expecting a working city.

Independence Day — 18 November

The proclamation of the Republic of Latvia in 1918. A parade, a torchlight procession through Riga and fireworks over the Daugava, with the whole city out in the cold. The most moving public day in the calendar and a good reason to put up with the worst month of the year.

Restoration of Independence Day — 4 May

The 1990 declaration restoring independence from the Soviet Union, marked quietly rather than loudly, with flowers at the Freedom Monument. The two independence days together tell you most of what you need to know about the twentieth century here.

The Song and Dance Celebration — about every five years

Tens of thousands of singers and dancers from across the country converge on Riga for a week of massed choirs, folk dancing and processions, in a tradition running back to 1873 and listed by UNESCO as intangible cultural heritage. The last nationwide celebration was in 2023 and the next is set for the summer of 2028. If your travel year is one of them, book a Riga room a long way ahead and check the dates, because they are announced well in advance and the city fills completely.

The Baltic Way anniversary — 23 August

In 1989 around two million people formed a human chain from Tallinn through Riga to Vilnius against Soviet rule. The anniversary is marked rather than celebrated, and in some years with events along the old route. It is worth knowing the date before you ask anybody about it.

The Riga Christmas market — late November to early January

Dome Square is the main one, with a second in Livu Square and lights through the Old Town. Weekends draw visitors from across the region; a weekday evening is far better. Dates shift a little each year, so check before fixing travel around them.

Liepaja as a European Capital of Culture — 2027

Liepaja holds the title in 2027, which means a year-long programme in a city that already runs on music. If your trip falls in that year it is worth building the Kurzeme part of it around whatever is on, and worth booking earlier than you otherwise would.

What it costs, and when

Latvia is among the cheaper countries in the euro area in every month, but the spread across the year is wide. The two expensive windows are the midsummer fortnight around Jani and the Christmas market weeks, and in Jurmala the whole of July and August. The cheap window is long: January through mid-March, and again in November. Riga’s Old Town charges a premium in every season and one street out of it does not, which is the single most useful thing to know about prices here.

Fishing here

The season shapes the fishing as much as everything else. Spring and autumn are the sea trout and salmon runs on the Gauja and the Salaca, both licensed and both restricted; high summer is pike, perch and bream on the Daugava, the Lielupe and the Latgale lakes; and winter is ice fishing, which in Latvia is an institution rather than an eccentricity. An angling card is required year round and some stretches are leased, so arrange it locally. The Guild has no Latvia guide on the shelf yet; the whole shelf is in The Guild Fishing Library.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Latvia is in the European Union, the Schengen area and the euro, so arrival from elsewhere in Schengen involves no check at all. Riga’s airport is the main hub for the three Baltic states and busiest in the summer months; if you are coming for the Song and Dance Celebration or for midsummer, book flights and rooms a long way ahead.

Latvia’s seasons — questions

What is the single best month to visit Latvia?

June, if you can only pick one. The light lasts until nearly midnight, the lilac and then the linden are in flower, the Gauja valley is green and the beach at Jurmala has warmed enough to be worth a towel, all without the full heat or the full crowds of July. The one caveat is Jani, the midsummer holiday on 23 and 24 June, when the country shuts and heads for the countryside. If you would rather have the country at its best looking than at its warmest, come in the first fortnight of October instead.

Is a Latvian winter really worth it?

Yes, with eyes open. From December to February it is cold, it is dark by mid-afternoon and the snow is real rather than decorative. What you get in exchange is Riga at its most atmospheric and its cheapest, a Christmas market in the Dome Square, the Opera and the concert halls in full season, markets and museums that are warm and uncrowded, and the strange sight of the Gulf of Riga frozen at the edges with people walking out on it. What you do not get is daylight for sightseeing after about three in the afternoon.

How light is midsummer, and how dark is December?

Riga sits at about fifty-seven degrees north, which is further north than any of the lower forty-eight states. Around the solstice in late June the sun is up for roughly eighteen hours and the sky never goes fully black, which is why those weeks are called the white nights. Around the solstice in December it is up for about six and a half hours and the middle of the day is a long grey dusk. Plan sightseeing for the daylight in winter and plan for the evenings in summer, because in June nobody goes to bed.

When can you actually swim in the sea?

July and August, and the first half of September for the hardy. The Baltic is shallow and brackish and it warms up slowly, so June is bracing even on a hot day and May is for paddling only. The Gulf of Riga at Jurmala is noticeably warmer and calmer than the open sea at Liepaja or Ventspils, because it is sheltered and very shallow a long way out, which also makes it the better coast for children.

When does the autumn colour peak?

The last week of September and the first two weeks of October, with the Gauja valley around Sigulda and Cesis the place to see it. The valley is beech, birch, aspen, maple and oak over sandstone cliffs, and when it turns, the trains out of Riga fill with Latvians going to look at it. The local name for the season translates as golden autumn, and the cable car across the gorge is the easiest place to stand in it. It ends abruptly with the first serious wind.

What should I pack?

Layers in every season and waterproof shoes in all but high summer. In winter you want a proper coat, a hat, gloves and boots with grip, because pavements ice over and the Old Town cobbles get slick. In summer you want a light waterproof for the thunderstorms, something with sleeves for the evenings, and insect repellent if you plan to walk in the forest or near the bogs. Latvia is informal, and nobody dresses up except for the Opera.

When is Latvia cheapest?

January through to the middle of March, once the Christmas market has closed. Riga hotel rooms are at their lowest, Jurmala is almost empty and the restaurants are full of locals rather than visitors. The second-cheapest window is November, which is the darkest and wettest month and the one we would recommend last for a first visit. The dearest weeks are the midsummer fortnight around Jani and the Christmas market period, in that order.
