Budva

Where to Stay in Budva

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Budva is the busiest resort on the Montenegrin coast and it is two quite different places wearing one name. There is a small walled old town on a headland, handsome and about the size of a large village, and there is a modern strip of apartment blocks, beach clubs and nightlife that runs north from it along a long shallow bay. Choosing between them is most of the decision. South of the town the coast gets prettier and quieter almost immediately — Bečići and Rafailovići, then Sveti Stefan and Miločer, then Petrovac — and it is worth knowing that you can have the Budva riviera without the Budva nightlife if you move a few kilometres.

The old town and the walls

Most atmosphericCompactLoud in high summer

The walled town on the headland is small enough to cross in five minutes and is the one part of Budva that looks like the pictures: stone lanes, three churches in a row, a little citadel with a view down both beaches. Rooms inside the walls are limited and mostly in converted houses, which means character, stairs, and no vehicle access to the door. It is lovely in the shoulder months. In July and August it is the centre of a very loud resort, and the clubs on the edge of the old town run until it gets light, so this is not the place to book if you want early nights.

Slovenska Plaza and the strip

Beach on the doorstepMost choiceModern and busy

North of the old town the long curve of Slovenska Plaža is backed by the bulk of Budva's hotels and apartments, with the promenade, the beach bars and the marina all a short walk apart. This is where the choice is widest and where families mostly end up, because everything is flat, close together and geared to the beach. It is a modern resort strip rather than anything historic, the beach is fine shingle and sand and is packed in high season, and the further north you go the further you are from the old town — ten minutes at the near end, half an hour at the top.

Bečići and Rafailovići

Better beachQuieter nightsShort hop to town

Over the headland south of Budva, Bečići has the longest and probably the best beach on this stretch, and Rafailovići at its far end is a small fishing village that has kept more of its character than anywhere else close to Budva. You are five to ten minutes from town by road, there is a coastal path over the headland for walking it, and the evenings are noticeably calmer. For most people wanting a beach holiday with Budva available rather than unavoidable, this is the better address. Trip.com files this shore under Budva, so the browse link below is the Budva list.

Sveti Stefan and Miločer

The famous viewExpensiveVery quiet

The fortified islet of Sveti Stefan, joined to the shore by a causeway, is the picture on the front of every brochure about Montenegro. The island itself is a resort rather than a village: it closed for five years after a dispute over access to the beaches and reopened to guests in the summer of 2026, with the state saying local people keep free use of two of the three beaches beside it and the third reserved for the resort. For everyone else the point of staying here is the village on the mainland above the causeway, which is quiet, well placed between Budva and Petrovac, and has the best view in the country from its terraces. Check what access any booking actually includes before you pay.

Petrovac

Family favouriteSmall-town feelFewer late bars

Twenty minutes further south, Petrovac is a small red-roofed town around a short beach with a pine-covered headland at each end and a promenade that takes ten minutes to walk end to end. It is the place Montenegrin families go, it is markedly cheaper than Budva, and it keeps a town life out of season when the strip up the coast has shut. If you want somewhere to sit still for a week with a car for day trips, Petrovac does that better than anywhere else on this coast.

Getting around Budva

On foot

The old town, the marina and Slovenska Plaža are one flat walk, and the promenade carries you most of the way. Beyond that the headlands get in the way: Bečići is a hill or a bus ride away, and Sveti Stefan and Petrovac are road journeys rather than walks.

Buses along the coast

The coastal buses are frequent, cheap and the way most people move between Budva, Kotor, Herceg Novi, Bar and Ulcinj. They run along the main road rather than the shore, so you are often set down at the top of a hill with a walk down to the water. Out of season the frequency drops a long way.

Driving and parking

Budva in August is the hardest place in the country to park, and the town is built around a one-way system that does not reward improvisation; book parking with the room if you possibly can. The road south towards Sveti Stefan and Petrovac is slow but very pretty, and the inland road up to Cetinje and Lovćen is the fast way to the mountains.

Boats and taxis

In summer small boats run from the marina along the coast and out to the islands, which is a pleasant way to see the shoreline and to reach beaches with no road. Taxis are plentiful; agree the fare or insist on the meter before you set off.

When to come, and what it costs

Budva is the clearest case on this coast of a town with two seasons and very little in between. July and August are hot, full and expensive, with the nightlife at its loudest and the beaches at their most crowded; a great many visitors love it exactly for that. June and September give you the same sea at a lower temperature of everything else, and May and early October are quiet, green and cheap, with warm days, a sea that is either not yet or no longer comfortable for long swims, and a fair number of seasonal places closed. From November to March the strip largely shuts and the old town becomes a small Adriatic town again.

Getting in: Montenegro is outside the EU and outside Schengen

Montenegro uses the euro but is not in the European Union and not in the Schengen area. That has one very useful consequence for a traveller: days spent here do not count against the ninety days in any hundred and eighty that Schengen allows, and the reverse is also true. Citizens of the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, Australia and the European Union do not need a visa for a short holiday, with ninety days in any hundred and eighty the usual allowance — confirm your own nationality’s terms with your own foreign ministry rather than with any website. Your passport should have been issued within the last ten years and be valid for at least three months beyond your departure, and you should make sure it is stamped on the way in and out, because those stamps are the record of your stay.

Every stay must be reported to the police. Montenegro’s own government says that duty falls on the accommodation provider, and hotels do it automatically at check-in; British government advice tells visitors to make sure they are registered within twenty-four hours and warns of a fine if they are not. In a hotel it is handled for you. In a private flat, ask your host to confirm it is done and keep the slip. Crossing to or from Croatia is a Schengen border with full checks and, in high summer, queues — and if you are driving a hire car across it, confirm first that the rental company permits it.

Budva — questions

Old town or the beach strip?

Old town if you are here for a few nights in the shoulder season and want atmosphere; the strip if you want the beach on the doorstep, a lift, a pool and somewhere to put the car. In July and August the honest answer for most people is neither — stay at Bečići or Rafailovići and walk or take the bus in, because you get the better beach and a quieter night for the same money.

Is Budva too noisy?

In high summer the area immediately around the old town genuinely is: the clubs there are among the best known in the region and they run very late. It is a local problem rather than a town-wide one. A few hundred metres north along the strip, or over the hill at Bečići, the nights are ordinary. Out of season the question does not arise.

Can I visit Sveti Stefan?

You can walk down to the causeway and photograph it from the road above, which is what nearly everyone does and is free. The island itself is a resort: it was closed for five years after a row over beach access and reopened to guests in the summer of 2026, so entry depends on being a guest or on whatever arrangement the resort is operating when you visit. The beaches on either side are the thing to check — the settlement provides for free local use of two of the three, with the third reserved. Confirm access before booking anything on the strength of it.

Is this a good base for day trips?

Very good for the coast and the near mountains: Kotor and Perast are under an hour, Cetinje and Lovćen about the same up the hairpins, and Bar and Ulcinj an easy run south. Podgorica is about an hour. The northern mountains around Žabljak are a long day each way and deserve a night of their own. A car makes all of this far easier than the buses do.

When is the sea warm enough to swim?

Broadly from June to September, warmest in August and still comfortable for most people well into September, which is why that month is such good value here. May and October are swimmable for the hardy and pleasant for everything else. We would rather say that than print a temperature — the Adriatic varies year to year, and a figure written in advance is a guess.
