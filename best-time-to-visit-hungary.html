Best Time to Visit Hungary

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Hungary has a continental climate and very little hedging in it: proper hot summers, proper cold winters, and two short shoulder seasons that are the best time to be here. The decision is usually between heat and company. Come in July and you get Lake Balaton at its warmest and Budapest at its emptiest of locals and fullest of everybody else; come in late September and you get the grape harvest, warm water, thin crowds and a lower bill. What follows is the year month by month, with the public holidays that will close a museum or fill a train.

The short answer

Three ways to choose, depending on what you are coming for.

For the cities

Late May to June, or September. Warm but not punishing, long light, tables out on the Danube embankment, and the queues manageable.

For value & quiet

November, or January after the first week. Cold and dark, genuinely cheap, the museums nearly empty and the baths at their best.

For the lake

Late June to early September. Balaton is shallow and warms fast; outside those weeks much of the shore is closed.

Month by month

What each month is actually like, for weather, crowds and price.

January

The coldest month, often below freezing all day, with grey skies and short afternoons. After the first week it is the cheapest time of year in Budapest, the concert season is in full swing, and the outdoor pools at Széchenyi are at their most atmospheric with snow on the stonework.

February

Still properly cold but the light is returning. Hungarian school holidays fall somewhere in the month and fill the hills, though not in a way a visitor notices much. Cities stay quiet and inexpensive. A good month for baths, museums and long lunches.

March

Thaw and mud, and not Hungary’s most flattering month, but it ends better than it starts. The fifteenth is a national day with parades and closures. Prices are still low and the cities are uncrowded.

April

Spring arrives, unreliably — warm days and cold days in the same week, blossom in the Buda hills, and Easter somewhere in the month, which closes a good deal on the Sunday and Monday. Lake Balaton is beautiful and mostly shut.

May

Lovely, and our joint pick with September. Green, long days, comfortable walking weather. The first of May is a public holiday that often merges into a long weekend. By the end of the month terraces are open everywhere and the lake is waking up.

June

Warm, long and bright, with the solstice giving close to sixteen hours of light. Swimming at Balaton becomes comfortable from about the middle of the month. Thunderstorms build in the afternoons and pass quickly. The last weeks before the school holidays are a sweet spot.

July

High summer and properly hot, often in the low to middle thirties Celsius. Hungarians head for the lake, so Balaton is full and dear while Budapest can feel emptied of locals. Plan the afternoons around water or shade, and check your room has air conditioning.

August

As July, with the heat sometimes worse. The lake is at its warmest and busiest. The twentieth is Saint Stephen’s Day, the biggest holiday of the year, with an enormous firework display over the Danube and the city packed for it. Book rooms months ahead for that week.

September

The best month of the year, to our mind. Warm into the second half, clear light, the lake still swimmable at the start, the crowds gone and prices down. The grape harvest runs through the month in Tokaj, Eger and Villány, which is the most enjoyable time to visit a cellar.

October

Autumn proper, and very beautiful in the northern hills and the Buda woods. Cool, often bright, sometimes wet. The twenty-third is a national day. All Saints on the first of November closes things and fills the trains, so plan around the last weekend.

November

The month we would skip, unless the price is the point. Short days, grey skies, damp cold and little on — until the middle of the month, when the Christmas markets open and Budapest becomes rather wonderful again.

December

Dark and cold, redeemed entirely by the markets, the lights along Andrássy and the baths. Book ahead for the weekends before Christmas. Christmas Day itself is very quiet; New Year in Budapest is busy and expensive.

Which part of Hungary, and when

The country is small, but its seasons are not all the same.

Budapest

All year. The baths, the concert halls and the museums make winter genuinely worth it, and the markets from mid November are a reason in themselves. Avoid the worst of the August heat if you can, and book well ahead for the week of the twentieth.

Lake Balaton

Mid June to early September, and really only then. Outside those weeks the water is cold and much of the shore — hotels, ferries, restaurants — is closed. May and late September are pleasant for walking the north shore and tasting the Badacsony wines without swimming.

The wine country

September and early October, for the harvest, when the cellars at Eger, Tokaj and Villány are at their busiest and most welcoming. Spring is quieter and perfectly good. Winter is cold and many small cellars keep short hours or open only by arrangement.

The Great Plain

Late spring and early autumn. The plain has no shade and July on the puszta is hard going; it is also at its best when the grass is green in May or turning in September. Debrecen itself, with its forest park and baths, is comfortable year-round.

Public holidays worth planning around

Hungary keeps its national days seriously. These are the ones that will change what is open or how full a train is.

15 March

The 1848 revolution. Speeches, flags and cockades, museums free in some years, offices and many shops closed. A good day to be in Budapest if you like a public occasion.

Easter & Whitsun

Good Friday, Easter Monday and Whit Monday are all holidays. Much closes on the Sunday and Monday, and Hungarians travel to family, which fills the roads and trains either side.

1 May

Labour Day, and the start of the outdoor season in practice. Often merges with the March or Easter weekends into a long break that fills the lake and the hills.

20 August

Saint Stephen’s Day, the founding of the state and the biggest day of the year. A huge firework display over the Danube, Budapest packed, rooms booked months ahead and priced accordingly.

23 October

The 1956 revolution and the proclamation of the republic. Ceremonies, closures and a quieter, more sombre feel than March.

1 November & Christmas

All Saints fills the trains as families visit graves. Christmas Day and Boxing Day close almost everything, including many restaurants; the markets wind down and the city goes very quiet indeed.

Hungary uses the Hungarian forint (HUF, written Ft), not the euro. Hungary has been in the European Union since 2004 but has kept its own currency and has set no date for changing it, so euro notes are not legal tender. A few hotels and tour desks quote in euros and some will take euro cash, almost always at a poor rate. Pay in forint. Cards and phones are taken nearly everywhere, including on buses, trams and at ticket machines, and you need very little cash — a small amount is handy for market stalls, thermal-bath lockers, church donation boxes and rural buses. Prices in shops include tax. In Budapest restaurants a service charge is often printed on the bill; check before adding a tip, and where there is none, ten per cent is plenty. Decline any card machine offering to charge you in dollars and let your own bank convert. Avoid the standalone exchange booths near the main tourist streets and at the airport, which are far worse than a bank machine or an ordinary exchange office in town.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS — the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years — is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today — nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Hungary is a straightforward case. It has been in the European Union since 2004 and in the Schengen area since the end of 2007, so everything above applies here in full, and flying in from Vienna, Munich or Amsterdam involves no passport control at all. What Hungary has not joined is the euro, so the currency is the forint. Its borders with Austria, Slovakia, Slovenia, Croatia and Romania are all internal Schengen crossings now and you will usually drive or ride straight through them. The borders with Serbia and with Ukraine are external Schengen frontiers, where there are proper checks and the queues can be long. None of that affects an ordinary trip to the places in this guide.

When to visit Hungary — questions

What is the best month to visit Hungary?

September, with late May and June close behind. In September the weather is still warm, Lake Balaton holds its summer heat into the middle of the month, the harvest is on in every wine region, and the August crowds and prices have gone. Late May and June give you long days and green countryside before the serious heat arrives. If your trip is only Budapest and the baths, the shoulder months matter less and winter has a real charm of its own.

How hot does Budapest get in summer?

Hot. July and August regularly reach the low to middle thirties Celsius, which is the nineties Fahrenheit, and a heatwave pushing higher is normal at least once a summer. The city is built of stone and holds the heat into the evening. Much of the older housing stock has no air conditioning, so check that your room does rather than assume it, and plan the middle of the afternoon around a bath, a cellar or a shaded terrace.

When is Lake Balaton warm enough to swim?

The lake is shallow, which means it warms quickly and cools quickly. Swimming is comfortable from roughly the middle of June to the middle of September, and the water is at its warmest in late July and August, when it can feel like a bath. The resort season runs with it: many hotels, restaurants and ferries on the lake open around May and close again in September, so a visit in April or October finds a beautiful but largely shuttered shoreline.

Is winter a bad time to come?

No, it is simply a different trip. December through February is cold, often grey, and much the cheapest time of year. Budapest's Christmas markets run from the middle of November to early January, the museums and concert halls are at their best, and sitting outdoors in a steaming thermal pool while it snows is one of the things Hungary does that nowhere else does as well. What you lose is the countryside, the lake and the long evenings.

When are the Christmas markets on?

Budapest's main markets, on Vorosmarty ter and outside St Stephen's Basilica, open in the middle of November and run to the end of December or the first days of January, with the basilica market usually the later of the two. Other cities have smaller markets over the same period. Weekends in December are busy and hotel prices rise, so book early; weekday mornings are calm.

Which month is cheapest?

January after the first week, and November before the markets open, are the two cheapest stretches of the year in Budapest. Both are cold and dark and both are genuinely good value, with the museums almost empty. On Lake Balaton the whole closed season from October to April is cheap, but a great deal there is shut.

Do public holidays disrupt a trip?

A few do. The twentieth of August, Saint Stephen's Day, is the biggest day of the Hungarian year and brings an enormous fireworks display over the Danube in Budapest - worth seeing, but the city is packed and rooms are booked long ahead. The first of November fills the trains as families visit graves. Easter Monday, Whit Monday, the first of May and the two March and October national days close offices and many shops, though in Budapest the restaurants, baths and major museums usually stay open. Check opening hours for the day you plan a particular museum.

What should I pack?

For summer, light clothes, a hat and a refillable water bottle, plus a swimming costume, flip-flops and a quick-drying towel for the baths, which you will use more than you expect. For winter, a proper coat, gloves and shoes with grip, because the pavements ice. In spring and autumn bring layers and something waterproof. All year, shoes you can walk cobbles in, and modest cover for shoulders and knees if you plan to go inside the churches.
