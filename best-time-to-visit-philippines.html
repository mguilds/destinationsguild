When to go

Best Time to Visit the Philippines

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated August 2026

The short answer is February to April, and March if you can only have one month. The longer answer is more useful, because the Philippines is an archipelago fifteen hundred kilometres from top to bottom, the two monsoons arrive from opposite directions, and the island that is at its worst in August may be at its best in November. This page runs through the seasons, then the twelve months one at a time, then the best time for each island group — and it tries to be plain about the typhoon season rather than either alarming you or pretending it is not there.

The two seasons, and the two monsoons that make them

Learn these two words and the Philippine year becomes legible: amihan and habagat.

The amihan — December to February

Coolest and driestPeak seasonChoppy on eastern coasts

The amihan is the northeast monsoon, and despite the word monsoon it is the dry one: cooler, clearer air pushed down from the north, with the most comfortable temperatures of the Philippine year. This is when the country looks like its photographs. The one thing it brings that catches people out is wind. Eastern and northern coasts can get a steady blow that makes small-boat crossings uncomfortable or cancels them altogether, and on Boracay it shifts the calm water from the famous White Beach around to Bulabog on the other side, which is why the kitesurfers come in these months and the swimmers move. Book early: this season includes the two most expensive weeks of the year.

The hot dry months — March to May

Calmest seasBest diving visibilityMay is punishing

As the amihan fades the wind drops, the sea flattens and the country enters what Filipinos simply call summer. March and April are the finest weeks of the year for island travel — the boats go out, the water is clear, and the underwater visibility that draws divers to Palawan, Malapascua and Apo Reef is at its best. The cost is heat, which climbs steadily through April and peaks in May, the hottest month, when the lowlands become genuinely draining and even Filipinos head for the hills. If you feel the heat, come in February or March rather than May.

The habagat — June to early November

Green and cheapAfternoon downpoursQuietest islands

The habagat is the southwest monsoon and it brings the rain, usually as heavy afternoon and evening downpours with bright mornings rather than the unbroken grey people imagine. Rooms are considerably cheaper, the islands are green and uncrowded, and a well-planned wet-season trip can be the best value in Asia. The rain falls unevenly across the country: the western sides of the islands, including Manila, Boracay and much of Palawan, take the brunt of the habagat, while the eastern coasts — Siargao, eastern Mindanao, Samar — are comparatively sheltered and have their finest weather in exactly these months.

Typhoon season — June to November, worst August to October

Peak Aug–OctEastern coasts most exposedPalawan largely spared

The Philippines sits in the most active tropical cyclone basin in the world and a substantial number of storms enter its watch area each year, though only a fraction make a serious landfall. The track matters more than the count: storms come from the east, so eastern Luzon, Samar, Leyte and the northern Visayas are the exposed coasts, while Palawan and the far south of Mindanao lie mostly below and to the west of the main path. For a traveller the usual consequence is a cancelled boat or a delayed flight rather than danger. Philippine storm warnings are clear and the country handles them with a good deal more experience than most. Take insurance that covers weather disruption, keep a spare day before flying home, and never argue with a boatman who says the crossing is off.

Month by month in the Philippines

What each month actually feels like, and where to be in it.

January

One of the two best months for weather: dry, bright and as cool as the Philippines gets, with the amihan keeping the air fresh. It is also the great festival month — the Ati-Atihan in Kalibo, the Sinulog in Cebu and the Dinagyang in Iloilo all fall in January, filling hotels for miles around and worth planning either into or around. Expect wind on eastern coasts and some rough small-boat crossings. Rates ease after the first week when the Christmas season finally lets go.

February

Arguably the pick of the year for an unhurried traveller: dry, still comparatively cool, seas beginning to settle, and the peak-season crowds thinning after January. The Chocolate Hills of Bohol are turning their dry-season brown, Baguio holds its flower festival, the Panagbenga, and whale-shark season is under way in Donsol. There is very little wrong with February anywhere in the country.

March

The best all-round month. The wind has dropped, the sea is calm, the rains are still weeks away and every island is open. Diving visibility is superb and the island-hopping boats run reliably, which in an archipelago is the whole game. It is hot but not yet oppressive. This is the month we would choose, and the month to book earliest.

April

Hot, dry and reliable, with the clearest water of the year. The complication is Holy Week, which falls in late March or April: much of the country closes for several days, domestic transport fills, resorts charge their highest rates and some restaurants shut. It is an extraordinary thing to witness, particularly in the provinces, but a poor week to be moving between islands without a firm booking. Check the dates for your year before you plan anything.

May

The hottest month, and the one we would steer an older traveller away from. The lowlands are heavy and humid and the cities are hard work. Two consolations: the sea is still calm and the diving still excellent, and the mountain towns — Baguio, Sagada, the Cordillera — are pleasant while everywhere else swelters. The first thunderstorms usually break towards the end of the month.

June

The habagat arrives and the rains begin, generally as afternoon storms rather than all-day rain. Prices fall away sharply, the islands empty and everything is suddenly green. A good month for value if you accept some wet afternoons and keep to the western and southern islands early in the season. Whale-shark season at Donsol tails off.

July

Wet, warm and quiet. Manila and the western coasts see the heaviest of the habagat, and the capital floods in a serious downpour, which snarls traffic for hours. Palawan and Mindanao fare better. This is bargain territory for anyone who does not mind rearranging an afternoon, and the diving in Coron and around Cebu goes on regardless.

August

The wettest month across much of the country and the start of the busiest stretch of the storm season. It is also when Siargao comes into its own, as the surf season begins on the eastern seaboard and runs through to November. Davao holds the Kadayawan harvest festival. If you travel in August, favour the east and the far south, and leave room in the schedule.

September

Statistically the peak of the typhoon season, and the month we would most caution against for a tightly-linked island itinerary. Rates are at their lowest and the country is beautiful and empty, so it is not a month to write off — simply one to spend in fewer places, ideally Palawan or Siargao, with a spare day before the flight home. Surfing at Cloud 9 on Siargao is at its best.

October

Still firmly in the storm season, though the rains begin to ease towards the end of the month in the west. Bacolod holds the MassKara festival, one of the most joyful events in the Filipino calendar. Eastern Mindanao and Siargao continue to have the better weather. Prices remain low and the crowds have not yet returned.

November

The turning month. The habagat retreats, the amihan begins to establish itself and the western islands dry out, usually from the middle of the month onwards. Storms are still possible — November has produced some of the country’s most serious ones — but the second half of the month is often lovely and still inexpensive, which makes late November one of the best-value windows of the whole year.

December

Dry, cool and festive. The Philippines keeps the longest and warmest Christmas season in the world, and it is a genuinely charming time to be here — lanterns, carols, church at dawn. It is also the most expensive and most crowded fortnight of the year from around the twentieth onwards, with domestic flights full and rates at their peak. Come in the first three weeks and you get the weather without the crush.

The cheapest time to go

The wet months, with two firm exceptions.

The green season — June to early December

Room rates on the resort islands fall a long way once the rains arrive, and rooms that would need booking months ahead in March become available at a week’s notice. Domestic flights are cheaper and less full, restaurants have tables, and the island-hopping boats are not shoulder to shoulder. If your dates are flexible and your itinerary is not fragile, this is where the value is. Late November is the sweet spot: low-season prices with the dry season already arriving in the west.

The two fortnights to avoid on price

Christmas and New Year, roughly the twentieth of December to the first week of January, is the busiest and dearest period of the Filipino year, with millions of Filipinos travelling home and the resort islands charging peak rates. Holy Week, the week before Easter, is the same story compressed into a few days. Both are wonderful to experience and expensive to travel in. If you are watching the budget, come either side of them.

Heat, storms and crowds

The three things that shape a Philippine trip more than the rainfall figures do.

Heat — the one that matters most

The Philippine lowlands are warm and humid every day of the year, and the difference between the mildest month and the hottest is smaller than in most countries — but it is felt. December through February are the kindest; April and especially May are hard going, and the humidity makes the figure on the thermometer misleading. Plan sightseeing for the morning, keep the middle of the day for the water or a rest, drink far more than you think you need, and if the heat is a real concern, choose February and add a few days in the cool of Baguio or the Cordillera.

Storms — a matter of where, not only when

The exposed coasts are the eastern ones. If you are travelling between June and November, an itinerary built around Palawan, or around Siargao and eastern Mindanao, is far more robust than one strung along the eastern Visayas. Keep the whole journey on one airline where you can, allow at least four hours between a domestic arrival in Manila and an international departure, and consider spending the last night near the airport rather than flying in on the morning you fly home.

Crowds and festivals

The Filipino travel calendar is driven by the church and by school holidays. January’s festivals fill Cebu, Kalibo and Iloilo; Holy Week empties the cities and fills the beaches; the Christmas fortnight fills everything. School holidays run roughly from April to early June, which is when Filipino families take to the resorts. None of this is a reason to stay away — the festivals in particular are among the finest in Asia — but book well ahead if your dates fall across one.

Best time by island

The national average is no use here. Read the island you are actually going to.

Manila and Luzon

Best Dec–AprFloods Jul–Sep

December to April, and January or February for the most comfortable city days. Manila takes the habagat squarely and a heavy downpour between July and September can flood streets and stop traffic for hours. The rice terraces of the north are green and photogenic from about June, but the mountain roads are least reliable then; Baguio and the Cordillera are cool and pleasant all year and are the standard escape from the April and May heat.

Palawan — El Nido, Coron and Puerto Princesa

Best Dec–AprSteadiest in storm season

The classic window is December to April, with March and April the finest for the island-hopping boats and the clearest water for the Coron wrecks. Palawan’s real advantage, though, is in the wet months: sitting west and south of the main typhoon track, it is the most dependable choice in the country between June and November. The caveats there are long road transfers on rough surfaces and small airports that close in weather, so leave margin either side.

Boracay and the western Visayas

Best Nov–AprBeach swaps sides

November to April, and this is the one island where the wind decides where you swim. During the amihan, from about December to March, White Beach is calm and Bulabog on the eastern side is choppy and given over to the kitesurfers; when the habagat takes over in the summer months the two exchange roles and White Beach can get the weather. January and February are busy and beautiful; May to October is quiet, green and much cheaper, with rain most afternoons.

Cebu and Bohol

Best Jan–MayDiving year-round

January to May, with the fast ferries between the two islands most reliable in the calm months from March. The Chocolate Hills earn their name in the dry season, turning brown from about February to May and green again in the rains. The diving off Malapascua, Moalboal and Balicasag runs all year and is not much affected by the season. January is festival month in Cebu City and the Sinulog fills every room in the province.

Siargao and eastern Mindanao

Best Aug–Nov for surfMar–Jun for calm

This is the island group that runs against the national calendar. The surf season at Cloud 9 and along the Siargao coast is August to November, precisely when the rest of the country is wettest, and those months are also when Siargao is at its liveliest. If you would rather swim and snorkel than surf, come between March and June, when the swell drops and the lagoons are glassy. Davao is comfortable most of the year and is one of the least storm-prone cities in the country.

Best time to visit the Philippines — questions

What is the single best month to visit the Philippines?

March, with February and April close behind. By March the amihan winds have eased, the sea has flattened, the rains have not yet arrived and the whole country is open, which matters in an archipelago where a rough crossing can cancel the day you had planned. February is nearly as good and a touch cooler; April is hotter but still reliable and brings the clearest water of the year for diving. If you want one month to aim at and can travel when you like, aim at March, and book the flights early because everybody else has worked this out too.

Is it foolish to visit the Philippines during the rainy season?

No, provided you choose where you go and leave slack in the plan. The habagat rains from June to November usually come as heavy afternoon downpours rather than all-day grey, the islands are green and quiet, and hotel rates fall considerably. The sensible adjustments are these: favour Palawan or the far south, which sit largely outside the main typhoon track, over the eastern seaboard of Luzon and the Visayas; do not build an itinerary of tight small-island hops; keep a spare day in hand before your international flight home; and take travel insurance that covers weather disruption. Travellers who do this generally have an excellent, inexpensive trip.

When is the Philippines cheapest?

June through early December, excluding the last two weeks of the year. That is the wet season, and rates on the resort islands can fall well below their dry-season levels, with rooms available at short notice that would need booking months ahead in March. The two periods to avoid if money matters are the Christmas and New Year fortnight, which is the busiest and dearest of the Filipino year, and Holy Week before Easter, when the whole country travels at once. January to April are the high season generally, with rates highest around Christmas, New Year and Easter.

Which months are the typhoon season, and how bad is it really?

Tropical storms can arrive at any point from June to November and the season is at its most active from August to October. The Philippines lies in the busiest cyclone basin on earth, and a good number of storms enter its watch area every year, but only a fraction make a damaging landfall and those overwhelmingly strike the eastern coasts of Luzon, Samar and Leyte rather than the western islands. The practical effect on a holiday is usually a cancelled boat, a delayed flight or a day indoors rather than anything worse. Follow the local warnings, which are clear and taken seriously, and never argue with a boatman who says the crossing is off.

When should an older traveller go?

February and March, and if the heat is a concern, February above all. The temperature in the Philippine lowlands is high all year, but December through February are the mildest months, with the amihan bringing cooler, drier air, and March adds the calmest seas without yet reaching the punishing heat of May. Avoid May, which is the hottest month, and avoid the September and October peak of the storm season, when cancelled boats and delayed flights make for tiring days. Anyone who prefers to stay cool can also head up to Baguio or the mountain towns, which are pleasantly cool the whole year round.
