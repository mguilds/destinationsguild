Cyprus, month by month

Best Time to Visit Cyprus

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Cyprus has the longest warm season in the Mediterranean and the shortest list of genuinely bad months, which makes “when should I go” a question about what you want rather than about what the weather will allow. The island is hot and completely dry from June to September, green and mild through a short wet winter, and spectacular in the two shoulder seasons either side. It also keeps two calendars at once: the coast and the Troodos mountains, an hour apart, are often twenty degrees and a season apart as well. Below is the year as the Guild sees it — month by month, coast and mountains separately, and the festivals and holidays that move the prices.

The short answer

If you want the sea, come in late September or October. This is the thing most first-time visitors get wrong: the Mediterranean here is at its warmest not in high summer but at the end of it, after the sea has had the whole season to heat up, and by late September the crowds and the peak prices have gone while the water is at its best of the year. Swimming is comfortable well into November on the south coast.

If you want to see Cyprus — the archaeology, the mountains, the wildflowers, the villages — come in April or May. The island is green, the hills are covered in flowers, the ruins can be walked at midday without suffering, and there is still snow on the tops of the Troodos in April some years. The sea is swimmable by the middle of May for most people and bracing before that.

July and August are hot, dry, busy and dearest, and inland Nicosia becomes genuinely punishing. They are the right choice only if you are tied to school holidays, in which case book a hotel with shade and water and plan the days around the middle of the afternoon rather than through it. December to February is the quiet, cheap, green half of the year: mild on the coast, wet in bursts, snowy on the peaks, with much of the south-east resort coast shut.

Cyprus month by month

January and February

CoolestWettestCheapest

The green months. Coastal days are mild rather than cold, typically in the mid-teens Celsius, with bright spells broken by short bursts of heavy rain — this is when most of the island’s annual rainfall arrives. The hills turn properly green, the almond blossom is out in February, and the Troodos tops usually carry snow, with the handful of short ski runs on Mount Olympus operating in a good year. Larnaca’s salt lake is full and the flamingos are on it. Prices are at their lowest and the towns belong to the people who live in them; against that, much of Ayia Napa and Protaras is closed.

March and early April

WildflowersWarmingVery good value

The best weeks of the year for walking, and the most underrated time to come. The rain is easing, the days are lengthening and warming into the high teens and low twenties, and the whole island is in flower — the Akamas and the Troodos foothills in particular are exceptional in March. The sea is still cold. Orthodox Easter often falls in this window and changes everything for a week; see the festivals section below.

Late April and May

The Guild’s pick for sightseeingWarm, not hotSea becoming swimmable

If you are coming to see the place rather than to lie on it, this is the time. Days settle into the mid to high twenties, the rain has essentially stopped, the countryside is still green, and Kourion, the Paphos mosaics and the Tombs of the Kings can be walked in the middle of the day without the misery of August. The sea warms through May and most people are swimming happily by the end of it. Prices are moderate and the resorts are open but not full.

June

Reliable heatLong daysBefore the peak

Summer proper, without the worst of it. Expect high twenties to low thirties on the coast, no rain to speak of, and the longest days of the year. The sea is comfortably warm. Prices climb through the month and the resorts fill as northern European schools break up, so the first half of June is appreciably better value than the second.

July and August

HottestBusiestDearest

The peak in every sense. Coastal highs sit in the low to mid thirties with sea breezes taking the edge off, while inland Nicosia regularly passes forty and the Mesaoria plain becomes an oven. There is effectively no rain. Beaches, flights and hotels are at their fullest and most expensive, and the Ayia Napa club season is at full volume. The Troodos is the escape hatch, several degrees cooler and an hour away. If you are coming now, choose a sea-facing base over an inland one and treat the middle of the afternoon as indoor time.

September

Warmest seaEmptying outStill hot

An excellent month and our favourite compromise. The heat eases a little from the August peak while the sea reaches its warmest of the whole year, and from the second week the families go home and prices start to fall. The light softens, the evenings become pleasant again, and everything is still open. If you can travel outside school holidays, the back half of September is the single best fortnight on the Cypriot calendar.

October

The Guild’s pick for a beach weekWarm seaQuiet

The sea stays warm all month, the days are in the mid to high twenties, the crowds have gone and the prices have come down. The first rain usually arrives somewhere in the second half of the month, in short bursts rather than settled grey, and the hills begin to green up again. Walking in the Troodos is at its best. Towards the end of the month the south-east resorts start closing for the winter, so check your hotel is trading if you book late in October.

November and December

MildGreen againMuch of the coast closed

The year turns. Days settle back into the high teens and low twenties, the rain returns properly, and the island goes green. Swimming is still perfectly possible on the south coast in November for the hardy. Ayia Napa and Protaras largely shut; Paphos, Limassol, Larnaca and Nicosia carry on as normal towns, and are cheap and pleasant. Snow begins on the Troodos tops in December in most years, and Christmas in the hill villages is a quiet, local affair.

Coast or mountains — they keep different calendars

The single most useful thing to understand about the Cypriot year is that the island has two of them. The Troodos massif rises to just under two thousand metres in the middle of the island, and the temperature falls roughly six or seven degrees for every thousand metres you climb. In practice that means the mountains run about a season behind the coast in spring and a season ahead in autumn, and the gap is at its most dramatic in winter, when you can leave a mild green coast and be in snow within an hour’s drive.

In winter

The coast is mild and wet, the mountains are cold and often white. Snow lies on the Troodos tops for some weeks most years between January and March, and a small ski operation runs on Mount Olympus when conditions allow — a genuine curiosity rather than a ski holiday, with a handful of short runs. The mountain roads are usually passable but can need chains or close briefly after heavy snow, so check conditions before driving up in January or February.

In spring

The coast is in flower in March while the hills above a thousand metres are still bare and cold. By late April and May the mountains come into their own: cool, green, full of wildflowers and perfect for walking the Caledonia and Artemis trails, at a time when the coast is already warming up.

In summer

This is when the mountains earn their keep. While the coast sits in the low thirties and Nicosia in the forties, the Troodos villages at a thousand metres and more are five to ten degrees cooler, shaded by pine and cedar, and a great many Cypriots decamp up there for August. If you are visiting in high summer, build in two or three days in the hills; it will be the part of the trip you remember.

In autumn

The mountains cool and empty first while the coast stays warm and the sea stays warmest of all. October is the ideal month to combine the two: mornings walking in the Troodos among the turning leaves, afternoons swimming on a coast that has gone quiet.

Festivals, holidays and when the island fills up

Orthodox Easter — the one to check

By a distance the biggest event in the Cypriot year, and the one that will catch you out, because it moves: it is calculated on a different calendar from the Western Easter and can fall anywhere from early April to early May, sometimes a week or more after the Western date. In Holy Week the villages fill with families coming home, church services run late into the night, and the Saturday midnight liturgy and the bonfires afterwards are extraordinary to witness. Hotels inland and in the hill villages book out, many businesses close for several days, and the roads on the Friday and the Monday are busy. Look up the date for your year before you book anything — to catch it or to avoid it.

Carnival, in Limassol

The ten days or so before the start of Orthodox Lent, which means it also moves with the Easter calendar and is usually in February or March. Limassol takes it more seriously than anywhere else on the island, with parties, children’s parades and a large procession through the city on the final Sunday. Rooms in central Limassol are scarce that weekend.

Kataklysmos, at the start of summer

The Festival of the Flood, fifty days after Orthodox Easter, is celebrated along the coast and most vigorously at Larnaca, where the seafront fills with stalls, music, boat races and a great deal of cheerful throwing of water. It is one of the few genuinely Cypriot festivals with no equivalent in Greece, and it is a good-humoured, family affair.

The wine and village festivals

The Limassol wine festival takes over the municipal gardens for about a week at the end of the summer, and through September and October the hill villages run their own harvest festivals — grapes at Omodos, and later the olives and the carobs. These are small, local and worth going out of your way for if you are in the Troodos in early autumn.

The school holidays, and what they do to prices

Cypriot prices are driven by the northern European school calendar rather than the local one. The expensive weeks are from roughly the start of July to the end of August, with a secondary bump at Easter and another over Christmas and New Year in the towns. The cheapest weeks of the year are January and February, and the best value for the weather is the first half of June and the second half of September.

What closes, and when

From about the end of October to the middle of March a large part of Ayia Napa and Protaras closes entirely — hotels, restaurants and bars shuttered for the winter. Paphos, Limassol, Larnaca and Nicosia are proper towns and carry on year-round, with only a minority of seasonal businesses closing. If you are booking the south-east coast outside the season, confirm with the hotel directly that it will actually be open.

What it costs, through the year

Cyprus is not a cheap Mediterranean destination in high summer and is very good value outside it. The swing between an August week in Ayia Napa and the same room in May or October is substantial, and the swing to January is larger still. Flights follow the same curve and are the bigger part of the difference for most visitors. Two local quirks are worth knowing: hotel rates in Nicosia and Limassol are driven by business travel and often fall at weekends, which is the reverse of the resorts; and car hire is the one thing that gets disproportionately expensive and scarce in peak season, particularly automatics, so book it early or come outside July and August.

Getting in: Cyprus is in the EU but outside Schengen

The Republic of Cyprus belongs to the European Union and uses the euro, but it is not in the Schengen area, and for a traveller that is useful on two counts: days spent in Cyprus do not count against the ninety days in any hundred and eighty that Schengen allows, and days spent in Schengen countries do not count against your Cyprus allowance. Cyprus is also outside the EU’s Entry/Exit System, so there is no biometric registration when you arrive. Citizens of the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, Australia and the European Union need no visa for a holiday of up to ninety days. British advice is a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for three months beyond departure; United States advice recommends six months of validity. Check your own nationality’s terms with your own foreign ministry.

One rule is worth more than all the rest: enter the Republic only through its own points of entry — the airports at Larnaca and Paphos and the southern seaports. Arriving through the north and then crossing south is treated as illegal entry and can mean a fine or a refusal. Crossing north for a day from the south is entirely legal and routine; take your passport, and remember that the time counts towards your ninety days in the Republic.

Best time to visit Cyprus — questions

What is the best month to visit Cyprus?

It depends on what you came for, and the two answers are months apart. For the sea, late September and October, when the water is at its warmest of the whole year and the summer crowds have gone. For sightseeing, walking and the archaeology, late April and May, when the island is green, the wildflowers are out and the ruins can be walked at midday. If you need one month that does both reasonably well, September is the usual answer.

Is Cyprus too hot in July and August?

On the coast it is hot but manageable, with highs in the low to mid thirties and a sea breeze; inland it genuinely is too hot, with Nicosia regularly passing forty degrees and almost no relief. If you are tied to the school holidays, stay on the coast rather than inland, choose a hotel with proper shade and water, do your walking and sightseeing before about ten in the morning, and take two or three days up in the Troodos, which is several degrees cooler.

When is the sea warmest?

From about late August through October, which surprises people who assume high summer. The Mediterranean here takes the whole season to warm up and then holds that heat into the autumn, so the water in October is warmer than it is in June. Swimming is comfortable for most people from around late May until well into November on the south coast.

Does it really snow in Cyprus?

Yes, on the tops of the Troodos, most years for some weeks between January and March. Mount Olympus reaches just under two thousand metres and carries enough snow in a decent winter for a handful of short ski runs, which is a genuine curiosity rather than a ski holiday. The coast an hour below stays mild and green throughout, and driving from one to the other on the same morning is one of the odder pleasures of a Cypriot winter.

Why does Orthodox Easter matter so much?

Because it is the biggest event of the Cypriot year and because it moves, which is what catches visitors out. It is calculated on a different calendar from the Western Easter and can land anywhere from early April to early May, sometimes a week or more apart from the date you are used to. In Holy Week village hotels fill with returning families, many businesses close for several days, and the midnight service on Easter Saturday is remarkable to see. Look up the date for your year before booking, whether you want to be there for it or well out of the way.
