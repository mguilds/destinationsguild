Where to Stay in Samaná

Where to Stay in Samaná — Best Hotels & Areas

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated August 2026

The Samaná peninsula is the Dominican Republic at its greenest and least built-up: a long finger of jungle-backed coast on the Atlantic side, with coconut plantations running down to some of the finest beaches in the Caribbean. There are no thirty-storey resort towers here. There is, from 15 January to 31 March, something rather better — Samaná Bay is the principal breeding and calving ground for the entire North Atlantic humpback whale population.

Three bases to choose between. Las Terrenas is the peninsula’s liveliest town and its most walkable, with a flat beachfront road, supermarkets, pharmacies, cash machines and several dozen restaurants — a French and Italian expatriate influence has left it eating unusually well. Las Galeras, at the far eastern tip, is one street down to a beach and a handful of places to eat: beautiful, quiet, and remote. Santa Bárbara de Samaná is the working town on the bay, and the place the whale boats leave from.

This is the coast for travellers who want the Dominican Republic rather than a resort compound — and, happily, its Atlantic-facing beaches are largely spared the sargassum that troubles the east.

Samaná, Practically

Getting there, walking around, and the excursions that are — and are not — suitable.

Where you can walk

Las Terrenas: bestLas Galeras: tiny

Las Terrenas is the best walkable base on the peninsula and one of the better ones in the country: a flat beachfront road, supermarkets, pharmacies, cash machines, dozens of restaurants and a beach promenade toward Punta Popy. The Pueblo de los Pescadores strip, destroyed by fire in 2021, has been rebuilt with wider walkways, lighting and drainage and is trading in phases, though as of early 2026 the formal reopening was still pending. Two real cautions: constant motorbike traffic, and poor street lighting after dark.

Santa Bárbara de Samaná is partly walkable — the malecón waterfront where the whale boats leave is flat and pleasant, but the town behind it climbs steeply. Las Galeras is walkable but tiny: one main street down to the beach, a handful of restaurants, and you need transport for everything else.

Playa Cosón, El Portillo and Cayo Levantado are resort settings with no walkable town at all; Cayo Levantado is reached only by boat.

Airports, roads and whether to drive

Fly El Catey (AZS)Not Punta Cana

El Catey / Samaná International (AZS) is the airport to aim for: about twenty-five to forty minutes to Las Terrenas, an hour to Santa Bárbara and an hour and a half to Las Galeras. A private transfer runs about sixty dollars for up to four people. Santo Domingo (SDQ) is roughly two and a half hours on the modern Northeast Highway. Punta Cana is four hours or more — do not plan a Samaná trip around it.

Driving: the highway is modern, but the final mountain descent into Las Terrenas has sharp curves and steep grades, the secondary roads are unlit at night and motorbikes are everywhere. Our default advice is a pre-booked private transfer in and out, then local taxis and tour pickups. Hire a car only if you are based at Cosón or Las Galeras, intend to roam, and are content with mountain driving in daylight. Take the full insurance, and note that the Playa Rincón access track needs four-wheel drive.

The whales, and the other great days out

15 Jan – 31 MarBook the 8am boat

Humpback whale watching is the reason many people come. The 2026 season runs 15 January to 31 March, strictly enforced. Boats leave the Santa Bárbara malecón for three- to four-hour trips with ninety minutes to two hours among the whales, a marine biologist aboard and firm rules on approach. Samaná Bay is part of a twenty-five-thousand-square-kilometre marine mammal sanctuary, the first in the Atlantic, and the principal breeding ground for the whole North Atlantic humpback population — the whales are here to mate and calve, not to feed, which makes them wonderfully surface-active. Sighting rates top ninety-five per cent at peak. Take seasickness medication an hour ahead and book the earliest departure for the calmest sea; boarding involves a step down into a small boat.

Los Haitises National Park is the gentlest great excursion here: boat tours from Samaná through mangrove channels to limestone caves holding Taíno pictographs. It is almost entirely waterborne, so it is easy on the legs — expect a step down from the boat and some slippery cave steps.

Playa Rincón is one of the most beautiful beaches in the Caribbean, reached either by a rough dirt track needing four-wheel drive or, far more pleasantly, by a fifteen- to twenty-minute fishermen’s boat from Las Galeras for twenty or thirty dollars return. Beach shacks grill fish and lobster; bring pesos in cash. Swim in the middle section — there are currents near the cliffs and the river mouth. And for a low-effort morning, Playa Bonita and Playa Cosón are long, flat, near-empty walking beaches minutes from Las Terrenas.

El Limón waterfall we would flag carefully. It is a forty- to sixty-minute trek each way on a muddy, sometimes steep jungle trail crossing two streams, and even if you take the horseback option the final stone staircase down to the pool must be walked. It is genuinely slippery after rain. Wonderful for the sure-footed; not for anyone with balance, knee or hip trouble.

Weather, and the seaweed question

Best: Feb–AprLargely sargassum-free

Samaná faces the Atlantic and is wetter and greener than the south-east coast, with rain spread through the year and a wetter spell from November to January. February to April is the best window — drier, sunny, and overlapping the whale season, which is a happy coincidence. July and August are hot and good for the beaches but sit inside hurricane season, which runs 1 June to 30 November and peaks from August to October.

Sargassum is largely not a problem here. The peninsula’s north-facing beaches — Las Terrenas, Playa Bonita, Cosón — sit sideways to the drifting belt and stayed clear even through the record years of 2025 and 2026. South-facing shores inside Samaná Bay can pick up patchy weed and some turbidity after heavy rain. The town beaches at Las Terrenas are reef-protected and calm; Playa Bonita and Cosón catch Atlantic swell in winter.

Frequently Asked Questions

The questions Guild readers ask most about staying in Samaná.

When can I see the whales in Samaná?

The 2026 season runs 15 January to 31 March and the dates are strictly enforced, with the best sightings from mid-February to early March. Trips leave from the Santa Bárbara malecón, last three to four hours with ninety minutes to two hours among the whales, and sighting rates exceed ninety-five per cent at peak. Samaná Bay is the principal breeding and calving ground for the entire North Atlantic humpback population. Book the earliest departure, when the sea is calmest, and take something for seasickness an hour beforehand.

Where should I base myself on the Samaná peninsula?

Las Terrenas for most visitors — it is the liveliest and most walkable town, with a flat beachfront road, shops, pharmacies, cash machines and dozens of restaurants, and it eats unusually well thanks to a long French and Italian influence. Las Galeras if you want quiet above everything: one street, a beautiful beach and very little else. Santa Bárbara de Samaná if the whales are the point of the trip, since the boats leave from its waterfront.

Which airport is best for Samaná?

El Catey / Samaná International (AZS) — about twenty-five to forty minutes to Las Terrenas, an hour to Santa Bárbara and an hour and a half to Las Galeras, with a private transfer around sixty dollars for up to four people. Santo Domingo (SDQ) is a reasonable alternative at roughly two and a half hours on the modern Northeast Highway. Do not route through Punta Cana: it is four hours or more away and turns arrival day into a marathon.

Is El Limón waterfall suitable for older visitors?

Often not, and we would rather say so. It is a forty- to sixty-minute trek each way on a muddy, sometimes steep jungle trail that crosses two shallow streams, and even if you take the horseback option — where a guide walks alongside — the final stone staircase down to the pool must be walked. It is genuinely slippery after rain. If footing or balance is a concern, the Los Haitises National Park boat trip is the gentler alternative and, we would argue, the more remarkable outing.

Is there seaweed on the Samaná beaches?

Largely not. The peninsula’s north-facing beaches — Las Terrenas, Playa Bonita and Playa Cosón — sit sideways to the drifting sargassum belt and stayed clear even through the record years of 2025 and 2026. South-facing shores inside Samaná Bay can pick up patchy weed and some turbidity after heavy rain. The town beaches at Las Terrenas are reef-protected and calm, while Playa Bonita and Cosón catch Atlantic swell in the winter months.

Do I need a car in Samaná?

Not if you base yourself in Las Terrenas, which is walkable, or if you are staying at a resort with transfers arranged. The highway from Santo Domingo is modern, but the final mountain descent into Las Terrenas has sharp curves and steep grades, the secondary roads are unlit at night and motorbikes are everywhere. Our advice is a pre-booked private transfer in and out, then local taxis and tour pickups. Hire a car only if you are based at Cosón or Las Galeras and are content with daylight mountain driving.
