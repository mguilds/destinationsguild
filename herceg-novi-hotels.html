Herceg Novi

Where to Stay in Herceg Novi

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Herceg Novi stands at the mouth of the Bay of Kotor, on a steep slope, and the slope is the first thing to understand about staying here. The town is built in terraces from the water up to the main road, joined by flights of steps rather than streets, and a room that is two hundred metres from the sea on a map can be a hundred steps above it. Against that it has the best climate in the bay, a seafront promenade that runs for kilometres with swimming platforms all along it, more greenery than anywhere else on the coast, and far fewer day visitors than Kotor. It is also the nearest Montenegrin town to Dubrovnik, which is how a good many people arrive.

The old town and the steps

Most characterSea viewsMany steps

The old quarter around the clock tower, the Kanli Kula fortress and the two squares is the attractive heart of the town, with cafes under the trees and the bay below. Staying here puts you in the middle of it with a view, and almost always means stairs — between the road and the door, and again between the door and the sea. If you are sure on your feet that is a fair trade for one of the loveliest outlooks on this coast. If you are not, read the description of the access carefully before booking, and ask specifically how many steps there are.

Šetalište Pet Danica: the seafront

Flat walkingSwim off the promenadeLively in summer

The promenade along the water runs for several kilometres from Igalo through the town and on towards Meljine, and it is flat the whole way — the one flat line in Herceg Novi. Rooms along it give you the sea on the doorstep, ladders and concrete platforms to swim from, and bars and restaurants spread along its length. It is the practical choice if steps are a problem, and the sociable one in summer. You are still usually a climb away from the old town above, so check how your address connects to the upper streets.

Igalo

Spa traditionFlatter groundQuieter and cheaper

West of the centre towards the Croatian border, Igalo is the spa end of town, long known for its mud and mineral treatments and still built around that trade. The ground is flatter here, the beds are cheaper, and the atmosphere is calmer and rather more old-fashioned than central Herceg Novi. It suits a longer, slower stay, particularly out of season, and it is the closest part of town to the border crossing if you are coming from or going to Dubrovnik.

Savina and Meljine

GreenestResidentialShort bus or walk east

East of the centre the slope is wooded and residential, with the Savina monastery in its gardens above the bay and the small settlement of Meljine beyond it. This is the quiet, leafy side of Herceg Novi, with small beaches and swimming spots below and a walk or short bus ride into town. It is a good choice for a week when you want somewhere to come back to rather than somewhere to go out from, and the monastery grounds are among the most peaceful places on the whole bay.

Across the water: Luštica, Rose and Žanjice

Boat beachesVery quietLimited in winter

The Luštica peninsula faces the town across the mouth of the bay and holds the clearest water and the emptiest beaches in the area — Rose, Žanjice, Mirista and the Blue Cave beyond them. In summer taxi boats run across from the Herceg Novi waterfront, which is much quicker than the long road round. Staying over there means a genuinely quiet holiday with very little around you, and a boat or a long drive for anything else; out of season the boats stop and most places close. Tivat, on the far side of the peninsula, is a separate base with its own marina and the nearest airport.

Getting around Herceg Novi

Steps and the slope

Herceg Novi is a town of staircases, and the main street, the promenade and the old town are on three different levels. Nothing is far in plan, but almost everything involves going up or down. Plan around that rather than against it: pick your level and move along it, and use the buses or a taxi for the climbs.

The promenade

The flat seafront walk is the town's best feature and its most useful route. You can walk from Igalo through the centre towards Meljine entirely at sea level, swimming where you feel like it. In summer it is busy in the evening and quiet first thing.

Buses, boats and the ferry

Local buses run along the main road through town and out to Igalo and Meljine. Coastal buses connect to Kotor, Budva and Podgorica. In summer taxi boats cross to the Luštica beaches. If you are driving on towards Kotor or Budva, the Kamenari–Lepetane ferry across the narrows cuts out the long loop round the inner bay and runs frequently.

The Croatian border and Dubrovnik

Dubrovnik and its airport are close, and many visitors arrive that way. The crossing is a land border between a non-Schengen country and a Schengen one, so expect passport checks in both directions and queues at peak times in summer. If you are driving a hire car, confirm with the rental company in advance that it may cross the border and that the insurance paperwork covers it.

When to come, and what it costs

Herceg Novi claims the mildest weather on the bay and the figures broadly bear it out: it is sunnier and a little drier than Kotor at the head of the bay, and the mimosa flowering in February is celebrated with a festival. Summer is hot but tempered by the sea at the mouth of the bay. The Guild's months here are May, June, September and early October, when the sea is warm, the promenade is pleasant and the town is not full. Winter is wet — this is a famously rainy corner of Europe — but the town stays open and working, which is not true of the resorts down the coast.

Getting in: Montenegro is outside the EU and outside Schengen

Montenegro uses the euro but is not in the European Union and not in the Schengen area. That has one very useful consequence for a traveller: days spent here do not count against the ninety days in any hundred and eighty that Schengen allows, and the reverse is also true. Citizens of the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, Australia and the European Union do not need a visa for a short holiday, with ninety days in any hundred and eighty the usual allowance — confirm your own nationality’s terms with your own foreign ministry rather than with any website. Your passport should have been issued within the last ten years and be valid for at least three months beyond your departure, and you should make sure it is stamped on the way in and out, because those stamps are the record of your stay.

Every stay must be reported to the police. Montenegro’s own government says that duty falls on the accommodation provider, and hotels do it automatically at check-in; British government advice tells visitors to make sure they are registered within twenty-four hours and warns of a fine if they are not. In a hotel it is handled for you. In a private flat, ask your host to confirm it is done and keep the slip. Crossing to or from Croatia is a Schengen border with full checks and, in high summer, queues — and if you are driving a hire car across it, confirm first that the rental company permits it.

Herceg Novi — questions

How many steps are we actually talking about?

Enough that it is the single most common complaint about accommodation here, and the single thing most worth checking before you book. The town is built on a slope in terraces, and a property advertised as central may be reached only by a long public staircase with no vehicle access at all. Ask the host directly how many steps stand between the nearest road and the door, and between the door and the sea. If the answer matters to you, the seafront at Pet Danica and the flatter ground at Igalo are the two places to look.

Is it a good base for the Bay of Kotor?

Yes, with one caveat. Kotor is about forty minutes by road if you use the ferry across the narrows, and Perast and Tivat are closer still, so the whole bay is comfortably within a day. The caveat is that Herceg Novi is at the outer end of the bay, so you are driving or taking the boat for most things; people who want to walk out of the door into the famous scenery usually prefer Kotor or Perast, and people who want to swim, sit and stay put prefer here.

Can I do Dubrovnik as a day trip?

Easily, and many people do — it is a short run up the coast. The border crossing is the variable: it is an external Schengen border, and in July and August the queues can be long in both directions, particularly in the middle of the day. Go early. If you are driving a hire car, check before you set off that it is permitted to leave Montenegro and that you have the insurance documents the border may ask for.

Where do people swim?

Off the promenade, mostly, from concrete platforms and ladders rather than sand — that is the normal arrangement all along this bay. There are small pebble beaches towards Meljine and at Igalo. For the clear water people rave about you want the Luštica side, reached by taxi boat in summer: Žanjice and Mirista are the usual targets, and the water there is a different thing altogether.

Does anything stay open in winter?

More than on the open coast. Herceg Novi is a working town rather than a pure resort, so the shops, cafes and a reasonable number of restaurants carry on through the winter, and the mimosa festival in February is a genuine local event rather than a tourist one. The beach bars, the boats across the bay and most of the seasonal apartments do close. Expect rain.
