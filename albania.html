Albania

The Albania Travel Guide

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Albania is a small country on the Adriatic and the Ionian with a great deal packed into it: four hundred-odd kilometres of coast, mountains over two thousand metres within sight of the beaches, Greek and Roman ruins nobody queues for, and two Ottoman hill towns kept whole enough to be a UNESCO World Heritage site together. For forty-five years it was the most closed country in Europe, sealed off under Enver Hoxha and sown with concrete bunkers; it opened in 1991 with almost nothing and has been rebuilding ever since. What that leaves a visitor today is a country that is unmistakably Mediterranean, noticeably cheaper than its neighbours, genuinely hospitable, and still a little rough at the edges in ways that are part of the appeal. The shape of it is simple: Tirana and the plain in the middle, beaches north and south of Durres, the Riviera running down the Ionian to the Greek border, the stone towns of Berat and Gjirokaster inland, and the Accursed Mountains in the far north.

Why visit Albania

A coast that is still catching up with itself

Albania has something over four hundred kilometres of coast, Adriatic in the north and Ionian in the south, and the southern half of it — the Riviera between Vlore and Saranda — is as good as anything across the water in Greece or Italy. It is busier every year and no longer a secret, but it is still cheaper than its neighbours and still has stretches with nothing on them.

Two Ottoman towns kept whole

Berat and Gjirokaster are a UNESCO World Heritage site together, and they are the reason to go inland. Both are towns of tall stone-and-timber houses stacked up a hillside under a castle, lived in rather than restored for show. Berat is the gentler of the two to walk; Gjirokaster is the more dramatic to look at.

Mountains that feel further from Europe than they are

The Accursed Mountains in the far north run to well over two thousand metres, with stone-roofed villages at Theth and Valbona linked by a day’s walk over a pass, and a ferry up Lake Koman through a gorge that people compare to a fjord. It is genuine mountain country and should be treated as such.

Butrint, and Roman and Greek ruins without the queues

Butrint sits on a wooded peninsula between a lagoon and the channel to Corfu, layered from Greek through Roman and Byzantine to Venetian, and has been on the UNESCO list since 1992. Apollonia, Durres’s amphitheatre and Antigonea are all worth a morning and none of them is crowded.

The bunkers, and a history worth reading first

Albania spent forty-five years under one of the most closed regimes in Europe and came out of it in 1991 with very little. The concrete bunkers in the fields, the two Bunk’Art museums in Tirana and the pyramid Enver Hoxha’s daughter designed as his mausoleum are all part of the same story, and the country is unusually willing to talk about it.

It is still inexpensive, and it is friendly

Albania is among the cheapest countries in Europe for a visitor, and hospitality here is close to a point of honour — the old code of besa, a promise kept, is not a tourist-board invention. English is widely spoken by anyone under forty, Italian by a great many people of all ages, and Greek in the south.

Albania at a glance

Capital

Tirana, inland on a plain between Mount Dajti and the sea, with something like a third of the country’s people in the city and its suburbs. Tirana International Airport Nene Tereza, the country’s main airport, is at Rinas about seventeen kilometres north-west of the centre, with a bus into town and taxis at the door.

Money

The Albanian lek, written ALL or L. Albania is not in the euro area and the lek is not pegged to the euro, although plenty of coastal hotels quote in euro as well. Cards work in Tirana and in the larger hotels and restaurants; cash is what works for minibuses, village guesthouses, beach bars and museum tickets.

Language

Albanian — shqip — which is a branch of the Indo-European family all on its own, with no close living relative. Italian is understood by a great many people, Greek in the south, and English by most Albanians under forty and by nearly everyone in tourism. Faleminderit for thank you is worth learning.

Borders

In NATO since 2009, an official candidate for European Union membership since June 2014, and negotiating since 2022 — but not in the European Union and not in the Schengen area, so arriving is a real border check rather than none. Neighbours are Montenegro, Kosovo, North Macedonia and Greece, with Italy forty-odd miles across the Strait of Otranto.

Getting about

Driving is on the right. There is effectively no useful passenger rail network for a visitor; the country moves by road, on intercity coaches and on the shared minibuses called furgons, which leave when they are full rather than to a timetable. A hire car is the usual answer, and the coast road and the mountain roads both deserve respect.

Shape of it

About twenty-nine thousand square kilometres — a little smaller than Maryland — and about two point three million people at the 2026 census, a number that has fallen sharply through emigration. Roughly three quarters of it is hill or mountain, rising to Mount Korab at 2,764 metres on the North Macedonian border. A parliamentary republic: Bajram Begaj has been president since 2022 and Edi Rama prime minister since 2013.

The regions

A small country with four quite different halves — the capital and its plain, the Adriatic beaches, the Ionian Riviera and the stone towns behind it, and the mountains and lakes of the north and east.

Tirana, Durres and the central plain

The middle of the country and where most visitors land. Tirana is the base; Durres is the main port and the nearest beach to the capital; Kruje sits up on the mountainside behind them with Skanderbeg’s castle on it. Everything here is inside an hour of everything else.

  • Mount Dajti — the ridge above Tirana, reached by cable car, and the quickest way to get out of the city for an afternoon
  • Apollonia — a Greek and then Roman city in the fields south of Durres, with a monastery in the middle of it and almost nobody there

Berat, Gjirokaster and the southern interior

The stone towns, and the part of the country that rewards slowing down. Both Berat and Gjirokaster are on the UNESCO list as the Historic Centres of Berat and Gjirokastra — Gjirokaster listed in 2005 and Berat added in 2008. The road between them runs through the Vjosa valley and takes about three hours.

  • Pogradec — the Albanian shore of Lake Ohrid, which joined the UNESCO site shared with North Macedonia in 2019; a quiet lake town with a long promenade

The Riviera and the Ionian coast

From Vlore the road climbs the Llogara pass and drops onto the Ionian, and for the next hundred kilometres it is beaches, headlands and villages down to Saranda and the Greek border. This is the busiest part of Albania in August and the loveliest in June and September.

  • The Blue Eye — Syri i Kalter, a spring that fills a very deep blue-green pool inland from Saranda, on the way to Gjirokaster

The northern mountains and the lakes

The Albanian Alps — the Accursed Mountains — and the big border lakes. This is the hardest part of the country to travel and the one that rewards the effort most. Give it its own days rather than a detour, and go between late June and September.

Things to do in Albania

Walk the Mangalem slope at Berat

The Ottoman quarter below Berat castle is a single steep face of white houses whose windows all look the same way, which is where the city of a thousand windows comes from. Walk up through it, go into the castle — which has families living inside the walls, and the Onufri icon museum in its church — then cross the old bridge to Gorica and look back at the whole thing.

Go to Butrint early, before the coaches

A wooded peninsula between a lagoon and the Vivari channel, with a Greek theatre, a Roman forum, a baptistery with a mosaic floor that is usually kept covered, and a Venetian fort on the top with the view. It is about half an hour from Saranda. Go first thing or late in the day and you may have the theatre to yourself.

Drive the Llogara pass onto the Riviera

The coast road climbs from Vlore through pine forest to around a thousand metres at the Llogara pass, and then the whole Ionian opens up below you with Corfu on the horizon. It is one of the great European coast drives. The descent is a long series of hairpins, so take it slowly and stop at the top.

Take the Lake Koman ferry

A passenger and car ferry runs for about three hours between Koman and Fierze through a reservoir in a gorge so narrow that the boat seems to be heading into a wall of rock. It is the standard way into the far north-east and it is worth doing for itself. Sailings are seasonal and the timetable changes, so check locally the day before rather than taking a figure off a website.

Spend a morning on the Hoxha years in Tirana

Bunk’Art 1, out near the Dajti cable car, is the government’s own nuclear bunker turned into a museum, and Bunk’Art 2 in the centre covers the interior ministry and the secret police. The House of Leaves nearby was the surveillance headquarters. Any one of the three explains more about modern Albania than a day of walking will.

Climb through Gjirokaster to the castle

Gjirokaster is built down a mountainside in slate and stone, and the castle sits along the top of it with a gun gallery, a captured American aircraft and a view down the Drino valley. The bazaar below is the place to eat, and the walk up is genuinely steep — which is why the town is called the city of a thousand steps.

When to go, and what it costs

Albania has a Mediterranean coast and a mountain interior, and the two keep different calendars. Late May, June and September are the best weeks of the year almost anywhere in the country: the sea is warm enough, the Riviera is not yet full or has just emptied, the stone towns are comfortable to walk and the mountain trails are open. July and August are hot, and the Riviera and Ksamil are genuinely crowded — this is when the Albanian diaspora comes home and the coast fills from both directions. October is lovely and quiet inland, with the sea still swimmable early in the month. November to March is the wet season on the coast, mild rather than cold, with many beach places shut; it is the cheapest time, it is a perfectly good time for Tirana, Berat and Gjirokaster, and it is snow and closed passes in the north. The dates to know are Independence and Flag Day on 28 November, Liberation Day on the 29th, and Summer Day on 14 March, which is an old Albanian spring festival and a public holiday.

Fishing here

Fishing in Albania means the Ionian and Adriatic from a small boat, the big border lakes — Ohrid at Pogradec, Skadar above Shkoder and the Prespa lakes — and the mountain rivers of the north and the Vjosa in the south for trout. Lake Ohrid has its own endemic trout and its own protective rules, the Vjosa is now a national park with restrictions that come with that, and licensing and seasons are handled locally and change, so this is a thing to arrange with a local guide on the ground rather than to work out in advance from abroad. Travelling in your later years

The stone towns are steep, and that is the whole point

Berat’s Mangalem and the whole of Gjirokaster are built up a slope on cobbles and slate, and both have the castle at the top. Neither is impossible and both are worth the effort, but choose your hotel by how far up it is rather than by its photographs, and in Gjirokaster ask whether a taxi can reach the door. Berat is distinctly the easier of the two to stay in.

Tirana and Saranda are the easy bases

Central Tirana is flat, and the boulevard, Skanderbeg Square and the Grand Park are level walking with taxis everywhere. Saranda’s promenade along the bay is flat too, although the town behind it climbs steeply, so a room on the front is worth paying for. Both are good places to sit still for several days and take day trips out.

Think hard about the driving

Albanian roads are much better than their reputation, but the coast road has long sequences of hairpins, mountain roads are narrow with drops, lane discipline in Tirana is its own subject, and a great many roads have no shoulder. A driver for a day or two costs little by European standards and is a reasonable way to see the Riviera or the Berat road without doing the work yourself.

Where the ground is difficult

Butrint is a long circuit on uneven stone and tree roots with a climb to the fort; the Llogara descent is a series of switchbacks that some passengers find uncomfortable; Ksamil’s beaches are reached down short unmade tracks; the Koman ferry involves boarding over a ramp; and the Theth and Valbona valleys are mountain walking rather than strolling. None of it rules the country out, but pick deliberately.

Getting in: what applies, and what does not

Albania is not in the European Union and not in the Schengen area, so the rules you may have read about for a European trip are not the rules for arriving here. Albania runs its own border and its own visa policy, and for most Western visitors it is one of the most relaxed in Europe.

United States citizens may enter as tourists without a visa and, unusually, may stay up to one year without applying for a residence permit; the State Department’s own country page says so plainly. Your passport needs to be valid for at least three months from the date you arrive and to have a blank page for the entry stamp. Longer than a year, or any work or study, needs a residence permit applied for after arrival at the border and migration police office for your area. Citizens of the European Union, the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia and the other Schengen Annex II countries are admitted without a visa for shorter periods, usually ninety days; check your own nationality’s allowance before you travel rather than assuming it matches the American one.

The United States currently places Albania at Level 2, exercise increased caution, in an advisory issued on 26 August 2026, on account of crime. What that means in practice for a visitor is ordinary care: violent crime here is overwhelmingly a matter between organised groups rather than something aimed at tourists, police response can be slow in remote areas, and demonstrations in Tirana can start at short notice and block traffic. Read the current advisory at travel.state.gov before you go, because the level and the wording change.

If your trip also takes in Greece, Italy or anywhere else inside the Schengen area — and a great many Albanian trips arrive via Corfu, Bari or Athens — then you will cross a Schengen border on the way, and these rules do apply to that crossing:

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Getting here: almost everyone flies into Tirana, which has the great majority of the country’s air links, with smaller airports at Kukes and at Vlore. Ferries run from Italy to Durres and from Corfu to Saranda, the Corfu crossing being much the most useful to a visitor doing the south. Coaches connect Tirana with Podgorica, Pristina, Skopje, Ohrid and Athens. Sailings, flights and coach timetables all change by season, so confirm them with the operator for your travel dates rather than trusting a figure on any website, including this one — which is why we do not print one.

Albania — questions

How many days do I need in Albania?

Five days is the sensible minimum and gets you Tirana, one stone town and a few days on the Riviera. A week lets you do Tirana, Berat, Gjirokaster and Saranda properly with Butrint and the Blue Eye in between, which is the classic first trip. Ten days or a fortnight adds either the far north — Shkoder, the Koman ferry, Theth and Valbona — or a slow stretch on the coast. The country is small but the roads are mountain roads, so journeys take longer than the map suggests.

Is Albania expensive?

It is among the cheapest countries in Europe for a visitor, and noticeably cheaper than Greece or Italy across the water. The exception is the Riviera and Ksamil in late July and August, when demand is far ahead of the number of beds and prices rise accordingly. We do not print figures because they move, but the pattern holds: the southern coast in high summer is a real premium, Tirana is modest, and the interior is very inexpensive.

Is Albania safe?

For an ordinary visitor, yes, and visitors are often surprised by how safe it feels. The United States places it at Level 2, exercise increased caution, on account of crime, and that advisory is about organised crime and about how quickly police can reach a remote area rather than about risk to tourists. The practical cautions are the usual ones: watch your bag in a crowd, use a metered or app taxi, avoid demonstrations, and drive defensively. Check the current advisory before you travel.

What language should I speak, and will English do?

Albanian is the only official language and it is a branch of Indo-European all on its own, so nothing you know will help you guess it. English is widely spoken by Albanians under about forty and by almost everyone working in tourism. Italian is understood by a great many people of all ages, a legacy of decades of Italian television, and Greek is common in the south. Faleminderit for thank you and përshëndetje for hello are worth the effort.

Do I need a car?

Not for Tirana, and not if your trip is Tirana plus Saranda by coach. For anything else a car makes a real difference, because there is no useful passenger railway and the interesting places are spread along roads rather than rails. The alternatives are intercity coaches, which are cheap and reasonably frequent between the main towns, and furgon minibuses, which are cheaper still and leave when they fill rather than to a timetable. Driving is on the right. If the hairpins or the Tirana traffic do not appeal, hiring a driver for a day or two is inexpensive here.

Does Albania use the euro?

No. The currency is the Albanian lek, and Albania is not in the euro area and not in the European Union, though it has been a candidate for membership since 2014 and has been negotiating since 2022. Many hotels and tour operators on the coast will quote and take euro, but you will usually do better paying in lek, and you will need lek for minibuses, small guesthouses, beach bars and museum tickets. Cards are fine in Tirana and in larger hotels.

When is the best time to go?

Late May to June and the whole of September, almost without qualification: the sea is warm, the Riviera is not overwhelmed, the stone towns are pleasant to climb and the northern trails are open. July and August are hot and busy on the coast. October is quiet and still fine inland. Winter is mild and wet on the coast with much of the beach trade shut, perfectly good for the cities, and snowy with closed passes in the mountains.
