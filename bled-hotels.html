Bled

Where to Stay in Bled

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Bled is a small town wrapped around a small lake, and almost everything written about it is about the one view: a wooded island with a church on it, a castle on a cliff above, and the Karavanke and the Julian Alps behind. The town itself takes half a morning. What the choice of where to sleep really decides here is how close you are to the water, how much of the summer crowd you hear, and how steep the walk home is. The promenade on the north-east shore has the views, the grand hotels and the bustle; the slopes above it are quieter and cheaper with a climb attached; Mlino on the south shore is the calm, flat, family side. And because the two best decisions many visitors make are to sleep at Lake Bohinj instead, or to go up to Kranjska Gora for the mountains, both are set out here as well.

The lakeside and the promenade

Best viewsWalk to everythingDearest rooms

The north-east shore, where the road along the water meets the little centre, is where the grand old hotels sit and where you pay for the view. The advantage is real: you step out of the door onto the six-kilometre path that rings the lake, the boat jetties and the rowing club are in front of you, and the island with its church is framed in the window. You are also in the middle of the only busy part of Bled, which on a July afternoon means coach parties, ice-cream queues and traffic on the shore road. Ask which side of the building your room is on and whether the view is the one in the photograph, because a lake-facing room and a car-park-facing room in the same hotel can be priced quite differently.

Above the lake, towards the castle and Grajsko brezje

QuieterThe famous viewA climb home

The slopes rising behind the north shore hold guesthouses, apartments and small hotels on streets that are residential rather than resort. Rooms cost less than on the water, the view from a few hundred metres up is the one that appears on the postcards, and the walk down to the lake takes five or ten minutes. The catch is the same walk in reverse at the end of the evening, uphill, and some of these lanes are steep enough to matter if you are carrying luggage or are unsteady on your feet. Worth asking the owner directly how steep the approach is before booking, and whether there is parking at the door.

Mlino and the southern shore

CalmestFamily-friendlyShort walk in

Round the western end and along the south shore, Mlino is the old village from which the pletna boats have crossed to the island for generations. It is the quiet side of Bled: a flatter, shadier stretch of the ring path, the better swimming spots, a few small hotels and a lot of apartments and rooms in houses. You are fifteen to twenty minutes’ level walk from the centre, which is the whole point. This is where we would send a family, anyone staying a week, or a visitor who finds the main shore too busy in season, and it is noticeably cheaper than the same standard of room on the promenade.

Lake Bohinj, half an hour on

WilderInside the national parkFewer beds

Bohinj is the other lake, bigger than Bled, deeper, colder, almost entirely undeveloped and wholly inside Triglav National Park with the mountains standing straight out of the water at the far end. It is about half an hour west of Bled by road or bus. If what you came to Slovenia for is walking, swimming in clean cold water and quiet, stay here instead and visit Bled for an afternoon rather than the other way round. The trade-offs are honest ones: far fewer rooms, so book early; limited eating out, especially off season; and you will want a car or patience with the bus timetable, because the villages around the lake are spread out.

Kranjska Gora, for snow and for the passes

Ski seasonMountain walkingFamily resorts

Forty minutes north-west, up against the Austrian and Italian borders, Kranjska Gora is Slovenia’s best-known ski resort and the foot of the Vršič pass road. In winter it is a proper if modest ski village with gentle runs that suit learners and children; in summer it is a walking and cycling base, with the old railway line to Italy turned into an easy bike path and the Planica ski jumps up the valley. Staying here instead of Bled makes sense for a snow holiday or a mountain week, not for a short sightseeing trip — it is an hour and a quarter from Ljubljana and the lake sights are a drive away.

Getting around

Bled is small enough to need nothing but your feet: the path all the way round the lake is about six kilometres, flat, mostly car-free and walkable in an easy ninety minutes, and the centre is a few streets. For the island, the traditional way across is a pletna, the flat-bottomed boat rowed standing up from Mlino and from the jetties on the main shore, and there are also rowing boats to hire if you would rather do the work yourself; either way you will have to climb the stone stairs from the landing to the church. The castle is a steep twenty minutes up on foot or a short drive. Buses connect Bled with Ljubljana, with Bohinj and with Radovljica and Lesce, and they are the practical way to arrive; the railway station called Lesce-Bled is about four kilometres out of town, so a train arrival needs a bus or a taxi at the end of it. A car is genuinely useful if you mean to get into the mountains, the Vintgar gorge or the Vršič pass, but parking around the lake is paid and fills early in summer, and the pass road is closed by snow in winter. Check whether your accommodation includes a parking space before you drive.

When to come, and what it costs

Bled has a short, crowded summer and a long, quiet shoulder, and the difference in both price and pleasure is large. July and August are hot, green and busy: the lake is warm enough to swim in, every boat is working, and the shore path and the island are at their most crowded in the middle of the day. June and September are our pick — warm, far quieter, everything open, and noticeably cheaper. May and early October are lovely and cool, with the hills at their best and some of the high trails still or already out of condition. Winter is a real season here but a different holiday: the lake may freeze at the edges, the surrounding resorts are skiing, and Bled itself is subdued, cheap and often beautiful in snow — with the caveat that some lakeside businesses close and the mountain passes shut. Whenever you come, the first and last hours of the day are when the lake is worth the journey, which is the best argument for sleeping here rather than visiting from Ljubljana.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Slovenia is in the European Union, the Schengen area and the euro, so an arrival from another Schengen country has no passport control and an arrival from outside it is a single check at the airport. For Bled this matters mostly because of where people fly into: Ljubljana’s airport at Brnik is the nearest, but a good many visitors arrive through Venice, Trieste, Klagenfurt or Zagreb and drive or take a shuttle, and all four are inside Schengen. If you plan to cross into Austria over the Wurzen or Loibl passes, or into Italy at Rateche, these are internal Schengen crossings and usually unmanned; carry your passport anyway. One practical rather than legal point: Slovenian motorways require a vignette, now bought electronically, and a hire car may or may not already have one, so check with the desk rather than assume.

Staying in Bled — questions

Is Bled worth staying overnight, or is a day trip enough?

A day trip from Ljubljana covers the sights — the lake walk, the island, the castle — and a great many people do exactly that. What a day trip cannot give you is the lake early in the morning or in the last of the light, which is when the water goes still, the coaches have gone and the place looks like the reason you came. If Bled is the picture that brought you to Slovenia, give it a night. If it is one stop among many, a day is honestly enough.

Bled or Bohinj?

Bled for the famous view, the island, the castle and the comfort of a developed little resort with plenty of rooms and restaurants. Bohinj for the mountains, the swimming, the walking and the quiet — it is larger, almost undeveloped, inside the national park, and about half an hour away. They are not really rivals: the honest answer for a stay of three nights or more is to sleep at Bohinj and spend an afternoon at Bled, because Bled handles visitors much better than Bohinj handles crowds.

How do you get to the island, and is there much on it?

By pletna, the traditional flat-bottomed boat rowed standing up, from Mlino or from the jetties on the main shore; by hired rowing boat; or by swimming, which people do in summer from the nearer shore. On the island there is the church of the Assumption with its bell tower, a short flight of ninety-odd stone steps up from the landing, a small cafe and a view back at the castle. It is a half-hour visit rather than an afternoon. The steps are the part to think about if stairs are difficult; there is no way up without them.

What about the cream cake?

The kremna rezina — a slab of vanilla custard and cream between two sheets of pastry, usually called kremsnita — is genuinely a Bled thing rather than a tourist invention, and several cafes on the shore and in the centre have been making it for decades. They all claim theirs is the original and the argument will not be settled here. Have one with coffee mid-afternoon, on a terrace facing the water, and do not plan on dinner straight afterwards.

Can I walk in the mountains from Bled without being a climber?

Yes, and this is what the area is really for. There are gentle valley and gorge walks within reach — the Vintgar gorge on its boardwalks is the best known and the most popular — graded hill paths up Ojstrica and Osojnica above the lake for the famous view down, and a great deal of marked waymarked walking in Triglav National Park at every level. Triglav itself is a serious two-day mountain route with exposed sections and fixed cables, not a hike; it needs a guide unless you are experienced, and conditions change fast. Check the forecast and the hut situation before you set out, and remember that high paths hold snow well into the summer.
