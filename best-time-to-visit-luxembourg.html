Luxembourg through the year

Best Time to Visit Luxembourg

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Luxembourg has no dramatic season. It is a temperate, green, rainy country with a mild winter, a warm rather than hot summer and a long shoulder at either end, which means there is no month you cannot travel in and no month that decides the trip for you. What changes through the year is what the country is for: beech woods and sandstone ravines from late spring to early autumn, the wine harvest on the Moselle in September, a lit medieval city and its Christmas markets in December, and a wet grey gap in between that is cheap and almost empty. Here is the year as it actually behaves.

The short answer

September if you can choose freely: the summer visitors have gone, the weather often holds, the Moselle is picking its grapes and the walking is at its best. May if you would rather have new leaf and long evenings than settled weather. December if you want the Christmas markets and a lit city on a rock, and do not mind short days. July and August are the warmest and the busiest, and perfectly good if you start early. November is the one month we would talk you out of.

Luxembourg month by month

January and February

ColdestQuietestCheapest

The low point of the year and the cheapest time to be here. Luxembourg’s winter is wet and grey more often than it is crisp and white, with temperatures hovering around freezing in the capital and rather colder up in the Ardennes, where snow does lie for spells and the higher roads occasionally need care. Daylight is short. The compensations are an old town with nobody in it, hotel rates at their lowest of the year including midweek, and the particular pleasure of the casemates and the museums when you have them to yourself. Buergbrennen, the bonfire night that marks the end of winter, falls on the Sunday after Carnival and is worth seeking out in a village.

March and April

ShoulderWoods waking upChangeable

Spring comes slowly and unreliably. March can give you three seasons in a day and April is the month when the beech woods of the Mullerthal and the Ardennes come into leaf, which is the single best thing about this time of year — the light through new beech is worth a walk on its own. Waterfalls run high after the winter rain. Rates are still off-season and the coach parties have not started. Take proper waterproofs and expect the sandstone paths to be slick.

May

Guild’s pickWalking weatherLong evenings

May is one of the two months the Guild would choose. The woods are fully out but not yet heavy, the evenings stretch past nine, the trails are open and dry more often than not, and the castles are staffed without being crowded. The Echternach dancing procession, a hopping procession through the streets that UNESCO lists as intangible cultural heritage, falls on Whit Tuesday, which moves with Easter and can land in May or early June; if your dates are near it, go, and book Echternach early.

June

Longest daysNational DayFestival season

The longest light of the year and the start of the festival run. National Day on 23 June is the date to plan around: the capital celebrates on the evening of the 22nd with fireworks over the Adolphe bridge and a crowd in the streets, then takes the day itself as a public holiday, when a good deal of the country is shut. The rest of the month is excellent walking weather, warm but rarely hot, with open-air concerts and village festivals most weekends.

July and August

WarmestBusiestSchueberfouer

High summer is warm rather than hot in an ordinary year, though the heat waves that have reached this part of Europe in recent summers can push it well up for a week at a time. It is the busy season: the castles at Vianden and Beaufort fill with coaches by late morning, and the Mullerthal car parks are full by ten on a fine weekend. Go early in the day and you will still have the ravines mostly to yourself. From late August the Schueberfouer, the big funfair on the Glacis in the capital, runs into early September and takes a great many of the city’s hotel rooms with it — check before you book.

September

Guild’s pickWine harvestWarm water in the air

The other month we would choose, and for many people the best of all. The summer crowds go home in the first week, the weather often holds warm and settled well into the month, and the Moselle slopes come alive with the grape harvest and the wine festivals that go with it along the river from Grevenmacher down to Schengen. The walking is at its best: dry paths, good light, and woods beginning to turn at the end of the month.

October

Autumn colourQuieterWetter

The Ardennes turn properly in October and the Mullerthal beech woods go copper, which is the second great reason to walk here. Rain becomes more frequent and the days shorten noticeably, but the countryside is at its most photogenic and the towns have emptied out. Some castle and museum opening patterns thin after the autumn school break, and the chairlift and smaller attractions in the Ardennes start closing for the season, so check anything your day depends on.

November

GreyestLowest ratesMarkets begin

The month we would steer a first-time visitor away from, honestly. It is wet, grey and dark, the walking country is at its least rewarding, and a lot of the seasonal attractions in the north are shut. What it does have is the lowest prices of the year and a capital that belongs entirely to the people who live in it. Towards the end of the month the Christmas markets begin to go up across the city squares, and the mood changes within a week.

December

Christmas marketsShort daysBusy weekends

The capital puts on Christmas markets across several of its squares, with an ice rink and lights through the old town, and the combination of a lit medieval city on a rock and a market in the square below is genuinely good. Weekends fill with visitors from across the three borders, so a weekday visit is calmer and cheaper. Daylight is short and the weather is a lottery. The markets wind down after Christmas and the first days of January are the quietest of the whole year.

The weather, and what it does to a trip

Rain is the constant

There is no dry season here. Rain is possible in every month and common in autumn and winter, usually as a grey drizzle rather than a downpour. It rarely ruins a day, but it does decide your footwear: the Mullerthal sandstone and the old town’s cobbles and steps are slippery wet, and a good sole matters more than a good coat.

The Ardennes are a different country

The north sits higher, and it is colder, wetter and snowier than the capital in winter and a few degrees cooler in summer. A forecast for Luxembourg City is not a forecast for Clervaux or the Our valley. In a hard winter the higher roads need winter tyres and some of the small seasonal attractions simply shut.

Summer heat has become a real thing

The classic description of this climate is mild summers in the low twenties Celsius. That still describes most of the season, but the heat waves that have reached northern Europe in recent years arrive here too, and older hotel stock in the valley quarters is not always air-conditioned. If you are heat-sensitive, ask the hotel directly rather than assuming.

The light, not the cold, is what shortens winter

Luxembourg sits far enough north that December afternoons go dark early, and it is the short grey days rather than the temperature that make winter feel long. Plan outdoor things for the middle of the day and let the evenings be museums, markets and dinner, which is how the country itself does it.

Dates worth planning around

Some of these are fixed and some move with Easter. Check the ones that matter for your own travel year before you book.

National Day — 23 June

A public holiday, with the real celebration on the evening of the 22nd: a torchlight procession, the changing of the guard at the palace and fireworks over the Alzette valley. Much of the country closes on the day itself. The two nights around it are the hardest of the year to find a room in the capital.

The Echternach dancing procession — Whit Tuesday

A hopping procession through the town to the basilica of Saint Willibrord, recognised by UNESCO as intangible cultural heritage, with thousands taking part. The date moves with Easter and falls in May or early June. Echternach is a small town and it fills completely.

The Schueberfouer — late August into early September

The national funfair on the Glacis in Luxembourg City, with roots in the fourteenth century. It is a fortnight of the whole country coming into town, and it takes most of the capital’s hotel rooms with it.

The Moselle wine festivals — September into October

Harvest time along the river, with festivals in Grevenmacher, Remich and the smaller villages on successive weekends, and cellars open to visitors. The best reason to be on the Moselle rather than in the capital.

Buergbrennen — the Sunday after Carnival

Villages across the country build and burn a bonfire to see off the winter. It moves with Easter like Carnival itself, it is a local rather than a tourist event, and being invited to stand at one with a hot drink is a better evening than anything on a February itinerary.

Éimaischen — Easter Monday

A market in the Fish Market quarter of the old town and in the village of Nospelt, where the traditional purchase is a Péckvillchen, a small clay bird-shaped whistle. Cheerfully odd, entirely local, and a good reason to be in town over the Easter weekend.

The Christmas markets — late November to the turn of the year

Markets across several squares in the capital, with lights through the old town and an ice rink. Weekends are busy with visitors from across the three borders; a midweek evening is far better. Dates shift a little each year, so check before fixing travel around them.

What it costs, and when

Luxembourg City is a business and institutions town, and that rather than the tourist season sets its hotel prices. The result is unusual and worth knowing: midweek nights in the capital are often dearer than weekend ones, because the people filling the rooms from Monday to Thursday are working. A Friday-to-Sunday visit can therefore be markedly cheaper than a Tuesday-to-Thursday one in the same month. Layer the ordinary seasons on top of that — cheapest from January to March and in November, dearest around National Day, the Schueberfouer and the December market weekends — and you have the shape of the year. Outside the capital, Echternach and Vianden follow the walking and castle season rather than the business week. And whatever else you spend, you will not be spending anything on transport.

Fishing here

The Ardennes rivers and the Upper Sure lake fish best from the spring opening through the summer, and the Moselle slows and warms later in the year. A permit is required and the rules differ between the state licence and the local lease on a given stretch, so arrange it locally rather than from abroad. Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Luxembourg is in the Schengen area from its beginning — the agreement was signed on its own Moselle bank in 1985 — so the land borders with Belgium, Germany and France carry no checks at all, and an arrival from outside Schengen is a single check at Findel airport. Nothing about the season changes any of this.

Luxembourg’s seasons — questions

What is the single best month to visit Luxembourg?

September, narrowly, with May a close second. September usually holds warm and settled after the summer crowds have gone, the Moselle harvest and its wine festivals are on, and the walking in the Mullerthal and the Ardennes is at its best. May gives you the same quiet paths with the woods newly in leaf and longer evenings, but less reliable weather. Either beats July, when the castles are busy and the car parks fill early.

How bad is the weather, really?

Mild and wet rather than harsh. This is a temperate, maritime-influenced climate with no dry season and no reliably hot or reliably frozen one: rain is possible in any month and frequent in autumn and winter, snow is an Ardennes matter more than a capital one, and extremes are rare though the recent summer heat waves have reached here. The practical answer is that no month is a write-off and no month is a guarantee, so pack a waterproof whatever the forecast says.

When is National Day and what actually happens?

National Day is 23 June, the Grand Duke’s official birthday, and it is a public holiday. The larger celebration is the evening before: a torchlight procession, a changing of the guard and fireworks over the Alzette valley from the Adolphe bridge, with the old town full. On the day itself there is a military parade and a Te Deum in the cathedral, and much of the country is closed. Hotel rooms in the capital go early for those two nights.

What is the Schueberfouer, and does it affect my trip?

It is the country’s great annual funfair, held on the Glacis ground just north of the old town from late August into early September, and it goes back to the fourteenth century. It is a proper city-wide event rather than a tourist attraction, with a particular food tradition attached to it. The practical point for a visitor is that it fills Luxembourg City’s hotels for a fortnight, so if your dates fall in that window, book well ahead or stay out of town and come in on the free train.

When is the Echternach procession?

On Whit Tuesday, which is the Tuesday after Pentecost and therefore moves with Easter — it falls in May or early June depending on the year. The hopping procession through Echternach to the basilica of Saint Willibrord is on the UNESCO list of intangible cultural heritage and draws thousands of participants and spectators. Check the date for your own travel year, and if you intend to be there, book Echternach accommodation months rather than weeks ahead.

Is anything closed out of season?

Some things, and it is worth checking the ones your day depends on. The castles and museums in the capital run year-round; the Bock casemates and several smaller Ardennes attractions, including the Vianden chairlift, work to a season and close or shorten in the colder months. Restaurants in the small Mullerthal and Moselle towns often take a winter break. Nothing closes the country down, but a January trip is a town-and-museum trip rather than a castle-and-trail one.
