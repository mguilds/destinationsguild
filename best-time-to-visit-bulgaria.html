Bulgaria, month by month

Best Time to Visit Bulgaria

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Bulgaria runs three calendars at once and they barely overlap. The inland plain is hot enough in July and August to reorganise your day around the shade; the Rila and the Pirin hold snow above two thousand metres into June and then give two of the best walking months in Europe; and the Black Sea has a genuinely short season, roughly mid-June to the end of September, outside which a good deal of the coast is boarded up. Add a ski season from mid-December to late March at a third of Alpine prices, and a rose harvest that happens at first light in the last fortnight of May. What follows is the month-by-month picture, the dates worth planning around and where the money goes.

The short answer

May, June and September are the best months in Bulgaria, and September is the one that does everything at once — warm sea, stable mountain weather, empty old towns. July and August are for the coast and the summer mountains, hot and busy, and the two weeks to book ahead for. Mid-January to early March is the ski season at its most reliable, and the cheapest serious skiing in Europe. November is the one month we would not choose: no snow, no walking and a lot of grey. If the roses are the point, come in the last week of May.

Bulgaria month by month

January and February

The ski season at its most reliable. Bansko, Borovets and Pamporovo all run, snow cover is usually good from mid-January, and the February half-term weeks are the busiest and dearest of the winter — the Bansko gondola queue at nine in the morning in half-term is the one thing people complain about. Inland it is cold and often grey, with the Thracian plain and Sofia around or below freezing and a thin snow cover that comes and goes. Sofia and Plovdiv are perfectly good winter city breaks if you dress for it and the museums are the point. The coast is shut: Sunny Beach and Golden Sands are boarded up, and Varna and Burgas are working cities with the sea alongside.

March

The hinge month and the hardest to plan. The upper slopes at Bansko often hold into the second half of the month and spring skiing there can be excellent, but the lower runs go patchy and the season can end abruptly. Down on the plain it begins to turn: almond and cherry blossom around Plovdiv and in the Rhodope foothills, the terraces putting tables out on a warm afternoon, and Martenitsa tassels on every coat lapel from the first of the month. Mountain roads above about 1,500 metres are still winter roads. Prices everywhere are at their lowest.

April

A good, cheap, slightly unreliable month. Sofia and Plovdiv are pleasant for walking, the monasteries are quiet, the hills are green and the wild flowers start in the Rhodopes. Rain is frequent and the mountains can still get snow. Orthodox Easter falls in April or early May and is the biggest family weekend of the year, which means full village guesthouses, a serious meal and many small businesses shut for a few days. The sea is far too cold to swim in and the resorts are only beginning to open at the end of the month.

May

The best all-round month in the country, and the one we would choose. Warm without the heat, long days, everything open and the prices still low. The Valley of the Roses is picked from the middle of the month, at first light, and the fields between Kazanlak and Karlovo are worth the drive. Plovdiv and Veliko Tarnovo are at their best. The Rila and Pirin lower valleys are open for walking, though the high lakes and the passes above two thousand metres still hold snow and the Seven Rila Lakes is not yet a casual day out. The sea is swimmable only for the hardy.

June

The other best month, and better than May for the mountains. The Rila lakes chairlift and the high huts come into service, the ridges clear through the month and the hut-to-hut walking opens up. The rose festival at Kazanlak is in the first days of the month. The coast works from the middle of June, with the sea warm enough and the resorts open but not yet at full August pitch, which makes it the best fortnight of the year for Nessebar and Sozopol. Inland it is hot by the end of the month on the Thracian plain.

July

High summer: hot, busy and the only time the whole coast is in full swing. The plain regularly goes past thirty-five degrees, Plovdiv in the afternoon is genuinely punishing and the sensible shape of a day there is early, long lunch, late. The mountains are the answer — Bansko, Borovets and the Rhodope villages are ten degrees cooler and run summer lift service — and so is the sea. Sunny Beach is at its loudest. Book coastal rooms ahead for July and August; this is the one time of year Bulgaria sells out.

August

The same as July and a little more so, with the addition of Bulgarian and Romanian family holidays filling the coast. The sea is at its warmest, the plain at its hottest and the cities at their emptiest of locals. It is the peak price fortnight on the coast and in the mountains both. Thunderstorms build over the ranges in the afternoons, which matters if you are high up: start early and be off the ridge by two. Towards the end of the month the heat breaks a little and the first good walking weather returns.

September

The quiet favourite. The sea stays warm into the second half of the month and the coast empties after the first week, which makes early September the best value week of the summer on the whole Black Sea. Inland the heat drops to something comfortable, the grape harvest is on in the Thracian Valley and around Melnik, and the mountains are at their most reliable for walking — stable weather, clear air, the huts still open. If you want one month that does the coast and the mountains and the cities all at once, this is it.

October

Beautiful inland and finished on the coast. The Rhodopes and the Balkan range turn, the light is long and low, the monasteries and the old towns are at their best with nobody in them, and Plovdiv is a pleasure. The high huts close through the month and the chairlifts stop, so the two-thousand-metre days are over by the end of it. The resorts shut down, and by late October the sea towns are winding up for winter. Prices fall back to the low season everywhere.

November and December

November is the low point of the year for a visitor: wet, grey, the mountains between seasons with no snow and no walking. It is the cheapest month and the cities still work. December picks up sharply — Sofia and Plovdiv put on Christmas markets, the ski resorts open as the snow arrives, usually from the middle of the month, and the week between Christmas and New Year is a full-price ski week booked a long way ahead. Orthodox Christmas practice means the Bulgarian holiday runs 24 to 26 December, with a large family meal on Christmas Eve.

The weather, and what it does to a trip

Three climates in one country

The Balkan range runs the full width of Bulgaria and splits the weather in two. North of it, the Danube plain has colder winters and a continental swing. South of it, the Thracian plain is warmer and genuinely hot in summer, which is why Plovdiv is usually the hottest city in the country. Then there is the third climate, the mountains, where the Rila and the Pirin hold snow into June above two thousand metres and where you can leave a thirty-degree valley and be in single figures an hour later. The coast is milder than the inland plain in both directions, and the sea itself is the thing that lags: warm in September, cold in May.

The Black Sea season is short

Shorter than most people expect. The water is realistically swimmable from the middle of June to the end of September, and the resort infrastructure runs on roughly the same dates: many hotels, restaurants and the whole of Sunny Beach simply shut from October to May. If you want the old towns without the crowds, late September and early October are perfect for walking Nessebar and Sozopol but too cold for a beach holiday. Varna and Burgas are real cities and stay open all year, which is why they make better out-of-season bases than any resort.

The ski season sets the winter calendar

Bansko, Borovets and Pamporovo generally run from mid-December to late March, with the most dependable snow from mid-January to early March. Bansko has the highest terrain and the longest season, Pamporovo the sunniest and gentlest. Snowmaking covers the lower runs at all three. The two weeks to avoid if you can are the New Year week and the February half-term, when prices peak and the Bansko gondola queue becomes a story in itself; the last week of January and the middle of March are the value weeks.

The high mountains set your summer calendar

The Seven Rila Lakes, the Pirin ridges and the hut-to-hut routes are a late-June-to-September proposition, not a May one. Snow lies in the corries into June, the chairlift at Panichishte and the mountain huts are seasonal, and afternoon thunderstorms are the normal pattern in July and August. Weather at 2,000 to 2,900 metres here turns faster than the forecast and there is no lift-served escape off most of it. Carry layers and waterproofs in August, start early, and treat the last lift down as a hard deadline rather than a plan.

Dates worth planning around

Martenitsa — 1 March

On the first of March Bulgarians give each other martenitsi, small red and white woollen tassels, and wear them pinned to a coat until they see the first stork or blossom, then tie them to a fruit tree. It is the most charming thing in the Bulgarian calendar and it goes on for weeks, so a March visitor will be given one. Baba Marta, the day itself, is not a public holiday.

Orthodox Easter — a moving date

The biggest family weekend of the year, and it usually falls later than Western Easter. Expect village guesthouses full, a large meal with lamb and red eggs, midnight services worth attending at any of the monasteries, and a number of small shops and family restaurants closed for a few days. Check the date for your own year, because it moves by several weeks.

The Rose Festival at Kazanlak — early June

The rose harvest runs from the middle of May to about the middle of June and is done at first light, because the oil degrades as the day warms. The festival itself is in the first days of June, with a rose-picking ritual, a parade and a queen, and it is frankly staged for visitors — but the valley, the distilleries and the fields at six in the morning are not, and they are the reason to come. Book Kazanlak accommodation well ahead for that weekend.

3 March — the national day

The Day of Liberation, marking the 1878 Treaty of San Stefano, with the main ceremony at the Shipka Pass memorial in the Balkan range where the decisive fighting happened. A public holiday, so banks and many shops shut, and the Shipka road is busy.

24 May — the alphabet and culture

The Day of Bulgarian Education and Culture and of Slavonic Script, which is a national holiday in honour of the Cyrillic alphabet and the saints who brought literacy — a thing worth seeing in the country where that alphabet was developed. Schools and towns hold processions, and it is one of the warmer public occasions in the calendar.

Surva at Pernik — late January

A masquerade festival of kukeri, the costumed and belled figures who dance through the streets to drive off evil spirits, held at Pernik south-west of Sofia in the second half of January. It is the biggest event of its kind in the Balkans, it is loud and genuinely strange, and it is an easy day trip from the capital in the middle of the ski season.

What it costs, and when

Bulgaria is one of the least expensive countries in the European Union and the euro changeover on 1 January 2026 did not alter that — the lev was converted at a fixed rate rather than repriced. Within the year the pattern is clear enough to plan around. The dearest weeks are the New Year ski week, the February half-term at Bansko, and the whole of late July and August on the Black Sea, when coastal rooms can double and genuinely sell out. The cheapest are November, and March and April inland. The best value of the year is the first fortnight of September on the coast, when the sea is still warm and the prices have already dropped, and late January or mid-March for skiing.

Fishing here

Bulgaria is better fishing country than its reputation, and it comes in three kinds. The mountain rivers and dam lakes of the Rila, the Pirin and the Rhodopes hold brown trout, and the big reservoirs — Iskar above Sofia, Dospat and Batak in the Rhodopes, Kardzhali on the Arda — are fished for carp, pike, perch and zander, with Dospat in particular known for its trout. The Danube along the northern border is big-river fishing for catfish, carp and zander, and the same rule applies here as on the Romanian bank: sturgeon are protected and anyone offering sturgeon fishing is offering something they should not. The Black Sea is fished from the ports for mackerel, bonito, bluefish and turbot, mostly from a boat out of Varna, Burgas or one of the smaller harbours. A licence is required for fresh water and is bought locally, seasons are dated and change, and many of the best dam-lake stretches are leased to local clubs, so this is a trip to arrange on the ground with someone who knows which water is open. Getting in: what applies, and what does not

Bulgaria is in the European Union and, since 1 January 2025, a full member of the Schengen area. Arriving from another Schengen country is an internal journey with no routine passport check. Since 1 January 2026 it has also been in the euro area, so there is no currency to change. The rules below apply when you first enter the Schengen area from outside it — for Bulgaria, that means arriving from Turkey, Serbia, North Macedonia or from beyond Europe.

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen external border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave. Ninety days in any one hundred and eighty is the visa-free tourist allowance for American, Canadian, British and Australian visitors, counted across the whole Schengen area rather than against Bulgaria alone.

Bulgaria’s seasons — questions

What is the best month to visit Bulgaria?

May, June and September, and if you must pick one, September — it is the only month that does the coast, the high mountains and the cities all well at once, with warm sea, stable walking weather and the crowds gone. May is the prettiest inland and the cheapest of the three. June is the best for the mountains. July and August are for the beach or the ski resorts in summer mode, and not for Plovdiv in the afternoon.

When is the Bulgarian ski season?

Roughly mid-December to late March, with the most reliable snow between mid-January and early March. Bansko has the highest terrain and holds the season longest, Borovets is closest to Sofia and Pamporovo is the sunniest and gentlest. Avoid the New Year week and the February half-term if you can: those are the peak-price weeks and the Bansko lift queues are at their worst. Late January and mid-March are the value weeks.

When can you swim in the Black Sea?

Realistically from the middle of June to the end of September, with August the warmest and early September the best combination of warm water and smaller crowds. May is too cold for most people and October is over. Many coastal hotels and most of Sunny Beach close from October to May, so an out-of-season coastal trip means basing yourself in Varna or Burgas, which are real cities and open all year.

When is the rose harvest?

Mid-May to about mid-June, picked at first light. The festival at Kazanlak is in the first days of June. If the roses are the reason for your trip, come in the last week of May or the first week of June, be prepared to be out of the hotel by half past five in the morning, and book early — Kazanlak is a small town and that weekend fills.

Is Bulgaria a good winter city break?

Sofia and Plovdiv both work well in December, with Christmas markets, serious museums, warm restaurants and very low prices, and Sofia has the advantage of Vitosha and Borovets within an hour if you want a day on snow. January and February inland are cold and often grey, which is fine for a museum-and-restaurant trip and poor for walking. The coast in winter is for Varna and Burgas as cities, not for the resorts.

Does the euro change what a trip costs?

No. Bulgaria adopted the euro on 1 January 2026 at a rate fixed irrevocably at 1.95583 lev to the euro, which was a conversion rather than a repricing, and the country remains among the least expensive in the European Union. What it does change is the admin: there is no longer any currency to change or carry home, and prices you find quoted in lev on older websites are from before the changeover.
