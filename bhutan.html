Bhutan Travel Guide

Researched and compiled by the Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Taktsang — the Tiger’s Nest — above the Paro valley. Photo: Wikimedia Commons.

Bhutan is the one country in the Himalaya that decided how many visitors it wanted before it decided how to attract them. You pay a fee for every night you stay, you travel with a guide, and in return you get a kingdom of about eight hundred thousand people that has not been rearranged for tourists. This guide covers the four valleys where our booking partner actually holds rooms — Thimphu, Paro, Punakha and Bumthang — the border town of Phuentsholing, and it is straight with you about the parts of the country where it holds none.

The regions of Bhutan

Six pieces of country, and the roads between them are slower than the map suggests. Where our partner lists rooms we link them; where it lists none we say so and leave the booking to the operator who drives you there.

Thimphu & the western valleysPART I

The capital, and the only city in the country — a long single valley of whitewashed buildings with painted window frames, the Tashichho Dzong at the north end and the great gilded Buddha Dordenma on the ridge above. Most trips begin or end with two nights here, and it is where our booking partner holds the widest choice of rooms.

Paro & the Tiger’s NestPART II

The valley you land in. Paro holds the country’s main international airport, its old dzong above the river, and the Taktsang monastery — the Tiger’s Nest — on the cliff two hours’ walk above the valley floor. Many visitors sleep here on the first and last night simply because the flights are early.

Punakha, Wangdue & PhobjikhaPART III

Over the Dochula pass and a long way down: Punakha sits far lower and warmer than Thimphu, in rice country where the Pho Chhu and the Mo Chhu meet at the old winter capital’s dzong. Wangdue Phodrang is next door, and the broad glacial bowl of Phobjikha — where black-necked cranes winter — is up a side road beyond it.

Bumthang & the centrePART IV

Four high valleys in the middle of the country, reached by a long day’s drive east or a short domestic flight into Bathpalathang. Bumthang is the oldest Buddhist ground in Bhutan and the quietest part of the standard route; the hotels are in and around Jakar, the main town.

Phuentsholing & the southern borderPART V

The town on the Indian border below the hills, the overland way in from West Bengal, and hot and humid in a way the mountain valleys never are. It matters for one more reason: the Department of Tourism has waived the Sustainable Development Fee for visits of up to 24 hours to Phuentsholing, Samtse, Gelephu and Samdrupjongkhar, within the marked border zones only — go beyond the Rinchending checkpost at Phuentsholing and the fee applies again.

The east and the high northPART VI

Trashigang, Mongar, Lhuentse and the high valleys up against the Tibetan border are the part of Bhutan that most visitors never reach, and we will not dress up what we found: our booking partner lists almost no rooms out there. The east is normally travelled with an operator who arranges the beds, and the far north is trekking country where an accredited guide is required in any case. We have left this part of the country without a booking button on purpose.

Before you book

The Sustainable Development Fee — read this one first

Bhutan charges a nightly levy on top of everything else you spend. The Department of Tourism puts it at USD 100 per night for adults of every nationality except India, USD 50 a night for children aged 6 up to their twelfth birthday, and nothing for children under 6. Indian nationals showing a passport or voter ID pay Nu. 1,200 per person per night, with the same concessions at Nu. 600 and nothing under 6. It is paid with the visa application, it is refunded for nights you do not use, and it pays for free healthcare, education and the country’s forest cover. There is also a one-off, non-refundable USD 40 visa fee. Figures read on the Department’s own site on 18 November 2026 and worth re-checking, because they have changed before.

Visas and permits

Everyone except Indian nationals needs a visa, and for most passports it must be approved before you travel. Apply online through the Department of Tourism’s portal, or have a hotel or certified operator apply for you; you will sign a short Pledge of Friendship as part of the form. Allow up to five working days. The visa is good for a stay of up to 90 days from entry and can be extended. Indian nationals need a permit and may obtain it at the point of entry, though the Department advises arranging it first to avoid the queue; Bangladeshi and Maldivian nationals need a visa but may apply on arrival. Travel insurance stopped being compulsory in April 2024; take it anyway.

You will have a guide, and that is the rule

You no longer have to buy a package from a tour operator — since the country reopened in September 2022 you can book your own flights and hotels, which is exactly what the booking links on these pages are for. But the Department of Tourism is unambiguous that a guide must accompany you at all times, that you need one to go inside the dzongs and monuments, and that treks must be run by an accredited operator or guide. Budget for the guide and the vehicle as part of the trip, not as an extra you might skip.

Getting there and getting about

Bhutan has two international airports, Paro and Gelephu, served by Drukair and Bhutan Airlines from Bangkok, Delhi, Kolkata, Bagdogra, Bodhgaya, Dhaka, Kathmandu, Guwahati, Dubai and Singapore. Nearly everyone flies into Paro, where the approach between the hills is the one piece of this trip you should have a window seat for. There are domestic airstrips at Bathpalathang in Bumthang, Yonphula in the east and Gelephu. On the ground you hire a vehicle with a driver, or your operator provides one: self-drive hire is not available. Bringing your own car costs Nu. 4,500 per car per day and a guide is still required.

When to go

October and November are the best weeks of the year — clear air after the monsoon, the mountains visible, and most of the big festivals. March to May is the other good window. June to September is the monsoon: wet, green, cloudy over the peaks and slower on the mountain roads. Winter is bright and cold in the western valleys, with snow above about 3,000 metres and high passes that can close. The month-by-month figures for four valleys are in the best time to visit Bhutan.

Money

The currency is the ngultrum (BTN), pegged one to one with the Indian rupee, and Indian rupees are accepted alongside it. ATMs and banks take Visa and Mastercard and cards work in the larger hotels and shops in the towns; assume they do not in the valleys. US dollars and rupees in cash are widely accepted, and you may bring in the equivalent of USD 10,000. The Department warns Indian visitors that USD-denominated cards do not work in Bhutan under Reserve Bank of India rules — bring a card valid for India, Bhutan and Nepal.

Fishing here

Bhutan’s rivers hold snow trout and introduced brown trout, and fishing is tightly licensed and closed outright around monasteries and on religious days; it is a permit-and-guide affair rather than something to arrange on the day. We have not written a Bhutan fishing guide yet, so rather than invent one we will point you at the shelf: the whole of it is in The Guild Fishing Library, and the nearest neighbour in spirit is our Mongolia fly fishing guide.

Bhutan questions, answered

How much does it cost to visit Bhutan?

More than its neighbours, on purpose, and the number you have to plan around is the Sustainable Development Fee. The Department of Tourism sets it at USD 100 per night for adult visitors of every nationality except India; children aged 6 up to their twelfth birthday pay USD 50 a night and children under 6 pay nothing. Indian nationals pay Nu. 1,200 per person per night, with the same child concessions at Nu. 600 and nothing under 6. On top of that there is a one-off, non-refundable visa application fee of USD 40. Those two charges come before a single night of hotel, meal, guide or vehicle, and they are what make Bhutan a considered trip rather than a cheap one. We read these figures on the Department of Tourism's own site on 18 November 2026; check them again before you pay, because they have been revised more than once.

Do you need a visa for Bhutan?

Almost certainly yes. Every nationality except India needs a visa, and for most passports it has to be approved before you fly. You can apply yourself through the Department of Tourism's online portal, or let a hotel or a certified tour operator apply on your behalf; you will need a scan of your passport, a passport photo, your arrival and departure dates and a card to pay the USD 40 fee and the Sustainable Development Fee for your whole stay. The Department says a correctly completed application takes up to five working days. Indian nationals need a permit rather than a visa and can get one at the point of entry on an Indian passport or voter ID, though the Department recommends arranging it beforehand. Visitors from Bangladesh and the Maldives need a visa but may apply in advance or on arrival. A Bhutanese visa allows a stay of up to 90 days from the date of entry.

Do you have to travel with a tour operator or a guide?

Not an operator, but yes to a guide. Since Bhutan reopened on 23 September 2022 you may book your own flights, hotels and transport independently, and plenty of visitors now do. What has not changed is the guide: the Department of Tourism states plainly that it is mandatory to be accompanied by a guide at all times in Bhutan, that a guide is required to enter dzongs and monuments, and that every trek must be run by an accredited operator or guide. The route permits that used to be needed have been dropped for visitors travelling with a certified guide. You also have to sleep in accommodation the Department has approved, which is one reason the hotels on this page are ordinary licensed hotels rather than private rentals.

When is the best time to go to Bhutan?

October and November first, then March to May. Autumn follows the monsoon and brings the clearest air of the year, the best mountain views and most of the big festivals; spring is the other fine window, drier than summer with rhododendron in flower on the passes. June to September is the Indian monsoon, which hits the southern hills hardest, makes mountain roads slower and less certain and keeps the high peaks behind cloud. Winter is sharp and bright in the western valleys and genuinely cold higher up, with snow common above about 3,000 metres and the high passes liable to close.
