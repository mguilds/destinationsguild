Nis

Where to Stay in Nis

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Nis is the third city of Serbia and the one the rest of the Balkans passes through: it sits at the crossroads where the road and rail from Belgrade split for Sofia and for Thessaloniki, and it has done since the Romans built Naissus there. Constantine the Great was born in it. The Ottomans held it for four hundred years and left the fortress whose walls still enclose the park in the middle of town. It is not a picturesque city — it is a working southern one, with a big university, a famous grill alley and thermal baths ten kilometres out — and it is cheap, friendly and much less visited than it deserves. Two nights covers it comfortably, three if you want the spa or the Roman sites.

The centre: Obrenoviceva and Kralja Milana

Most centralLevel walkingCheap rooms

The pedestrian heart, running from the fortress bridge up through Obrenoviceva to King Milan Square, with the shops, the cafes, the National Theatre and most of the city's hotels on or just off it. It is flat, it is five minutes from the fortress gate and ten from the grill lanes, and it is where you want to be for a short stay. Prices here are low even by Serbian standards. The square and the main streets are busy in the evening and there is some traffic noise on the ring streets.

The fortress and Kazandzijsko Sokace

Best eatingAtmosphereWalk everywhere

Across the Nisava from the main square, the Ottoman fortress encloses a green park with the Istanbul Gate, a hammam, a mosque and the excavations of Naissus inside the walls. Immediately beside the bridge is Kazandzijsko Sokace, the Coppersmiths' Alley — one short lane of grill houses with tables down the middle, and the best cheap eating in Serbia. Staying on this side puts you next to both. It is lively until late and that is the whole character of it.

Niska Banja

Thermal spaLevel parksOlder visitors

The spa town ten kilometres east of the city, at the foot of Suva Planina, where thermal water has been used since Roman times and the sanatoria and hotels sit around level parks and promenades. It is a different sort of stay from the city — quiet, geared to treatment and rest, inexpensive, and long popular with older guests. Regular buses run in to Nis in about twenty-five minutes. The right base for a week of sitting still.

The university and Medijana side

ResidentialGood valueNear the Roman villa

East of the centre towards the river bend, the newer part of town with the university faculties, the hospital, apartment blocks and the Mediana archaeological site — the Roman imperial villa complex associated with Constantine, with mosaic floors under cover. Rooms here are apartments and small hotels at the bottom of the price range, with a fifteen- or twenty-minute walk or a short bus ride into the centre.

The bus and railway stations sit close together on the western edge of the centre, and there are cheap rooms around them. Getting around Nis

On foot

The centre is flat and small, and the walk from King Milan Square across the bridge to the fortress and the grill lane takes about five minutes. Pavements are decent in the pedestrian core and patchier beyond it. The one thing worth knowing is that the city sprawls east and the sights outside the centre — Mediana, Cele Kula, Niska Banja — are all on that side and want a bus or a taxi.

Buses

City buses are frequent and very cheap, and are how you reach Niska Banja, Mediana and the Cele Kula. Pay on board or buy a card. The intercity bus station handles everywhere else, and Nis is a major hub for the south and for the Bulgarian and Macedonian borders.

Taxis

Inexpensive and the easy option for the outlying sites; a ride across town costs very little. Use a marked rank or have your hotel call one, and check the meter.

To and from the airport and the motorway

Nis Constantine the Great Airport is a small one about four kilometres from the centre, with a handful of low-cost European routes — useful if one of them happens to serve you, and not a substitute for Belgrade. The city sits on the main motorway corridor, so it is about three hours by road from Belgrade, under two from Sofia and a straightforward drive to Skopje.

Getting out to the sights

The Cele Kula or Skull Tower is on the Constantinople road a couple of kilometres out; Mediana is a little further on the same side; Niska Banja is twenty-five minutes by bus. Further afield, the Devil's Town rock pillars are about an hour and a half south and want a car or a tour, and the Stara Planina mountains and Midzor are east towards the Bulgarian border.

When to come

Nis is the hottest city in Serbia in summer and sits in a valley that holds the heat. May, June and September are the best months by a clear margin, and September is particularly good: warm, dry and with the grill lane tables outside. July and August regularly push into the mid thirties and the middle of the day is for shade; it is also when the city is quietest, as the students are away. October is mild and pleasant. November to February is cold, grey and often foggy in the valley, cheap, and perfectly workable for the fortress, the museums and the thermal baths at Niska Banja, which are at their most appealing in winter. The city's own date is the Nisville jazz festival in the fortress in August, which moves from year to year.

Getting in: what applies, and what does not

Serbia is not in the European Union and not in the Schengen area, so it runs its own border. British passport holders may visit without a visa for up to 90 days in any 6-month period, with a passport valid for at least 90 days beyond the day they leave — that is what the UK government’s Serbia travel advice said when we read it on 26 November 2026. Many other nationalities including the United States are admitted visa-free for comparable periods; we could not open the American source on the day we wrote this, so check your own. Two Serbian rules catch people out: make sure your passport is actually stamped on entry, because leaving without an entry stamp can mean charges of illegal immigration, a heavy fine and a prison sentence; and you must register with the local police within 24 hours, which a hotel or registered apartment does for you automatically. If Kosovo is also in your plans, read the Kosovo paragraph in The Serbia Travel Guide before you book anything — entering Serbia from Kosovo without an existing Serbian entry stamp usually means being refused.

If your trip also crosses into Hungary, Croatia, Bulgaria, Greece or anywhere else in the Schengen area — and most flights from North America reach Serbia through a Schengen hub — the following applies to that crossing and not to Serbia:

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Nis — questions

Is Nis worth a stop?

Yes, and it is the obvious break on the drive south from Belgrade towards Greece or Bulgaria. The fortress park, the grill lanes, the Roman sites and the spa make an easy two days, it is cheaper than Belgrade or Novi Sad, and almost nobody from Western Europe stops here, which shows in how you are treated. It is not a beautiful city and nobody in Nis pretends otherwise.

What is the Cele Kula?

The Skull Tower, on the old Constantinople road out of the city: a small structure the Ottomans built after the battle of Cegar in 1809, faced with the skulls of Serbian fighters, as a warning. A chapel was later built around it. A handful of skulls remain. It is a grim, short visit and an important one, and it is a couple of kilometres from the centre by bus or taxi.

Where do I eat in Nis?

Kazandzijsko Sokace, the Coppersmiths' Alley beside the fortress bridge — a short lane of grill houses with tables down the middle of it. Cevapi, pljeskavica stuffed with kajmak, grilled peppers, salad, and rakija that arrives whether or not you ordered it. Early evening, outside, no ceremony. It is the single best cheap meal in the country and it is why a good many Serbs come to Nis at all.

What is there at Niska Banja?

A thermal spa town ten kilometres east, used since Roman times, with sanatoria, hotels, treatment houses and level parks laid out for walking. It is inexpensive, quiet, and traditionally where people come for rheumatic and cardiac treatment, so it suits a slow week rather than a sightseeing base. Buses run in to Nis in about twenty-five minutes.

Is Nis a good base for the south?

It is the natural one. The motorway corridor puts Sofia under two hours away and Skopje a straightforward drive; Devil's Town is about ninety minutes south; the Stara Planina mountains and Serbia's highest peak, Midzor at 2,169 metres, are east on the Bulgarian frontier. For the monasteries at Studenica and Sopocani you are better based further west.

Was Constantine really born here?

At Naissus, which is Nis, yes — he is the best known of the seventeen Roman emperors born in what is now Serbia. The imperial villa complex at Mediana on the eastern edge of the city, with mosaic floors under cover, is associated with him, and excavations of the Roman city sit inside the fortress walls. The airport is named after him, which tells you how much the city makes of it.
