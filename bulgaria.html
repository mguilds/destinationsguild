Bulgaria

The Bulgaria Travel Guide

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Bulgaria is the quiet one in this corner of Europe, and the one that repays a visitor fastest. It is a country of mountains and one long coast: the Balkan range runs the whole width of it from the Serbian border to the Black Sea and gives the peninsula its name, the Rila and the Pirin rise in the south-west to Musala at 2,925 metres, the highest point anywhere in the Balkans, and the Rhodopes roll south towards Greece in long forested ridges full of villages. Between and around them: Sofia, a capital with a two-thousand-metre mountain on its doorstep and Roman streets under its squares; Plovdiv, which has been lived in continuously for some eight thousand years and has a Roman theatre still in use for concerts; Veliko Tarnovo on its gorge, the medieval capital; the Valley of the Roses, where most of the world’s rose oil is distilled; monastery churches and Thracian tombs on the UNESCO list; and 378 kilometres of Black Sea coast that runs from package resorts to walled old towns on rocky peninsulas. It joined the European Union in 2007, the Schengen area on 1 January 2025, and the euro on 1 January 2026 — which means the lev in your guidebook is out of date. It remains one of the least expensive countries in the Union.

Why visit Bulgaria

Plovdiv is one of the oldest cities in Europe, and it is a pleasure to be in

People have lived on these three hills for something like eight thousand years, which makes Plovdiv one of the oldest continuously inhabited cities in Europe, and the layers are all still visible: a Thracian settlement, a Roman theatre that holds concerts to this day, a Roman stadium under the main shopping street with its curved end exposed like a cross-section, Ottoman mosques, and a hillside of enormous nineteenth-century merchant houses in the Old Town. It was European Capital of Culture in 2019 and spent the money well. Kapana, the old craftsmen’s quarter, is now the best few streets of bars and small galleries in the country.

Rila Monastery, and the monastery country around it

Rila is the great set piece of Bulgarian travel and deserves the reputation: a tenth-century foundation rebuilt in the nineteenth, high in a forested valley south of Sofia, with striped arcades on four storeys around a courtyard and a church covered inside and out in frescoes. It is on the UNESCO World Heritage list and it is an easy day from Sofia. Bachkovo, south of Plovdiv, is the second-largest and much less visited. Boyana Church on the edge of Sofia is small, dark and holds thirteenth-century portrait frescoes that are genuinely startling — also UNESCO-listed, and twenty minutes from the city centre.

Two serious mountain ranges, and the cheapest good skiing in Europe

The Rila and the Pirin are real alpine country, with glacial lakes, marked hut-to-hut routes and Musala at 2,925 metres, the highest peak in the Balkans. Pirin National Park is on the UNESCO list. In winter the same mountains carry Bansko, Borovets and Pamporovo, which between them are the best value lift-served skiing on the continent by a wide margin — not the longest or the highest, but a week there costs a fraction of the Alps and the snow record is decent from January to March.

A coast with two quite different halves

The Black Sea coast runs 378 kilometres and splits cleanly. The big resorts — Sunny Beach, Golden Sands, Albena — are package country, cheap and very busy in July and August, with Sunny Beach in particular being somewhere you either want or very much do not. Then there are the old towns: Nessebar, on a rocky peninsula and on the UNESCO list for its churches and wooden houses, and Sozopol, which is the same idea with fewer coaches. North of Varna the coast empties out towards Kaliakra. Out of season the whole thing goes quiet, which is its own reason to come.

Thracian tombs, and a Roman layer nobody mentions

The Thracians left gold and they left tombs, and two of the tombs are on the UNESCO list: the painted fourth-century-BC tomb at Kazanlak, in the Valley of the Roses, and the Sveshtari tomb in the north-east with its caryatid figures. Add the Madara Rider, a horseman carved into a cliff in the eighth century and also UNESCO-listed, the rock-hewn churches at Ivanovo above the Rusenski Lom, and Roman remains under half the city centres in the country. The Varna Archaeological Museum holds the Varna gold hoard, the oldest worked gold yet found anywhere.

It is in the EU, in Schengen and now in the euro, and still inexpensive

Bulgaria joined the European Union in 2007, became a full Schengen member on 1 January 2025 and adopted the euro on 1 January 2026. For a visitor that means no routine border check arriving from elsewhere in Europe, and no currency to change — two frictions that used to be part of a Bulgarian trip and are simply gone. What has not changed is the price level: it remains one of the least expensive countries in the Union, and the euro arrived at a fixed conversion rate rather than as a repricing.

Bulgaria at a glance

Capital

Sofia, in the west of the country in a high basin at around 550 metres with Vitosha mountain directly above it. About 1.2 million people in the city. Sofia Airport is the main international gateway and is unusually easy to reach: metro Line 1 runs from Terminal 2 into the centre in about twenty minutes, which is also the answer to the city’s taxi problem. Varna, Burgas and Plovdiv have their own airports, and the two coastal ones carry heavy summer charter traffic.

Money

The euro, since 1 January 2026. Bulgaria is the twenty-first member of the euro area. The lev was converted at a rate fixed at 1.95583 to the euro and stopped being legal tender on 1 February 2026, so any price you see quoted in lev is from before that. Cards are taken almost everywhere, contactless is normal and city transport mostly accepts it; keep some cash for village shops, monastery donation boxes and mountain huts.

Language

Bulgarian, a South Slavic language written in Cyrillic — which was developed here in the late ninth century and which Bulgaria brought into the European Union as a third official alphabet in 2007. Major signs, stations and the metro are dual-signed in Latin script; small-town signs are not. English is widely spoken by younger people and in tourism, Russian by many older Bulgarians, and German along the coast. One local habit worth knowing: a shake of the head can mean yes and a nod can mean no. It is less common among younger city Bulgarians than it was, but it still catches visitors out in villages, so listen for the word as well as watching the head.

Borders

In NATO since 2004, in the European Union since 2007, a full member of the Schengen area since 1 January 2025 and in the euro area since 1 January 2026. Neighbours are Romania across the Danube to the north, Serbia and North Macedonia to the west, Greece to the south and Turkey to the south-east. The Greek and Turkish land borders are external Schengen borders and are where the Entry/Exit System applies; the Romanian crossings are now internal.

Getting about

Driving is on the right, and a vignette is required for the motorways — electronic, sold online and at borders and filling stations, and usually already on a hire car. The motorway network links Sofia, Plovdiv and Burgas and is being extended towards Varna, so the main corridors are quick and everything else is not. Trains are cheap and slow; the Sofia to Plovdiv and Sofia to Varna lines are usable but the bus is often faster. Within cities, Sofia has a two-line metro and the others are tram, trolleybus and bus.

Shape of it

About 111,000 square kilometres, a little smaller than Iceland or the state of Ohio, with a population of roughly six and a half million that has been falling for years through emigration and a low birth rate. Mountain and hill country make up most of it: the Balkan range east to west, the Rila and the Pirin in the south-west, the Rhodopes in the south, the Danube plain in the north and the Thracian plain in the middle. A parliamentary republic and a member of the single market.

The regions

Six parts of the country that feel genuinely different from one another — the capital under its mountain, the Thracian plain, the Balkan range and the Valley of the Roses, the Pirin and the south-west, the Rhodopes, and the coast.

Sofia and the west

The capital in its high basin with Vitosha above it, the Rila mountains and the great monastery an easy day to the south, and the ski resort of Borovets on the northern slope of the range.

  • SofiaGuild guide
  • Rila Monastery — a tenth-century foundation rebuilt in the nineteenth, striped arcades around a frescoed church, on the UNESCO World Heritage list, and about two hours south of Sofia
  • Boyana Church — small, dark and holding thirteenth-century frescoes of startling quality, on the edge of Sofia under Vitosha and also UNESCO-listed
  • Borovets — the oldest of the Bulgarian ski resorts, on the north side of the Rila an hour from Sofia, and a walking base in summer
  • Vitosha — the 2,290-metre mountain on the city’s doorstep, a nature park, reached by road or chairlift, with the Stone Rivers boulder fields and real walking an hour from the centre

Plovdiv and the Thracian plain

The hot middle of the country: the old city on its hills, the wine valley around it, and the monastery and mountains closing the plain to the south.

  • PlovdivGuild guide
  • The Roman theatre and the stadium — a second-century theatre still used for concerts, and the curved end of the stadium exposed beneath the main shopping street
  • Bachkovo Monastery — the second-largest in the country, founded in 1083, in a wooded valley half an hour south of Plovdiv and far quieter than Rila
  • The Thracian Valley — the wine country either side of the Maritsa, with a run of estates open to visitors between Plovdiv and Stara Zagora
  • Asenovgrad and Asen’s Fortress — a thirteenth-century fort on a crag at the mouth of the Chepelare gorge, the road south into the Rhodopes

The Balkan range and the Valley of the Roses

The spine of the country and the long valley behind it: the medieval capital on its gorge, the rose fields, the craft villages and the pass where the 1877 battle was fought.

  • Veliko Tarnovo — the medieval capital, stacked above a loop of the Yantra with the Tsarevets fortress on its hill, and the best base in the north
  • Kazanlak — the centre of the Valley of the Roses, with the painted fourth-century-BC Thracian tomb on the UNESCO list and a rose festival in the first days of June
  • Shipka Pass — the pass over the Balkan range with the memorial to the 1877 battle, and one of the great views in the country from the top
  • Tryavna and Etar — a nineteenth-century craft town with its own school of woodcarving, and an open-air museum of working water-powered workshops outside Gabrovo
  • Arbanasi — a village of fortified stone merchant houses on the ridge above Veliko Tarnovo, five kilometres out and a different century

Pirin and the south-west

The highest and wildest mountains in the country, the ski town under them, and the wine villages on the warm southern side towards Greece.

  • BanskoGuild guide
  • Pirin National Park — a UNESCO World Heritage site of glacial lakes, limestone ridges and old pine forest, with Vihren at 2,914 metres above Bansko
  • Melnik — a tiny village under sandstone pyramids in the far south-west, making the dark Shiroka Melnishka Loza wine in cellars cut into the rock
  • Rozhen Monastery — a walled fourteenth-century monastery on the ridge above Melnik, reached on foot from the village in under an hour
  • Rila lakes — the Seven Rila Lakes, a chairlift and a walk from Panichishte, and the best day on foot in the country if the weather holds

The Rhodopes

Long forested ridges running south to the Greek border, with villages that kept their architecture, caves, gorges and the gentlest of the ski resorts.

  • Pamporovo — the sunniest and most sheltered of the ski resorts, high in the Rhodopes above Smolyan, and gentle enough to learn on
  • Shiroka Laka — a village of white houses with black timber, a school of bagpipe playing, and the road over to Devin
  • The Devil’s Throat and Yagodina caves — two very different caves in the Trigrad gorge, one a waterfall swallowed whole by the mountain
  • Belintash and Perperikon — a Thracian rock sanctuary on a plateau, and a whole rock-cut city above Kardzhali, both still being excavated
  • Smolyan and the Smolyan lakes — the long thin town that is the base for this end of the range, and the lakes in the forest above it

The Black Sea coast

Three hundred and seventy-eight kilometres with two characters: the big resorts, and the walled old towns on their rocky peninsulas. Varna is the northern capital, Burgas the southern door.

  • VarnaGuild guide
  • BurgasGuild guide
  • Nessebar — an old town of churches and wooden houses on a rock peninsula, on the UNESCO World Heritage list, and walkable in a morning
  • Sozopol — the quieter of the two old towns, a Greek foundation on a headland south of Burgas with a beach on either side
  • Sunny Beach — the biggest resort on the coast, cheap and very loud in July and August, immediately north of Nessebar

Things to do in Bulgaria

Stand in the Roman theatre at Plovdiv

It was built in the second century, seats several thousand, looks out over the plain towards the Rhodopes, and is still a working venue — opera and concerts in summer. Half an hour’s walk away the stadium that ran under the modern main street has been excavated at one end, so you can stand in a sunken marble horseshoe with shops above your head. Between them is the Old Town, which is a hill of enormous painted merchant houses from the 1840s and 1850s, several of them open as museums.

Spend a day at Rila Monastery

Two hours south of Sofia, up a forested valley, and worth the whole day rather than the coach stop most people give it. The courtyard with its striped arcades is the photograph; the church interior, the museum with Rafail’s wooden cross, and the walk up to the hermitage of Saint Ivan behind the monastery are the reasons to stay. Dress as you would for any working monastery — it is one, with monks in residence.

Ski a week for what a weekend costs in the Alps

Bansko is the biggest and has the most serious terrain, under Todorka and Vihren in the Pirin, with a gondola from the town. Borovets is the oldest, on the Rila an hour from Sofia. Pamporovo is the sunny, gentle one in the Rhodopes and the best place in the country to learn. None of them is the Alps for size or altitude, and the lift queues at Bansko in the February half-term weeks are genuinely bad; set against that, a week with lift pass, lessons and hotel costs a fraction of a French or Austrian equivalent.

Walk the Seven Rila Lakes

A chairlift from Panichishte and then a loop on foot past a chain of glacial lakes at around two thousand metres, with the option of climbing to the ridge above them. It is the best single day on foot in Bulgaria and it is busy on a fine summer weekend. The weather turns fast at that height, the last lift down matters, and the walk is on rock and loose path rather than a graded trail.

See the Varna gold

The Varna Archaeological Museum holds the Varna necropolis hoard — the oldest worked gold yet found anywhere, from graves dug in the fifth millennium BC. It is a small, old-fashioned museum and the gold is in a couple of rooms, which somehow makes it land harder. The city around it has a Roman bath complex, the largest in the country, and a sea garden that runs the length of the front.

Walk into Nessebar in the evening

The old town sits on a rock peninsula joined to the mainland by a narrow neck, and it is on the UNESCO list for its churches — a remarkable number of them for somewhere this size, several in ruins and all worth the wander — and its nineteenth-century wooden houses with overhanging upper floors. It is very busy by day in summer, with coaches from Sunny Beach next door. Go in the evening or stay the night and have it to yourself at seven in the morning.

Drive the Valley of the Roses in late May

The oil-bearing rose is picked by hand at first light in late May and early June, because the oil goes off as the day warms, and the fields between Kazanlak and Karlovo are the centre of a trade that supplies most of the world’s rose oil. There is a festival at Kazanlak in the first days of June with a rose-picking ritual that is for visitors but pleasant anyway. In the same valley: the painted Thracian tomb, the Shipka memorial church, and the pass over the Balkan range.

Find the Thracians

They are the layer underneath everything and they are easy to miss. Start with the gold in Sofia’s National Archaeological Museum and the Panagyurishte treasure, then the tombs: Kazanlak, painted and UNESCO-listed, and Sveshtari in the north-east with its stone caryatids. Then the open-air ones — Perperikon above Kardzhali, a city cut into the rock, and the sanctuary on the Belintash plateau. The Madara Rider, a horseman carved into a cliff face in the eighth century, belongs to the Bulgars who came later and is on the UNESCO list too.

When to go, and what it costs

Bulgaria keeps three calendars at once: a hot inland plain, two high mountain ranges and a short Black Sea summer. May, June and September are the best weeks almost everywhere — warm without the heat, the Plovdiv terraces full, the mountains open and the coast still cheap. July and August are hot, regularly above thirty-five degrees on the Thracian plain, and they are the only weeks the Black Sea really works; they are also when Sunny Beach and Golden Sands are at their busiest and dearest. October is lovely inland, with the Rhodopes turning and nobody about. January to March is the ski season at Bansko, Borovets and Pamporovo, with the February half-term weeks the busiest and the lift queues at Bansko worth planning around. The high mountain huts and the Rila lakes chairlift are summer operations; the Seven Rila Lakes is a July-to-September day, not a May one.

Fishing here

Bulgaria is better fishing country than its reputation, and it comes in three kinds. The mountain rivers and dam lakes of the Rila, the Pirin and the Rhodopes hold brown trout, and the big reservoirs — Iskar above Sofia, Dospat and Batak in the Rhodopes, Kardzhali on the Arda — are fished for carp, pike, perch and zander, with Dospat in particular known for its trout. The Danube along the northern border is big-river fishing for catfish, carp and zander, and the same rule applies here as on the Romanian bank: sturgeon are protected and anyone offering sturgeon fishing is offering something they should not. The Black Sea is fished from the ports for mackerel, bonito, bluefish and turbot, mostly from a boat out of Varna, Burgas or one of the smaller harbours. A licence is required for fresh water and is bought locally, seasons are dated and change, and many of the best dam-lake stretches are leased to local clubs, so this is a trip to arrange on the ground with someone who knows which water is open. Travelling in your later years

Sofia is flat; the old towns are not

Central Sofia is genuinely level walking on wide pavements, with the metro to cover the distances, and it is the easiest of the Bulgarian cities to get around. Plovdiv’s Old Town is the opposite: it is a hill, the lanes are steep and the surface is large round cobbles of the ankle-turning sort. The lower town around the Main Street is flat and has most of the hotels, so you can stay level and go up the hill once, by taxi to the top and downhill on foot.

Cobbles, and what they cost you

Plovdiv’s Old Town, Nessebar, Sozopol, Veliko Tarnovo’s old street and Arbanasi are all cobbled or stone-paved, and these are the original uneven sort rather than smoothed setts. They are hard on an unsteady ankle, slippery in the wet and awkward for a wheeled case. Flat shoes with a real sole make more difference than anything else, and in Nessebar the sensible move is to leave the car or coach outside and accept that the whole old town is a walking surface.

Think about the altitude as well as the walking

The Rila lakes sit at around two thousand metres and the chairlift takes you most of the way up, which means you can be at altitude within an hour of leaving a valley hotel. That is a real consideration for anyone with a heart or breathing condition, and the weather at that height changes faster than the forecast. Vitosha above Sofia, at a little under 2,300 metres, is the same point in miniature. The monasteries, the museums and the coast are all at low level.

Where the ground is difficult

Rila Monastery’s courtyard is level but the hermitage walk behind it is not; Tsarevets at Veliko Tarnovo is a long climb on a bare hill with no shade; Asen’s Fortress is a stair-climb to the church; the Rhodope caves involve wet steps and in one case a few hundred of them; and Perperikon is an unimproved rock site with no handrails. Against that, Plovdiv’s Roman theatre has a level approach from the top, the Varna museum and the Sofia museums are flat and lift-served, and the coastal promenades at Varna and Burgas are long, level and shaded. Pick deliberately rather than ruling the country out.

Getting in: what applies, and what does not

Bulgaria is a member of the European Union, which it joined in 2007, and since 1 January 2025 it has been a full member of the Schengen area — air and sea borders opened in March 2024 and the land borders followed on New Year’s Day 2025. That is a genuine change from the advice in older guidebooks: arriving from another Schengen country, Romania included, is now an internal journey with no routine passport check. The rules below are the ones that apply when you first enter the area, which for Bulgaria means arriving from Turkey, Serbia, North Macedonia or from outside Europe.

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen external border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave. United States, Canadian, British and Australian citizens may visit as tourists without a visa for up to ninety days in any one hundred and eighty, counted across the whole Schengen area rather than against Bulgaria alone — so a longer European trip uses the same allowance.

The money is the part worth knowing before you pack. Bulgaria adopted the euro on 1 January 2026 and is the twenty-first member of the euro area. The lev was converted at a rate fixed irrevocably at 1.95583 to the euro, both circulated side by side through January 2026, and the lev ceased to be legal tender on 1 February 2026. So there is no longer any currency to change, and a guidebook or website quoting prices in lev is describing the country before the changeover. If you have lev notes left from an earlier trip, Bulgarian banks and post offices exchanged them through the end of 2026 and the Bulgarian National Bank will exchange them indefinitely, free of charge.

Read the current advisory yourself at travel.state.gov before you go; the level and the wording change, and it is a two-minute check.

Getting here: Sofia carries most of the year-round international traffic and has a metro line from Terminal 2 into the city, which is the simplest airport arrival in the Balkans. Varna and Burgas take heavy summer charter traffic from across Europe and are the sensible way to the coast. Plovdiv has a small airport with a handful of routes. Trains and buses arrive from Romania, Serbia, North Macedonia, Greece and Turkey, and the Istanbul and Bucharest road crossings are busy. Flights, trains and coach timetables all change by season, so confirm them with the operator for your own dates rather than trusting a figure on any website, including this one — which is why we do not print one.

Bulgaria — questions

Does Bulgaria use the euro?

Yes, and this is the one thing most guidebooks still have wrong. Bulgaria adopted the euro on 1 January 2026, becoming the twenty-first member of the euro area. The old lev was converted at a rate fixed irrevocably at 1.95583 lev to the euro, both currencies circulated side by side through January 2026, and the lev stopped being legal tender on 1 February 2026. Prices in shops, restaurants and hotels are in euro. If you have lev notes left from an earlier trip they are not dead money: Bulgarian banks and post offices exchanged them through the end of 2026, and the Bulgarian National Bank will exchange them indefinitely and free of charge.

How many days do I need in Bulgaria?

A week does Sofia and Plovdiv properly with a day out to Rila Monastery, which is the trip most first visitors should take. Ten days adds either the Black Sea coast or Veliko Tarnovo and the Balkan range. A fortnight gets you the Rhodopes as well, which is the part people are most surprised by. A ski week at Bansko is a trip of its own. The country is compact by European standards but the mountain roads are slow, so plan around the ranges rather than across them.

Is Bulgaria expensive?

No. It is consistently among the least expensive countries in the European Union, and that did not change when the euro arrived — the conversion was at a fixed rate, not a repricing. Eating and drinking are the standout value, the ski resorts are the cheapest serious skiing in Europe, and the Black Sea in high summer and Bansko in the February half-term weeks are the only times you will feel a real squeeze. We do not print figures because they move.

Is the Cyrillic alphabet a problem?

Less than you would think, and it is worth half an hour before you go. Bulgaria is where the Cyrillic alphabet was developed, in the First Bulgarian Empire in the late ninth century, and the country brought it into the European Union as a third official alphabet in 2007. Motorway signs, railway stations, airports and the Sofia metro are dual-signed in Latin script. Away from those, street signs and small-town bus timetables are in Cyrillic only. Learning to sound out the fifteen or so letters that differ from Latin turns most signs into something readable, because a great many words are international ones spelled out.

Is Bulgaria in the Schengen area now?

Yes. Bulgaria became a full member of the Schengen area on 1 January 2025, at the same time as Romania, after its air and sea borders opened in March 2024. Arriving from another Schengen country is now an internal journey with no routine passport check, which a great many websites have not caught up with. The Entry/Exit System applies when you first enter the Schengen area from outside it.

Do I need a car?

Not for Sofia, Plovdiv, Varna or Burgas, and not for a ski week at Bansko, which is reachable by transfer and has a gondola from the town. For the Rhodopes, the Balkan villages, the Valley of the Roses and the small coastal towns a car changes the trip completely, because the good places sit along roads rather than rails. Driving is on the right, and a vignette is required to use the motorways — it is electronic, sold online and at border crossings and filling stations, and hire cars usually have one already, which is worth confirming at the desk.

Is Bulgaria safe?

For an ordinary visitor it is a normal European country and most trips pass without incident. The practical cautions are the city ones: pickpockets on crowded Sofia transport, and taxis. Taxi overcharging at Sofia airport and outside the big hotels is the single most common complaint from visitors, and the answer is simple — use the metro from the airport, or book through an app, or take a taxi from an official rank and look at the per-kilometre rate on the window before you get in. In the mountains the hazards are weather and distance rather than crime. What should I eat?

Shopska salad, the tomato, cucumber and white brine cheese dish that is on every table in summer. Banitsa, the cheese pastry that is breakfast. Tarator, a cold cucumber and yoghurt soup that is the right answer to a thirty-five degree afternoon. Kavarma, a slow clay-pot stew. Kebapche and kyufte off the grill, with lyutenitsa, the roasted pepper relish, on the side. Bulgarian yoghurt deserves its reputation — the bacterium that makes it is named after the country. Then rakia, the fruit brandy, which arrives before the meal rather than after, and the wine, which is the real surprise: Melnik in the south-west and the Thracian Valley around Plovdiv both make reds worth carrying home.
