Valletta

Where to Stay in Valletta

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Valletta is a city you can walk end to end in twenty minutes and still not finish in three days. The Knights of St John laid it out on a bare peninsula after the siege of 1565 as a grid on a ridge, which means almost every cross street runs downhill to water at both ends. The whole city is a UNESCO World Heritage site and it is small - about a thousand yards long - so the choice is not really which district but how high up the ridge you want to be, how much of a hill you are willing to walk, and whether you would rather sleep in the city itself or across the water and come in by ferry. It has also changed a great deal in ten years: townhouses that stood empty are now small hotels, and the city that used to empty at six in the evening now has somewhere to eat at nine.

Where to stay in Valletta, area by area

The parts of Valletta a visitor actually chooses between, and who each one suits.

The Guild does not name a hotel until an Explorer has confirmed it on the hotel’s own website, so this guide sets out the areas and sends you to the full, current list for each one rather than printing a shortlist we cannot stand behind yet. Named picks will be added to this page as they are checked.

Republic Street and the upper town

Most centralFlattest walkingBusiest by day

Republic Street runs the length of the ridge from the City Gate to Fort St Elmo, and the blocks either side of its upper half are the easiest part of Valletta to stay in. You are a few minutes from St John’s Co-Cathedral and its Caravaggios, the Grandmaster’s Palace, the Upper Barrakka Gardens and the bus terminus outside the gate, and crucially you are on the flat: the ridge streets run level while the cross streets drop away in steps. The trade is daytime noise and cruise-ship crowds between about ten and four. By early evening the day visitors have gone and it becomes a quiet city again.

The lower town towards Fort St Elmo

QuietestResidentialSteep cross streets

Past the halfway point the city thins out and becomes residential, with laundry across the alleys, corner grocers and small houses that have been in the same families for generations. Hotels here are mostly converted townhouses of a dozen rooms or fewer. It is quieter, usually cheaper, and a genuinely nicer place to come home to, but you are ten to fifteen minutes further from the gate and that walk is up a slope with steps in it. If you are arriving by taxi with luggage, check that a car can actually reach the door - several of these lanes are stepped and no vehicle gets in.

Strait Street and the old entertainment quarter

Restaurants and barsLively at nightNoisy rooms

The long narrow lane the British navy knew as the Gut was for decades the roughest street in the Mediterranean and then, for decades more, derelict. It is now the city’s eating and drinking street, narrow enough that tables run down the middle of it and the noise comes straight up the walls. Staying on or beside it puts you in the middle of Valletta’s evenings, which is either exactly what you want or exactly what you do not. Ask specifically for a room at the back or on an upper floor, and be honest with yourself about Friday and Saturday.

Floriana, just outside the gate

CheaperNear the bus hubShort walk in

Floriana is the suburb on the landward side of the fortifications, between the city gate and the rest of the island. It is a ten-minute level walk into Valletta, it has the main bus interchange and a great deal of parking, which Valletta itself has almost none of, and its rooms are usually cheaper than the same standard inside the walls. What it lacks is the thing people come to Valletta for: you do not step out of the door into the old city. For a driving trip, or an early flight, it is a sensible compromise.

The Three Cities across the Grand Harbour

Best views backVery quietFerry or taxi in

Birgu, Senglea and Cospicua sit on the far side of the Grand Harbour and are older than Valletta - Birgu was the Knights’ first base and the heart of the 1565 siege. The waterfront below Birgu is now a yacht marina with restaurants along it, and the view back at Valletta from there, floodlit at night, is the best in Malta. The ferry across takes a few minutes and runs frequently through the day; late at night you will want a taxi round the head of the harbour. Stay here for quiet, for the view and for a much more local evening, and accept that every trip into the capital is a short crossing.

Getting around

Valletta is walked, and the one thing to understand before you book is that the grid is built across a ridge: the long streets are level, the cross streets fall away in staircases. There are two lifts worth knowing - the barrakka lift from the Grand Harbour waterfront up to the Upper Barrakka Gardens, and the one at the Victoria Gate end - and both save a serious climb. No visitor should rent a car for a stay in Valletta: the city is pedestrianised in parts, controlled by camera elsewhere, and parking is close to impossible. The bus network reaches everywhere on the island and all of it radiates from the terminus outside the city gate, so a Valletta base is the easiest place on Malta to travel out from. Two ferries run from below the walls: one to Sliema in about five minutes and one to the Three Cities in about three. Both are cheap, frequent and far pleasanter than the bus around the creeks. Malta drives on the left and the airport is about twenty-five minutes away by road.

When to come, and what it costs

Valletta’s own best weeks are April, May, late September and October, when the stone is warm, the sea is swimmable and you can walk uphill at midday without suffering. July and August are hot - regularly in the mid thirties Celsius, with humidity that makes the evenings sticky - and the city takes the full weight of the cruise season, so the middle of the day belongs to tour groups. Winter is mild, about fifteen degrees, and genuinely quiet; it is also the wet season, and when rain comes to Valletta it comes hard and briefly and runs down the stepped streets. On price, the pattern is high summer and the Easter and Christmas weeks at the top, November to February at the bottom, with the two shoulder seasons in between and worth every euro of the difference.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Malta is as straightforward as Europe gets for an American or Canadian visitor. It has been in the European Union since 2004, in the Schengen area since 2007 and on the euro since 2008, so an arrival from another Schengen country has no passport control at all, and an arrival from outside it is a single check at Malta International Airport. There is no land border to cross. The ferry between Malta and Sicily is an internal Schengen crossing; the one to Tunisia is not, and is checked.

Staying in Valletta — questions

Should I stay in Valletta or in Sliema and St Julian’s?

Stay in Valletta if what you want is the old city, the art and quiet evenings in a place with very few chain hotels. Stay in Sliema or St Julian’s if you want a sea-front promenade, a bigger choice of modern rooms with pools and balconies, and more of a resort feel. The two sides are five minutes apart by ferry, so you are not choosing between the sights and the sea - only between which one you want to walk out into in the morning. Valletta is the better base for day trips by bus; Sliema has the better swimming.

Is Valletta hard work on foot?

In parts, yes, and it is worth knowing before you book rather than after. The streets that run the length of the ridge are level and easy. The ones that cross them drop steeply and many of them are literally staircases, which the Knights built shallow so that armoured men could climb them. Pick a hotel on or near one of the level streets, use the two public lifts between the waterfront and the city, and the place is manageable for most people. If stairs are genuinely difficult, say so when you book - several of the small townhouse hotels have no lift at all.

How long does Valletta itself need?

Two full days covers the city properly: St John’s Co-Cathedral and the two Caravaggios, the Grandmaster’s Palace and its armoury, the Upper and Lower Barrakka Gardens, the fortifications and Fort St Elmo, and time to wander the lower town. Most visitors give it an afternoon on the way through, which is a pity. Use Valletta as the base for the rest of the island and you need three or four nights; the whole country is within an hour of the bus terminus.

Do I need to book St John’s Co-Cathedral or the Hypogeum ahead?

The Hypogeum of Hal Saflieni, the underground neolithic burial complex at Paola, is the one that catches people out: visitor numbers are strictly limited to protect it and tickets routinely sell out weeks or months ahead, so book it before you book anything else. St John’s Co-Cathedral sells timed tickets online and you should take one in the busy months, but you can usually get in within a day or two. Both are closed or restricted on some Sundays and public holidays - check the dates for your week.

Is English really enough?

Yes, completely. English is an official language of Malta alongside Maltese, it is the language of government and much of the schooling, and effectively everyone you deal with in a hotel, a restaurant, a museum or on a bus speaks it fluently. Maltese is a Semitic language written in the Latin alphabet - the only one there is - and you will hear it everywhere, but nobody expects a visitor to have any of it. A gracious ’grazzi’ for thank you is pleasant and that is as far as anyone needs to go.
