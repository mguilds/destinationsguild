Eger

Where to Stay in Eger

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Eger is a small baroque town of about fifty thousand people in the hills two hours north-east of Budapest, and it is the easiest place in Hungary to spend two nights outside the capital. Everything is within a fifteen-minute walk: the castle that held off an Ottoman army in 1552, the main square with its enormous Minorite church, the northernmost Ottoman minaret left standing in Europe, a Turkish bath still in use, and a valley of wine cellars on the town’s western edge. Where you sleep here matters less than in a big city, with one real exception: how you plan to get home from the cellars.

The baroque centre — Dobó tér and the streets around it

Walk everywhereRestaurants and cafesSome weekend noise

The obvious choice and the right one for most visitors. Dobó István tér is the square the whole town turns around, with the Minorite church on one side and the castle gate a few minutes uphill; the pedestrian streets running off it hold most of the restaurants, the cafes, the basilica and the Lyceum with its painted library. Almost everything you have come to see is inside a ten-minute walk, which in Eger means the whole town. Rooms are mostly in converted townhouses and small family hotels, prices are low by any western European standard, and the one thing to check is a lift, because three floors and no lift is common here. There is some noise at weekends around the square, but this is not a party town and it is over by midnight.

Towards the castle and the quiet streets north

QuietestShort uphill walkViews over the roofs

The lanes climbing north and east from the square towards the castle walls, and the residential streets beyond them. This is where the small guesthouses and the handful of quieter hotels are, usually with a garden and often with a view down over the baroque roofs and the minaret. You are five to ten minutes’ walk from everything, the nights are silent, and the rooms tend to be larger and cheaper than on the square itself. The cost is the gradient, which is modest but real, and the cobbles, which are not. If steps and uneven stone are a concern, ask exactly where the entrance is before booking rather than after.

The Szépasszony-völgy — out among the wine cellars

Wine on the doorstepNo walk homeLittle else nearby

The Valley of the Beautiful Women lies about twenty minutes’ walk west of the centre, or a very short taxi ride: a horseshoe of hillside with dozens of small cellars cut into the rock, where the growers pour their own wine, including the Egri Bikavér that the English-speaking world calls Bull’s Blood. A few guesthouses and small hotels sit around the valley itself. The case for staying here is simple and good: you can taste properly and walk to bed. The case against is just as simple — there is very little else out here, you will want a taxi into town for anything that is not wine, and out of season a number of the cellars keep short hours or open by arrangement only.

Out of town — the spa hotels towards Egerszalók

Thermal waterFamily roomsCar or bus needed

Six kilometres south-west of Eger, Egerszalók has a well-known thermal complex built around a white limestone hill where the hot spring has deposited its minerals over the centuries, and the larger spa hotels of the area are there rather than in Eger itself. Rooms are modern, with lifts, parking and the kind of family and half-board arrangements the town centre does not offer, and the bathing is the point. It suits a longer, slower stay, a family, or anyone for whom cobbles and stairs are a problem. You will need the bus or a car to reach Eger’s own sights, which is the whole trade.

Getting around

Eger is small enough that the answer is almost always your feet. The castle, the square, the basilica, the minaret and the Turkish bath are all within a few hundred metres of one another, and the streets in the middle are largely pedestrian. The surfaces are cobbled and the town sits on a slope, so the walking is easy but not flat.

The railway station is about a kilometre south of the centre, a fifteen-minute walk or a short local bus ride, and the bus station is closer in. Trains from Budapest Keleti take roughly two hours, some direct and some with one easy change, and intercity buses do the same journey in a similar time. Neither needs booking far ahead outside holiday weekends.

For the Szépasszony-völgy, walk out in the afternoon and take a taxi back; it is a twenty-minute walk and the fares are small. For Egerszalók and the villages, local buses run through the day but thin out in the evening, so check the last one before you settle in. A car is useful if you mean to tour the wine region or go on into the hills towards Tokaj, and a nuisance in the centre, where parking is paid and limited.

When to come, and what it costs

September and early October are the best weeks to be in Eger, because the harvest is on and the cellars are at their busiest and most welcoming, with the weather usually still warm. Late May and June are the other good stretch: green, long days and few people. July and August are hot and the town is busy with Hungarian holidaymakers, though it never feels crowded the way Budapest does. Winter is cold and very quiet, which has its own appeal with the thermal bath and a cellar fire, but a number of the valley cellars close or keep short hours and the castle grounds are bleak.

Eger is inexpensive. Rooms in the centre cost a fraction of Budapest, the restaurants are good value and a tasting in the valley costs very little. The exceptions are the harvest weekends and the national holidays in March, May and August, when the town fills with weekenders from Budapest and prices rise; book ahead for those. Hungary uses the Hungarian forint (HUF, written Ft), not the euro. Hungary has been in the European Union since 2004 but has kept its own currency and has set no date for changing it, so euro notes are not legal tender. A few hotels and tour desks quote in euros and some will take euro cash, almost always at a poor rate. Pay in forint. Cards and phones are taken nearly everywhere, including on buses, trams and at ticket machines, and you need very little cash — a small amount is handy for market stalls, thermal-bath lockers, church donation boxes and rural buses. Prices in shops include tax. In Budapest restaurants a service charge is often printed on the bill; check before adding a tip, and where there is none, ten per cent is plenty. Decline any card machine offering to charge you in dollars and let your own bank convert. Avoid the standalone exchange booths near the main tourist streets and at the airport, which are far worse than a bank machine or an ordinary exchange office in town.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS — the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years — is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today — nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Hungary is a straightforward case. It has been in the European Union since 2004 and in the Schengen area since the end of 2007, so everything above applies here in full, and flying in from Vienna, Munich or Amsterdam involves no passport control at all. What Hungary has not joined is the euro, so the currency is the forint. Its borders with Austria, Slovakia, Slovenia, Croatia and Romania are all internal Schengen crossings now and you will usually drive or ride straight through them. The borders with Serbia and with Ukraine are external Schengen frontiers, where there are proper checks and the queues can be long. None of that affects an ordinary trip to the places in this guide.

Staying in Eger — questions

Is Eger worth an overnight stay, or just a day trip?

Both work, but a night is much better. A day trip from Budapest gives you the castle, the square and the basilica and leaves no time for the thing most people come for, which is an evening among the cellars in the Szepasszony valley. Stay one night and you can taste without watching the clock or the last train. Two nights lets you add the Turkish bath, the Lyceum library and a morning in the hills.

What is the Valley of the Beautiful Women?

A horseshoe of hillside about twenty minutes walk west of the centre, where dozens of small cellars are cut into the rock and the growers sell their own wine from the barrel. It is informal rather than polished: you walk from door to door, pay very little, and most places will pour you a small measure to try. Egri Bikaver, the red blend known abroad as Bull's Blood, is the local name, but the whites and the Egri Csillag blend are often the better buy. Take cash, eat something first, and plan a taxi home.

Where is the castle, and how long does it take?

Eger castle sits on the rock immediately above the main square, five minutes walk uphill from Dobo ter. Allow two hours for the walls, the casemates and the small museums, longer if you take the underground tour of the gun galleries. The 1552 siege, in which a small garrison held out against a far larger Ottoman army, is the story every Hungarian schoolchild knows, and the castle is told around it.

Can you swim in Eger itself?

Yes. Eger has a bath complex in the town park with thermal and swimming pools, and a small Ottoman-era Turkish bath beside it that has been in use in some form since the seventeenth century. It is a real working local bath rather than a showpiece, which is much of its charm. Take a costume, flip-flops and a towel, and check the day's opening hours, which vary between the pools.

How do I get to Eger from Budapest?

Trains leave from Budapest Keleti and take about two hours, some direct and some with a straightforward change; intercity buses take a similar time and run frequently. Fares are low and neither normally needs booking far in advance outside holiday weekends. The station is about fifteen minutes walk south of the centre, or a short local bus ride.

Do I need a car?

Not for Eger itself, which is walkable end to end. A car earns its keep only if you mean to tour the wine villages, visit Egerszalok under your own steam or drive on east towards Tokaj. In the centre it is a liability, with paid and limited parking and several pedestrian streets.

Is Eger good for older or less mobile travellers?

The town is small and the distances are short, which helps a great deal, but the centre is cobbled and sits on a slope, and many of the townhouse hotels have three floors and no lift. Ask about lifts and the entrance before booking. The castle involves a climb and uneven ground. If stairs and cobbles are a real problem, the modern spa hotels out towards Egerszalok are the easier base.

How many days do you need?

One night is enough to see the town and spend an evening in the valley. Two nights is the comfortable answer and lets you add the bath, the Lyceum and a slower morning. Three if you want to use Eger as a base for the northern hills or the wine villages around it.
