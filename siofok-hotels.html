Siófok

Where to Stay in Siófok

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Siófok is the busiest town on Lake Balaton and the one most visitors mean when they say they are going to the lake. It sits on the flat south shore about an hour and a half from Budapest, split in two by the Sió canal that drains Balaton into the Danube, with the harbour and the water tower in the middle. The water here is shallow for a very long way out, which is why Hungarian families have come for a century and why it is warm enough to swim in from June to September. It is also the loudest place on the lake in July and August, and that is the whole of the decision: how close to the strip you want to be.

Where to stay in Siófok, area by area

Four parts of a resort town that changes character every few hundred metres along the shore.

The Aranypart — the Gold Coast and Petőfi sétány

The main beachBars and clubsLoud in high summer

East of the canal, the long shore road and promenade that is Siófok’s front. This is the stretch with the big beaches, the water slides, the pedalos, the ice-cream and lángos stands and, at its western end, the large open-air clubs that give the town its reputation. In June and September it is a pleasant family promenade. In July and August the western end of it is Hungary’s loudest summer address, and a room above the strip will not be quiet before three in the morning. The further east you go the calmer it becomes, and the hotels at the far end are a different proposition entirely. If you book on the Gold Coast, look at exactly how far from the clubs you are before you commit.

The Ezüstpart — the Silver Coast, west of the canal

QuieterFamiliesShort bus or walk into town

The western shore, on the far side of the Sió canal, and the answer for most people who want the lake rather than the party. The beaches are the same shallow warm water, the promenade is greener and far calmer, and the hotels are mostly family places with gardens, often half-board, and a good deal cheaper than the equivalent on the Gold Coast. It is fifteen to twenty minutes’ walk or a short bus ride into the town centre and the harbour. The trade-off is simply that there is less immediately outside the door — fewer restaurants, less going on in the evening — which is exactly what people choose it for.

The town centre — the harbour, the water tower and the market

Ferries at the doorShops and restaurantsTwo minutes to the beach

The middle of Siófok, around the old water tower on the main square, the market and the harbour where the Balaton ferries leave for Balatonfüred and Tihany on the far shore. This is the practical part of town: the supermarkets, the chemist, the bus and railway stations, the year-round restaurants and the boat timetable, with the beach a couple of minutes away on either side. Rooms are a mix of older hotels and apartments, usually the best value in Siófok, and you are within walking distance of both shores. It is busy in the daytime in season and reasonably quiet at night, being a few hundred metres back from the clubs.

Back from the lake — the streets inland and towards Sóstó

Cheapest roomsParkingTen minutes to the water

The grid of quiet residential streets running inland from the shore road, and the area around the small Sóstó lake behind the town. Guesthouses and apartments here cost markedly less than anything on the water, almost always come with somewhere to park, and are a flat eight to fifteen minutes’ walk from a beach. For a family staying a week, or for anyone who intends to drive around the lake, this is the sensible choice and it is where a good many Hungarians actually stay. What you give up is the view and the ability to walk back from dinner in a swimming costume.

Getting around

Siófok is flat and strung out along the shore, so walking works within each part of town and gets tiring between them — the Gold Coast promenade alone runs for kilometres. Bicycles are the local answer, and the Balaton cycle path that circles the whole lake passes through the town; hire shops are easy to find in season. Local buses run along the shore road and over the canal, and taxis are cheap.

Trains from Budapest-Déli and Kelenföld take about an hour and a half on the fast services and run along the whole south shore, which makes hopping between the lake towns simple and cheap. The station is in the middle of Siófok, a few minutes from the harbour. Buses from Budapest take a similar time.

The Balaton ferries are the pleasure here rather than the necessity: the car ferry and the passenger boats cross from Siófok harbour to Balatonfüred and Tihany on the north shore in well under an hour, and the service runs from roughly the spring to the early autumn with a reduced timetable at either end. A car is genuinely useful if you mean to tour the north shore wine villages or reach Hévíz and Keszthely at the far end of the lake; in Siófok itself in August it is mostly a parking problem.

When to come, and what it costs

Siófok has a season and it is short. Mid June to early September is when the lake is warm enough to swim, the ferries run properly and the town is open. July and August are the peak: hot, crowded, expensive by Hungarian standards and very loud at the western end of the Gold Coast. Late May, early June and the second half of September are the sweet spots, with the town open, the prices lower and the beaches civilised, though the water at either end of that range may be colder than you would like. From October to April a great deal of Siófok is simply shut, and the shore is beautiful, grey and empty.

On price, the lake is the one part of Hungary that is not cheap in high season. A room on the Gold Coast in late July can cost more than the equivalent in Budapest, and the whole south shore books out months ahead for the August holidays and the big festival weekends. Shift a fortnight either side of that and the same room falls sharply. The Silver Coast and the inland streets are always better value. Hungary uses the Hungarian forint (HUF, written Ft), not the euro. Hungary has been in the European Union since 2004 but has kept its own currency and has set no date for changing it, so euro notes are not legal tender. A few hotels and tour desks quote in euros and some will take euro cash, almost always at a poor rate. Pay in forint. Cards and phones are taken nearly everywhere, including on buses, trams and at ticket machines, and you need very little cash — a small amount is handy for market stalls, thermal-bath lockers, church donation boxes and rural buses. Prices in shops include tax. In Budapest restaurants a service charge is often printed on the bill; check before adding a tip, and where there is none, ten per cent is plenty. Decline any card machine offering to charge you in dollars and let your own bank convert. Avoid the standalone exchange booths near the main tourist streets and at the airport, which are far worse than a bank machine or an ordinary exchange office in town.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS — the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years — is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today — nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Hungary is a straightforward case. It has been in the European Union since 2004 and in the Schengen area since the end of 2007, so everything above applies here in full, and flying in from Vienna, Munich or Amsterdam involves no passport control at all. What Hungary has not joined is the euro, so the currency is the forint. Its borders with Austria, Slovakia, Slovenia, Croatia and Romania are all internal Schengen crossings now and you will usually drive or ride straight through them. The borders with Serbia and with Ukraine are external Schengen frontiers, where there are proper checks and the queues can be long. None of that affects an ordinary trip to the places in this guide.

Staying in Siófok — questions

Is Siofok too noisy for a family holiday?

It depends entirely on where you book. The western end of the Gold Coast promenade holds the big open-air clubs and in July and August it is genuinely loud until the early hours. The eastern end of the same shore, the Silver Coast across the canal and the quiet streets inland are all ordinary family resort, and Hungarian families have been holidaying on exactly those streets for generations. Look at the map and the distance to the clubs before you book, not the town name.

When is Lake Balaton warm enough to swim?

Roughly the middle of June to the middle of September. The lake is shallow - much of the south shore stays waist-deep a long way out - so it warms quickly in early summer and can feel like bathwater in late July and August. It also cools quickly, so late September swimming is for the hardy, and from October to May it is cold.

Gold Coast or Silver Coast?

The Gold Coast, east of the canal, if you want the main beaches, the promenade life and plenty of places to eat, and you either want the nightlife or will book well away from it. The Silver Coast, west of the canal, if you want the same water with far less noise, more greenery and lower prices, and do not mind a short walk or bus ride into town. Families who have been before mostly choose the Silver Coast or the quiet inland streets.

Can you get to the north shore from Siofok?

Yes, and it is the best day out here. Passenger boats and a car ferry cross from Siofok harbour to Balatonfured and the Tihany peninsula, taking well under an hour, with a full timetable in summer and a reduced one in spring and autumn. Tihany's abbey on its headland and the Badacsony wine hills are the two things worth crossing for. Check the last boat back before you settle into lunch.

How long is the train from Budapest?

About an hour and a half on the fast services from Budapest-Deli or Kelenfold, and the line runs along the whole south shore, so moving between the lake towns afterwards is simple. Trains are busy on summer Friday evenings and Sunday afternoons, when half of Budapest is going the same way, so travel midweek if you can or reserve a seat.

Is there anything to do in Siofok if it rains?

Not a great deal, which is the honest answer. There is a thermal and wellness complex, the galleries and the market in town, and the Sio canal lock to walk out to, but Siofok is a beach resort and a wet week here is a long one. The usual rainy-day answers are the thermal baths, a train along the shore or the drive round to the museums at Keszthely and the thermal lake at Heviz.

Do I need a car at Lake Balaton?

Not for Siofok itself, which has a station in the middle of town, buses along the shore and bicycles for everything else. A car is worth having if you mean to tour the north shore wine villages, reach the small towns between the resorts, or go on to Heviz and Keszthely at the western end. In August, parking near the beaches is the price you pay for it.

How many days do you need?

For the beach, as many as you want - this is a week-long holiday town rather than a sightseeing one. For a traveller touring Hungary, two nights is enough to swim, walk the promenade, take the ferry across to Tihany and see what the Hungarian summer is actually like. Outside the swimming season, half a day is plenty.
