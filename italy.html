Why visit Italy

Few countries pack this much history, art, landscape and sheer pleasure into a single trip — or make it feel this effortless.

Layers of history

The Colosseum and the Forum, Pompeii under its volcano, medieval hill towns and the whole of the Renaissance — three thousand years, all still standing.

Art at every turn

Michelangelo's David and the Sistine Chapel, Botticelli in the Uffizi, mosaics in Ravenna — Italy holds more of the world's great art than anywhere else.

The food and wine

Every region cooks its own way: Roman carbonara, Neapolitan pizza, Tuscan bistecca, Bolognese ragù — paired with Chianti, Barolo and Prosecco.

Coast and countryside

The Amalfi Coast and Cinque Terre, the Tuscan vineyards, the northern lakes and the peaks of the Dolomites — beauty in every direction.

How the country divides up

Rome & the centre

Almost every trip begins in Rome, and it should. The Colosseum and the Forum by morning, St Peter's and the Vatican Museums, coins in the Trevi Fountain and carbonara after dark — the city layers three millennia into a few walkable days. East and north lie the green hills of Umbria, where Assisi keeps the basilica of St Francis and Perugia crowns its ridge above the valley.

Florence, Tuscany & the north-centre

Florence is the cradle of the Renaissance — the Uffizi, the Duomo, Michelangelo's David — and the perfect base for Tuscany, from the towers of Siena and the leaning campanile of Pisa to Chianti's vineyards. On the plain to the north, Bologna is Italy's food capital, all porticoed streets and ragù. Trains link them in under an hour.

Venice & the north

Venice is unlike anywhere on earth — a city of canals, gondolas and Byzantine gold, with no cars and no equal, best savoured over a night or two. West lies Milan, the fashion and design capital and home of Leonardo's Last Supper; Verona keeps its Roman arena and Juliet's balcony; and the mirror-calm shores of Lake Como and the Dolomites rise beyond.

Naples, the Amalfi Coast & the south

The south runs on a hotter, louder rhythm. Naples is raw, thrilling and the birthplace of pizza, with Pompeii and Vesuvius on its doorstep. From Sorrento you reach the cliff-clinging villages of the Amalfi Coast and the island of Capri. Further out, Palermo opens up Sicily — Greek temples, baroque towns and Arab-Norman markets.

Top things to do in Italy

The experiences worth building a trip around.

Ancient Rome

The Colosseum, the Roman Forum and Palatine Hill, then the Pantheon and a coin in the Trevi Fountain. Book timed tickets and go early.

The Vatican

St Peter's Basilica and the Vatican Museums, ending in the Sistine Chapel under Michelangelo's ceiling. One of the great sights on earth.

Renaissance Florence

Botticelli and Leonardo in the Uffizi, the David at the Accademia, and the view over the terracotta rooftops from the Duomo's dome.

Get lost in Venice

St Mark's Basilica and the Grand Canal, a gondola or the cheaper vaporetto, and the quiet back-lanes once the day crowds leave.

The Amalfi Coast

Cliffside Positano and Ravello, a boat to Capri, and lemon groves above an impossibly blue sea — one of the world's great coastlines.

Eat your way through

Pizza in Naples, ragù in Bologna, bistecca in Florence, gelato everywhere — every region cooks its own way, paired with its own wine.

Best time to visit & getting there

When to go

April–June and September–October are the sweet spots: warm, bright and just before or after the peak crush, ideal for cities, coast and countryside alike.

July–August is hot and busy, and many city businesses close in mid-August for Ferragosto — though the beaches and lakes are in full swing.

November–March is low season: cooler, quieter and cheaper, perfect for museums, food and Christmas markets, with the Alps and Dolomites open for skiing.

Getting there

Rome Fiumicino (FCO) and Milan Malpensa (MXP) are the two main long-haul gateways, with direct flights from across the world.

Venice (VCE), Naples (NAP), Bologna (BLQ) and Pisa (PSA) also take international flights, and Italy's fast trains link the major cities so smoothly that many visitors skip domestic flying altogether.

The currency is the euro. Cards work almost everywhere; carry a little cash for small cafés and rural spots. Always check your current visa and passport-validity requirements before booking.

Italy travel FAQ

When is the best time to visit Italy?

April to June and September to October are the sweet spots — warm, bright and just before or after the peak crush. July and August are hot and crowded, and many city businesses close in mid-August for Ferragosto, though the coast and lakes are in full swing. November to March is low season: cool, quieter and cheaper, ideal for the cities, museums and food, with the Alps open for skiing.

What currency is used in Italy?

The euro (EUR, symbol €). Cards, including contactless and phone payments, are accepted almost everywhere — hotels, restaurants, shops and even many market stalls. Carry a little cash for small cafés, tips and rural spots. ATMs (bancomat) are widespread and usually the best way to get euros, though watch for machines that offer to charge you in your home currency, which costs more.

Rome, Florence or Venice — which should I choose?

Rome is the grand all-rounder: ancient ruins, the Vatican, great food and enough to fill many days. Florence is compact and made for art and Renaissance architecture, and the perfect base for Tuscany's hill towns and vineyards. Venice is unlike anywhere else on earth — canals, palazzi and no cars — magical but small, and best savoured over a night or two. On a first trip most travellers link all three by fast train, which connects them in a few hours.

How many days do you need in Italy?

For a first trip, ten to fourteen days is ideal: a few days each in Rome, Florence and Venice, linked by high-speed train, with time added for Tuscany, the Amalfi Coast or the lakes. With only a week, pick two cities rather than trying to see the whole country — Rome and Florence, or Venice and Florence, make a natural pair.

Is Italy safe for travellers?

Italy is one of the most-visited countries in the world and is generally very safe for travellers. The main thing to guard against is petty theft — pickpockets and bag-snatchers work crowded stations, buses and tourist sights in the big cities, so keep valuables secure. Use licensed taxis or official apps, validate train tickets where required, and check your government's current travel advisory before you go.
