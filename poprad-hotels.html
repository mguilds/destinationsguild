The High Tatras

Where to Stay in Poprad

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Poprad is not the reason you come here; it is the door. The town sits at about 670 metres on the open plain directly below the High Tatras, and the whole range stands up out of the fields in front of it like a wall — which is the view that sells the place. The Tatras are the smallest genuinely alpine range in the world: about twenty-five kilometres end to end, rising to Gerlachovsky stit at 2,655 metres, with granite walls, glacial tarns, marked ridge routes and huts you walk between, all compressed into a space you can see from one viewpoint. Poprad has the airport, the railway station, the supermarkets, the thermal park and the cheapest beds; the resort villages strung along the mountain foot — Tatranska Lomnica, Stary Smokovec, Strbske Pleso — have the trailheads and the cableways. The narrow-gauge electric railway links the lot, which means you can do this whole trip without a car.

Where to stay in Poprad, area by area

The parts of Poprad a visitor actually chooses between, and who each one suits.

Poprad town, and old Spisska Sobota

CheapestSupermarketsRailway hub

The practical base. Poprad has the range of hotels, the shops, the restaurants that are open in November, the thermal water park at AquaCity next to the station, and the lowest prices within reach of the mountains — usually a good deal below the resort villages for the same standard of room. It is also where the trains and the airport are. The town centre itself is ordinary, but Spisska Sobota, a separate medieval quarter on its northern edge, is a genuinely handsome walled square of burgher houses with a church full of Master Pavol carving, and has the best small hotels in the area. Stay in Poprad if you want value, shops and an easy arrival, and take the railway up the hill each morning.

Tatranska Lomnica

The big cablewaySki and walkTrees

The eastern resort village, under the pyramid of Lomnicky stit, and the one we would pick for a first Tatra stay. It has the cableway to the 2,634-metre summit of Lomnicky stit, in two stages via Skalnate pleso, which is the easiest way anywhere in Europe to stand on a serious alpine summit; it has the Tatra National Park museum and a botanical garden; it has the ski area on the same lifts in winter; and it is a wooded, slightly old-fashioned place of large timber-and-stone hotels under the pines rather than a modern resort. Trailheads for the eastern valleys start from here or from Skalnate pleso.

Stary Smokovec and the middle of the range

FunicularMost centralGood transport

The old centre of Tatra tourism, halfway along the mountain foot, where the roads from Poprad and from the other villages meet and where the funicular climbs to Hrebienok at about 1,280 metres, right at the tree line and the start of several of the best valley walks. It is the most connected of the villages — the Tatra railway, the buses and the road all pass through — and it has the grand old Grand Hotel at the top of the slope and a scatter of smaller places around it. Pick it if you want to be in the middle and move about easily rather than be committed to one end of the range.

Strbske Pleso, up at the lake

HighestLakesideDearest

A village at about 1,350 metres built around its own glacial lake, with the peaks standing straight out of the far shore. It is the highest of the resorts, the one that holds snow longest, and the most expensive. The lakeside circuit is level, surfaced and short, which makes it the single best piece of easy walking in the Tatras; from the same spot, marked trails climb to the Popradske pleso and up towards Rysy on the Polish border. There is a chairlift and a small ski area, a ski-jumping complex left from the 1970 world championships, and a rack railway down to the main line. It is a cold, bright, thin-aired place and in high summer it is busy from mid-morning.

Down in the Spis, for castles rather than peaks

CastlesQuieterCar useful

If the mountains are half of your plan and the Spis basin is the other half, consider sleeping below the range instead. Levoca, half an hour east of Poprad, is a walled UNESCO town with a complete Renaissance square and the tallest Gothic wooden altar in the world in its church; Spis Castle is fifteen minutes further on; Kezmarok, with one of the eight UNESCO wooden churches, is twenty minutes north. Prices are lower again than Poprad, the towns are more interesting in the evening than a resort village, and the mountains are still forty minutes away. A car makes this version of the trip work.

Getting around Poprad

The Tatra Electric Railway

The thing that makes a car optional. A narrow-gauge electric line — the TEZ, locally the tatranska elektricka — runs from Poprad station along the foot of the range through Stary Smokovec to Strbske Pleso, with a branch to Tatranska Lomnica and a rack section on the western climb. End to end takes about an hour, it costs very little, it runs all year and roughly hourly or better, and it stops at every village and several trailheads. Buy tickets at the station machines or on board. It is also simply a good ride, with the wall of the Tatras alongside the whole way.

The cableways, funiculars and chairlifts

These are the trip for a great many visitors and they need booking. The Lomnicky stit cableway from Tatranska Lomnica runs in two stages: a large car to Skalnate pleso, then a small one to the summit at 2,634 metres, where tickets are timed, limited in number and sell out in summer and at half-term — book online ahead rather than turning up. The funicular from Stary Smokovec to Hrebienok is cheap, frequent and needs no booking. There are chairlifts at Strbske Pleso and at Tatranska Lomnica for the ski area. All of them close in high wind, which is common on the summit section, and all take a maintenance break in spring and autumn — check before you plan a day around one.

Walking: the season and the closure you must know about

The highest trails in Tatra National Park are closed by law from 1 November to 15 June to protect wildlife, so the walking year is the second half of June to the end of October whatever the valley weather is doing. Within that window the trails are well marked and colour-coded, the huts are open and serve food, and the valley walks from Hrebienok and Strbske Pleso are straightforward. Above the tree line the ground is granite, exposed and serious: afternoon thunderstorms are the normal summer pattern, so start at first light and plan to be down by early afternoon. Some ridge routes require a licensed mountain guide, and the summit of Gerlachovsky stit may only be climbed with one unless you belong to a national UIAA club.

Poprad Airport, and getting here

Poprad-Tatry is the highest airport in central Europe and has a very small timetable, mostly seasonal charter, so most visitors arrive another way. The train from Bratislava takes around four hours; from Kosice about an hour and a quarter, which is the useful one. Buses run from Krakow over the Polish border in about four hours. By car the D1 motorway reaches Poprad from both directions but is not continuous the whole length of the country, so allow more time than the distance suggests. A motorway vignette is required.

Day trips from the mountain foot

Within an hour: Spis Castle and Levoca, Kezmarok and its wooden church, the Dobsinska Ice Cave, and the Slovak Paradise gorges for the ladder routes. A little further: the Pieniny and the Dunajec gorge, where wooden rafts are poled down the river along the Polish border, and the Belianska Cave at the eastern end of the range. The Polish side of the Tatras at Zakopane is about two hours by road over the border, and because both countries are in Schengen there is no formality in crossing.

When to come

This is the part of Slovakia where the month decides the trip. Late June to mid-October is the walking season, because the highest trails are closed by law until 15 June and the huts and lifts work to roughly the same window; September is the best of it, with settled weather, clear air, the huts still open and the August crowds gone. July and August are the busy, warm, thunderstorm-in-the-afternoon weeks and the one time the Tatra villages genuinely sell out. Mid-December to late March is the ski season, with the February school holidays the dearest fortnight. May and late October into November are the dead spells: the trails shut, the lifts in maintenance, the villages half closed and the prices at their lowest. The cableways still run in most of that time, so a look at the mountains is always available even when the walking is not.

A note on the altitude for anyone who would rather ride than walk: the summit station of Lomnicky stit is at 2,634 metres and the cableway gets you there in minutes from a valley at under 900. That is a fast gain, the air at the top is thin and cold in any season, and there is no gradual acclimatisation. If you have a heart or breathing condition, the middle station at Skalnate pleso or the funicular to Hrebienok gives most of the view with far less of the effect, and nobody minds if you stop there.

Slovakia has used the euro since 1 January 2009 — it was the first of the central European countries to adopt it and the second post-communist state after Slovenia, so there is no Slovak koruna to change and anything quoting one is describing the country before 2009. Cards are taken almost everywhere a visitor goes and contactless is normal on city transport; keep a little cash for a mountain hut, a village pub, a chairlift ticket office in the smaller valleys and a church collection box.

Getting in: what applies, and what does not

Slovakia is in the European Union, in the Schengen area since 21 December 2007 and in the euro since 1 January 2009, so arriving from Austria, Czechia, Poland or Hungary is an internal journey with no routine passport check and nothing to change. The rules below apply when you first enter the Schengen area from outside it.

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen external border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave. United States, Canadian, British and Australian citizens may visit as tourists without a visa for up to ninety days in any one hundred and eighty, counted across the whole Schengen area rather than against Slovakia alone.

We could not read the United States travel advisory for Slovakia on the day this page was written — the State Department’s site would not answer us — so we are not printing a level here rather than quoting one from memory. Read the current advisory yourself at travel.state.gov before you go; the level and the wording change, and it is a two-minute check.

Poprad — questions

Should I stay in Poprad or up in the mountain villages?

Poprad for value, shops, restaurants that stay open out of season, the thermal park and an easy arrival; the villages for being at the trailhead when the weather window opens at six in the morning. The railway takes about twenty to forty minutes between them and runs all year, so Poprad is a perfectly good base for walking — you simply start a little later. Our recommendation: first Tatra visit, three or four nights, take Tatranska Lomnica for the cableway and the trees; longer trip or tighter budget, take Poprad or Spisska Sobota and use the train.

When can I actually walk in the High Tatras?

From 15 June to 31 October, because the highest trails in the national park are closed by law outside those dates to protect wildlife, and the huts and chairlifts work to roughly the same season. A May trip to the Tatras is a cableway-and-valley trip, not a walking one, whatever the weather is doing in the town. Within the season, September is the best month and the second half of June is the freshest. Afternoon thunderstorms are routine in July and August, so start early.

Do I need to book the Lomnicky stit cableway?

Yes, for the top section, and in advance. Tickets to the summit are timed and the number sold each day is limited, and they sell out in summer, at weekends and during the February and Easter holidays. Book online before you travel. The lower section to Skalnate pleso is easier to get on. Both close in high wind, which is common up there, so have a second plan for the day — and if your summit slot is cancelled for weather you have not lost the trip, because Skalnate pleso and Hrebienok are both worth the ride on their own.

Can I climb Gerlachovsky stit, the highest peak?

Only with a licensed Slovak mountain guide, unless you are a member of a national UIAA-affiliated club. That is the law in Tatra National Park, not a commercial arrangement: there is no marked trail to the summit, the usual routes up the Velicka proba and down the Batizovska proba are chained scrambles with exposed sections and awkward route-finding on the ridge, and the ascent is around a thousand metres from Sliezsky dom or over 1,600 from the valley. Guides are readily hired in the villages. It is a serious day and it is not a walk.

Is there anything here for someone who does not want to hike?

A great deal, and this is one of the Tatras’ real strengths. The cableways and the funicular put you in high mountain country with no climb at all. The lakeside circuit at Strbske Pleso is level and surfaced. The Tatra railway is a good ride in itself. There are thermal pools at AquaCity in Poprad and at Besenova further west, caves at Belianska and Dobsinska, the Tatra National Park museum at Tatranska Lomnica, and the castles and walled towns of the Spis half an hour down on the plain. A week here without a serious walk is an easy week to fill.

Do I need a car?

No, if your trip is the mountain villages and the lifts — the Tatra Electric Railway and the local buses cover all of that and the parking in the villages is paid and tight in season. Yes, if you want the Spis castles, the wooden churches, the Slovak Paradise and the caves without building the day around bus timetables. A common compromise is to base in a village without a car and hire one for a day or two, or to take a guided day out to the Spis.

What about bears?

The Tatras have bears, though the central ranges further west — the Low Tatras, the Velka Fatra and the hills around Banska Bystrica — are the heart of the bear country rather than the high bare granite of the Tatra peaks. Slovakia put its brown bear population above 1,300 animals in 2025 and there have been serious attacks in the country, including a fatal one in central Slovakia in April 2025. Hundreds of thousands of people walk here every year without seeing one. The rules are simple and worth following: stay on the marked trails, make noise on blind corners and in berry scrub, do not walk alone at first or last light, never leave food or rubbish at a hut or in a car park, never approach a cub, and call 112 if you have a problem.

Are the mountain huts worth staying in?

Yes, and they are part of what makes this range feel bigger than it is. Several are reachable on a marked trail in a few hours from a village, they serve hot food and beer, and they let you start a high day from altitude rather than from the valley — which, given that you want to be off the ridge before the afternoon storms, is the difference between a comfortable day and a rushed one. They are basic, they are busy in season, and they must be booked ahead; bring a sleeping liner, cash and a torch.
