Where to Stay in Jaipur

Where to Stay in Jaipur — Best Hotels & Areas

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated August 2026

Jaipur is the one Indian city where a palace hotel is not a marketing conceit. Several of them really were royal houses, with documented lineage and rooms the family actually lived in, and staying inside one is a large part of why people come. The practical question is which quarter. Civil Lines and C-Scheme — flat, leafy, colonial-era streets with pavements that genuinely exist — is the right answer for most visitors, and is where we would send anyone over about fifty-five. Bhawani Singh Road, just south, is the same virtues with more garden. The Pink City itself is glorious to walk in the morning and hard work to sleep in. The Amer road resorts are handsome and a long way from everything. Below are Jaipur’s most enduring hotels by style, each with an honest note and a Book Me link.

Choosing Your Neighbourhood

In Jaipur the choice is less about price than about stairs, heat and how far you are willing to be driven.

Civil Lines, the Pink City, Bani Park or the Amer road?

Civil Lines and C-Scheme are the default recommendation and the answer if you want only one: a flat, leafy, low-noise colonial grid with wide pavements, ten to fifteen minutes from the City Palace and about twenty-five from Amber Fort, with the best restaurants and bars and good private hospitals. Bhawani Singh Road and the Narain Niwas quarter, just to the south, share those virtues with rather more garden and rather less bustle; Rambagh Palace and Narain Niwas both stand here. The Old or Pink City — Gangapole, Johari Bazaar, the lanes under the Hawa Mahal — is the most atmospheric and by some distance the hardest work: narrow lanes, uneven paving, motorbikes, cattle, constant horns, persistent touts around the monuments and very little shade. It is wonderful to walk from a hotel at eight in the morning and punishing as a base. If you want it anyway, take a haveli such as Samode and insist on a ground-floor room. Bani Park, northwest of the station, is the guesthouse and homestay belt: quiet residential streets, good value, nothing to walk to after dark. The Amer road and Kukas corridor to the north has the big resorts with big grounds and big pools, at the cost of forty to fifty minutes each way into the old city and dinner confined to the hotel. Our practical rule for an older visitor: sleep in Civil Lines or C-Scheme, hire a car and driver by the day at roughly twenty-five to forty dollars, and treat the Pink City as somewhere you are driven to at eight in the morning rather than somewhere you sleep.

Luxury

Two quite different kinds of grandeur: a genuine royal residence in the middle of the city, and a walled desert estate outside it where every room is on one level.

Rambagh Palace

$$$$ LuxuryBhawani Singh RoadRoyal residenceSeventy-eight rooms

Built in 1835 as a garden house and by 1925 the principal residence of Maharaja Sawai Man Singh II, who was crowned here in 1931 and lived here with Maharani Gayatri Devi; it became India’s first palace hotel in the nineteen-fifties and has been run by Taj since 1972. Seventy-eight rooms and suites, peacocks on the lawns, and a scale of ground that means you can walk for twenty minutes without leaving the gardens. Suvarna Mahal serves royal Indian cooking in a gilded banqueting hall; the Polo Bar keeps Man Singh’s trophies; and Steam is a bar built inside a restored Victorian railway carriage, which sounds absurd and is delightful. There are indoor and outdoor pools and lifts throughout. Ask for a Palace Room or Historical Suite in the original wing rather than a garden-wing room.

The Oberoi Rajvilas

$$$$ LuxuryGoner RoadThirty-two-acre estateLuxury tents and villas

Thirty-two walled acres on the southeastern edge of the city, built around a restored Shiva temple some two hundred and eighty years old where a daily aarti is held and guests are welcome. Seventy-odd accommodations run from Premier Rooms through luxury tents to villas with private pools; the tents are permanent structures with teak floors, freestanding baths and air-conditioning, not camping. Surya Mahal handles Rajasthani and pan-Indian cooking, and the spa occupies an eighteenth-century haveli moved to the site. For an older traveller this is the easiest luxury address in Jaipur on the legs: everything is single-storey, the paths are level and golf carts run between them. The trade is distance — ten kilometres to the city centre and half an hour or more to Amber Fort.

Boutique

Jaipur’s boutique hotels are old family houses that took in guests, which is their charm and also their catch: beautiful courtyards, irregular levels and stone staircases that were never built with a handrail.

Samode Haveli

$$$ BoutiqueOld City, GangapoleAbout 200 years oldGarden pool

A princely town mansion of around two hundred years, built for the Samode family, whose descendants still live in part of it and run the hotel. Fifty rooms, thirty of them suites, arranged around courtyards; the famous one is the Sheesh Mahal Suite, a low mirrored chamber two centuries old that has to be seen rather than described. Dinner is served on a raised terrace around a fountain, candlelit after dark. The pool is large and ringed by arched terraces and day-beds — a real asset when the afternoon becomes impossible. It stands just inside the northern city walls, twenty minutes from the station. The honest caution: it is a labyrinth of levels. Ask specifically for a ground-floor room with its own patio; one room is adapted for wheelchair use and there are ramps in the public areas.

Hotel Narain Niwas Palace

$$ BoutiqueNarain Niwas, Kanota BaghBuilt 1928Bar Palladio

Built in 1928 by General Amar Singh of Kanota, opened to guests as a four-room bed and breakfast in 1978 and now run by the third generation of the family, standing in ten acres of garden with old cannons on the lawn and a small museum indoors. Around fifty keys, hand-painted walls, antique furniture and a pool. In the grounds sits Bar Palladio, the blue-and-gold Italian restaurant and bar that has become one of the loveliest rooms in India — book it several days ahead, as it fills. One warning we would repeat to any older guest, because a recent visitor put it well: ask for a lower-floor room in advance. The upper rooms are reached by a good many narrow stone steps with no handrail.

Budget & Value

Two long-running family hotels that have kept their reputations for decades. Neither is a comfort pick in the palace-hotel sense; both are honest, central and genuinely inexpensive.

Hotel Pearl Palace

$ ValueHathroi FortTwenty-five roomsPeacock rooftop restaurant

A budget institution of some twenty-five years standing, west of the centre and within walking distance of M. I. Road: twenty-five rooms, each decorated differently, many with a small balcony, at a price that still surprises. The rooftop Peacock Restaurant has been praised in the guidebooks for years and has live music most evenings, which brings us to the one piece of booking advice that matters — ask for a room well away from and below the restaurant floor, because the rooms directly beneath it hear everything. Note also that Pearl Palace Heritage is a different hotel at a different address under related ownership; the booking sites merge their reviews, so specify which one you want.

Hotel Arya Niwas

$ ValueSansar Chandra RoadHotel since 1983Lawns and veranda

Family-run since 1983 in a building around a century old, and the unusual thing about it for a central budget hotel is the greenery: wide lawns and a deep veranda of cane chairs where guests sit out morning and evening, a few minutes from M. I. Road and a short auto-rickshaw from the old city. Rooms run from economy singles to family deluxe, so there is real range inside a low price. Chitra Cafe serves plain, hygienic, home-style food — and here is the thing to know before booking rather than after: the hotel is entirely vegetarian and does not serve alcohol, which suits some travellers very well and others not at all. Lift provision is not stated anywhere, so email and ask.

Family-friendly

What works with children in Jaipur is grounds — somewhere safe to roam while the grandparents sit in the shade, and a proper pool for the hours when nobody sensible is outdoors.

Jai Mahal Palace

$$$ FamilyCivil LinesEighteen acres of gardensPuppet shows

Taj’s second Jaipur palace, with origins reaching back to 1745, restored behind an Indo-Saracenic front with Art Deco interiors: a hundred rooms among pavilions and colonnades set in eighteen acres of formal Mughal gardens. Those gardens are why it works for three generations at once — children can roam safely within the walls while everybody else sits. There is a children’s playground, a games room with billiards, horse-buggy rides on the lawns and evening puppet shows and Rajasthani dancing, alongside a vast pool and three restaurants. Civil Lines is flat, quiet and central: ten to fifteen minutes to the City Palace, about twenty-five to Amber Fort. Ask for a garden-facing room in the main wing. Note that pets are not accepted.

Fairmont Jaipur

$$$ FamilyKukas, Amer roadReal children’s clubSeparate children’s pool

A modern palace in the Mughal-Rajput manner on the Amer road, fifteen kilometres north of the city: two hundred and forty-five rooms and suites, a large outdoor pool with a separate children’s section, and a children’s club that is the real thing rather than a token playroom — cooking classes, pottery, bangle-making, puppet shows and folk performances. Zoya serves Rajasthani food, Anjum does a proper tea, and Aza is the library bar. There is a spa with sauna, steam and hammam. It sits about seven and a half kilometres from Amber Fort, which is convenient, and the honest caveat that recent guests keep repeating is the other side of that coin: it is secluded, and the old city is forty to fifty minutes each way.

Adults-only

We could not verify a single Jaipur hotel that excludes children as a matter of policy, so we will not name one. It is worth saying why, because one popular booking site advertises a hundred and forty-four adults-only hotels in Jaipur and the list includes Rambagh Palace and two large resorts that openly market to families — it is a scoring artefact, not a record of policy. For a couple who want a child-free feel, the honest route is a very small heritage house where children are simply rare: Rajmahal Palace RAAS, with thirteen rooms, or one of the quiet garden wings at Samode Haveli. Both are atmosphere rather than policy, and we would rather tell you that than pretend otherwise. The live listings are below.

Pet-friendly

Pet policies in Jaipur are poorly documented and most of the five-star hotels refuse animals outright — the Jai Mahal Palace and the Jaipur Marriott both state that they do not accept dogs. Two heritage houses do, and both should be confirmed in writing when you book rather than taken on trust from a listing site.

Rajmahal Palace RAAS

$$$$ Pet-friendlyC-SchemeBuilt 1729Thirteen rooms

Built in 1729 and later the home of Maharaja Sawai Man Singh II and Maharani Gayatri Devi; it remains the royal family’s residence, with the hotel occupying part of it. Thirteen rooms and suites, several named for guests who genuinely stayed — there is a Queen Elizabeth suite, and others for Jackie Kennedy and Lord Mountbatten. The Colonnade and the flamboyant pink dining room are the public spaces, and there is an Art Deco pool in a fountained garden. It is tiny, calm and flat, about ten minutes from the City Palace. Dogs are listed as welcome at no extra charge on two reputable booking sites, but the hotel itself publishes no pet policy, so write to the reservations office and get the confirmation in your hand before you fly.

Hotel Diggi Palace

$$ Pet-friendlyC-SchemeDates to the 1730sPeacocks on the lawn

A Diggi family haveli of the seventeen-thirties, run as a heritage hotel in the middle of C-Scheme: about seventy-five rooms of several grades around ancestral gardens and courtyards where peacocks and parrots wander, with a pool pavilion and long lawns. It states a pet-friendly policy on its own website, which is more than most Jaipur hotels manage; the listing sites add that there is no fee, that particular rooms are set aside and that one animal is the limit, so confirm that limit directly. One correction, since it is repeated everywhere: the Jaipur Literature Festival began here in 2006 but outgrew the house years ago and now runs elsewhere. Rooms connect by galleries with some steps — ask for a ground-floor heritage room.

Jaipur Hotel FAQ

The questions travellers ask most before booking a room in Jaipur.

How many nights does Jaipur need?

Three, honestly, and four if you would like a slow day. The first for the City Palace, Jantar Mantar and the Hawa Mahal from the outside; the second for Amber Fort at opening, followed by an afternoon indoors; the third for Nahargarh or Jaigarh, the bazaars, and a block-printing or gem workshop. Two nights can be done but it forces Amber Fort and the City Palace into consecutive tiring mornings, which in the heat is a poor bargain.

Is a palace hotel actually worth the money in Jaipur?

Unusually, yes. Rambagh Palace and Rajmahal Palace RAAS are real former royal residences with documented lineage rather than themed new-builds — Rambagh was the home of Man Singh II and Gayatri Devi, and Rajmahal still belongs to the family. What you are buying is genuine nineteenth-century rooms, gardens that give you somewhere civilised to be at two in the afternoon when the streets are unbearable, and staff enough to matter when you are tired. The imitations are the modern ‘palace-style’ resorts on the Amer road: pleasant hotels, but the architecture is new.

Should we book an elephant ride at Amber Fort?

Our recommendation is no. The rides still run, but under mounting restriction and with well-documented welfare problems — animals declared unfit for work, infections found in the herd, and a long campaign to phase the practice out. There is no blanket ban, and we will not pretend there is; nor will we pretend the concerns are trivial. The jeep costs about four hundred rupees for up to five people against twelve hundred for two on an elephant, reaches the same Sun Gate, and carries none of the difficulty. If you would like to be near elephants, choose a sanctuary where you watch rather than ride.

Can we walk around the Pink City, or do we need a car?

Both, and the pattern matters. The bazaars reward walking, but the paving is uneven and usually occupied, the traffic is relentless and there is very little shade. What works is a car and driver by the day, at roughly twenty-five to forty dollars, who sets you down at one end of a bazaar and collects you at the other. Do not plan to walk between the major monuments, and do not plan to walk anywhere much at all between April and June.

Is Jaipur comfortable for travellers in their sixties and seventies?

Yes, with planning — it is one of India’s easier cities for an older visitor, with good private hospitals, widespread English, reliable hotel cars and a flat central district. The real risks here are heat and uneven ground rather than anything sinister. Travel between November and February; book Civil Lines or C-Scheme; insist on a ground-floor or lift-served room at any heritage house, because the stone stairs are steep and often without a rail; sightsee from eight to eleven and again after half past four; and accept in advance that parts of Amber Fort, and the ramped interior of the Hawa Mahal, may not be worth the climb. The photograph of the Hawa Mahal that everyone wants is taken from the café terraces across the road in any case.
