Best Time to Visit Denmark

Best Time to Visit Denmark

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Denmark is a maritime country at the latitude of southern Scotland, which means two things: the weather is milder than the map suggests and far less settled than you would like. There is no dry season and no reliable one; what changes through the year is the light, and the light changes a great deal. Midsummer in Copenhagen gives better than seventeen hours of daylight and a dusk that never quite finishes; midwinter gives about seven. Choose the month for the light, pack for rain whatever you choose, and you will not go far wrong.

The short answer

Three good answers, depending on what you came for.

For the best of everything

Late May and the first half of September. Long days, open gardens, warm enough water, lower rates and no crowding. Our own pick.

For high summer

Late June to mid-August. Warmest, longest light, every beach and harbour bath open. Busiest on the coast and dearest in the summer-house towns.

For the indoor Denmark

Late November to December. Christmas markets, Tivoli lit up, candles everywhere — and about seven hours of daylight. Cheap outside the holiday weeks.

Month by month

Daylight, weather, what is open and what it costs. Denmark is small, so the whole country runs on much the same calendar — the coast is the part that opens and shuts.

January

The quietest month and the cheapest. Short grey days, temperatures either side of freezing, rain more likely than snow. Museums, swimming halls and long lunches; not a month for the coast. Tivoli is shut for most of it.

February

Much like January with a little more light, and the Copenhagen light festival makes a virtue of the dark. Danish school holidays in the middle of the month fill the family attractions. Still the low season for rooms.

March

The turn. Daylight passes twelve hours around the equinox and the clocks go forward at the end of the month, which transforms the evenings. Cold, windy and often beautiful. The starling murmurations over the Wadden Sea begin.

April

Unreliable and improving. Beech woods come into leaf late in the month, the gardens open, Tivoli starts its season, and the light is already long — but you can get four seasons in an afternoon. Easter closes a good deal for several days.

May

Our favourite month in Denmark. Everything green and newly open, light running to ten at night by the end of it, modest rates and no crowds. Several public holidays fall in May, so check before counting on a Monday.

June

The best all-round month. Midsummer light, warm enough to sit out, the harbour baths open, and the Danish holidays not yet started. Midsummer eve on the twenty-third is marked with bonfires on every beach, and is worth planning around.

July

Peak summer and the Danish holiday month. Warmest water, every seaside town full, summer houses let months ahead and rates at their highest on the coast. Copenhagen itself is quieter than usual because the Danes have left it.

August

Summer with the pressure easing after the middle of the month. Still warm, still long evenings, and the best month for food and festivals in the cities. The sea is at its warmest now rather than in July.

September

The connoisseur’s month, with the first half of it as good as late May. Clear air, autumn colour coming, quiet beaches, falling prices and the murmurations starting again over the Wadden Sea. Darkness returns noticeably by the end.

October

Shoulder tipping into winter. The clocks go back, the coastal towns begin to shut, and the weather turns wet and windy. Good value in the cities, and the autumn school week in the middle of the month is busy at the family attractions.

November

The hardest month: dark, wet and with little open outside the cities. It redeems itself in the last week, when the Christmas markets open and Tivoli lights up. Cheapest rooms of the year until then.

December

Short days and a country that has spent centuries learning to make the indoors pleasant. Christmas markets, lights, long meals and Tivoli at its best. Prices spike over the holiday weeks and a great deal closes for several days around Christmas itself.

Daylight and weather, plainly

Copenhagen sits at about fifty-six degrees north, roughly level with Edinburgh and well south of Oslo or Stockholm, and its daylight swings accordingly: a little over seventeen and a half hours at midsummer, and about seven at midwinter. The north of Jutland gains perhaps another half-hour in summer and loses the same in winter — a real difference by the time you are at Skagen, but nothing like the Arctic.

What the figures understate is the dusk. In June the sky over Denmark never goes properly dark; the light fades to a deep blue around eleven and starts coming back before three. It is the single best thing about a Danish summer and the reason the whole country eats outside.

The weather is Atlantic and changeable rather than extreme. July, the warmest month, averages around the low seventies Fahrenheit — roughly twenty-two degrees Celsius — in the afternoon; January hovers either side of freezing. Snow falls most winters but rarely lies long in the south. Rain is spread through the year with the wettest months in autumn, and the wind is the thing visitors underestimate: this is a flat country with a sea on three sides, and an onshore gale can make a mild day feel raw. Bring a windproof layer in any month and waterproof shoes in any month but July.

Denmark uses the Danish krone (DKK, written kr), not the euro. Denmark is a member of the European Union but voted to keep its own currency and has set no date for changing it, so euro notes are not legal tender — though the krone is held in a fixed band against the euro, which is why the rate barely moves from one year to the next. Pay in kroner. Denmark is one of the most nearly cashless countries in the world: cards and phones are taken everywhere, including on buses, at museum desks and at market stalls, and a good many places no longer handle notes at all, so you need very little cash and may need none. Prices in shops include the twenty-five per cent sales tax, which is among the highest in Europe and is already in the figure on the shelf. Tipping is not expected and never chased — service is in the bill, and rounding up is plenty. Decline any card machine that offers to charge you in dollars; pay in kroner and let your own bank convert.

The calendar

The fixtures worth planning a trip around, or avoiding.

Spring

The starling murmurations over the Wadden Sea marshes — the Danes call it sort sol, black sun — run through March and April and again in autumn. Tivoli opens its summer season around the start of April.

Midsummer

Sankt Hans aften, the twenty-third of June, is kept with bonfires on beaches the length of the country. It is the most Danish evening of the year and costs nothing to join.

Summer festivals

Roskilde, at the end of June, is one of the largest music festivals in Europe and fills every room for miles. The Copenhagen Jazz Festival takes over the capital in early July; Aarhus holds its own festival week at the end of August, and Odense marks Hans Christian Andersen in the same stretch.

Autumn

Culture Night in Copenhagen in October opens hundreds of buildings for one evening. The murmurations return over the marshes. Tivoli runs a Halloween season through the second half of the month.

Christmas

Markets open across the country in the last week of November and run to Christmas. Tivoli’s Christmas season is the one most visitors come for, and the one to book a room around.

Public holidays

Easter closes a great deal for the best part of a week, and May carries several holidays that fall on different dates each year. Christmas and New Year shut most things for several days. Check the dates before you count on a particular Monday.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS — the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years — is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today — nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Denmark has been in the European Union since 1973 and in the Schengen area since 2001, so everything above applies here in full. Denmark did not join the euro, so the currency is the krone, and the rate is held in a narrow band against the euro. One seasonal note: if your plans include crossing to Malmö over the Øresund Bridge, carry your passport even though both countries are in Schengen, because Sweden has run identity checks on that crossing on and off for years.

Best time to visit Denmark FAQ

What is the best month to visit Denmark?

Late May or the first half of September, if you want one answer. Both give long days, open gardens and beaches, mild weather, lower rates than high summer and no crowding. June is the best of the full summer months; July is the warmest and the busiest on the coast. December is worth it for the Christmas markets if you accept seven hours of daylight.

How many hours of daylight does Denmark get?

Copenhagen gets a little over seventeen and a half hours at midsummer and about seven at midwinter, with roughly twelve at the equinoxes. The far north of Jutland gains about another half-hour in summer and loses the same in winter. In June the sky never goes fully dark, which matters more to the feel of a trip than the raw figure suggests.

Is Denmark cold?

Less than its latitude implies. The sea keeps it mild: January averages hover either side of freezing and July afternoons sit around the low seventies Fahrenheit, or about twenty-two Celsius. What catches people out is the wind rather than the temperature — the country is flat with sea on three sides, so bring a windproof layer in any month.

When is the cheapest time to go to Denmark?

January, February and November, outside the Danish school holidays and before the Christmas markets open. Hotel rates in the cities drop noticeably and the coastal summer-house towns are at their quietest, though many of them are also closed. Late April, early May and the second half of September give you far better weather for only a little more.

Does it snow in Denmark?

Most winters, yes, but it rarely lies long in the south and a Danish winter is usually grey and wet rather than white. Reliable snow is not a reason to come here; if that is what you are after, Norway and Sweden are the better answer.

Is it worth visiting Denmark in winter?

For the cities and for Christmas, yes. Copenhagen, Aarhus and Odense are at their most characteristic in the dark months, with markets, lights and the long indoor meals that gave the world the word hygge. For the coast, the dunes and Skagen, no — much of it closes, and what is left is for people who positively want an empty beach in a gale.
