Best Time to Visit Sweden

Best Time to Visit Sweden

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Sweden runs a thousand miles from the beech woods of Skåne to the reindeer pasture above the Arctic Circle, so “when should I go” depends entirely on which Sweden you mean and what you want from it. Below is the short answer, then every month set out plainly, then the two things that decide a Swedish trip more than the weather does: the light, and the holiday calendar.

The short answer

For a first visit, go in late August or early September. The sea and the lakes are at their warmest after a summer of heating up, the archipelago boats are still running, the mosquitoes of the north have gone, the light is still long, and both the crowds and the prices have stepped down from July. If you want the midnight sun you need June or early July and you need to be north of the Arctic Circle. If you want the northern lights you need the dark half of the year — late September to late March — and you need to be in Lapland. Those three answers point at three different trips, and trying to combine them in one visit is the commonest planning mistake we see.

Two dates to put in the diary before you book anything. Midsummer, in the second half of June, is the one holiday Sweden takes completely seriously: shops close, restaurants close, public transport thins out and the cities empty. It is wonderful to be invited to and bleak to arrive into unprepared. And July is the Swedish holiday month, when the country goes to its summer houses and the islands and mountain lodges fill up months ahead.

Month by month

Daylight, weather and what is actually running. Sweden is long, so the north and the south are described separately where they differ.

January

Deep winter. Polar night ending in Lapland around mid-month, reliable snow in the north, and the best aurora odds of the year. Stockholm is dark, cheap and quiet after the first week.

February

The best of the winter north: light coming back fast, snow at its most dependable, aurora still strong, and the ice roads and dog sledding fully running. Swedish half-term weeks are busy and priced accordingly.

March

Our pick for a winter trip. Long enough days to see the landscape, dark enough nights for the lights, and the snow still good. The ski season in the mountains is at its best.

April

An awkward month. Winter activities winding down, summer ones not open, slush in the cities and the lake ice unsafe. Stockholm and Gothenburg are perfectly pleasant; Lapland is between seasons.

May

Lovely and underrated. Everything suddenly green, the light long, blossom in Skåne, modest prices and no crowds. Midnight sun begins in the far north at the end of the month. The sea is still too cold to swim.

June

The month the country comes alive, and the one to handle with care. Midsummer, in the second half, is the biggest holiday of the year: shops, restaurants and whole small towns shut for several days. Around it, June is glorious.

July

Peak summer, and the Swedish holiday month. Warmest weather, every island boat running, and the cities oddly empty as their residents head for the summer houses. Book the archipelago and the mountains early.

August

Our favourite month. The sea is at its warmest, the northern mosquitoes have gone, the berries are ripe, the crowds thin after the middle of the month and the first real darkness returns to the north by the end of it.

September

The connoisseur’s month. Autumn colour, clear air, quiet cities, falling prices, and the aurora possible in Lapland from the second half. Some island boats and northern lodges have already stopped for the year.

October

Shoulder tipping into winter. Dark drawing in quickly, rain and wind on the west coast, good aurora in the north and good value everywhere. Little is running outdoors.

November

The quietest month and the least rewarding for a first visit. Polar night begins in the far north late in the month. Cheap, dark and often wet rather than snowy.

December

Short days, Christmas markets, St Lucia on the 13th and a particular northern charm if you want it. Stockholm gets about six hours of light; Kiruna gets none. Prices spike over the holidays.

The light, which matters more than the weather

Sweden’s seasons are governed by daylight rather than by temperature, and the difference between the top and the bottom of the country is extreme. In Stockholm, midsummer gives about eighteen and a half hours of sun and a dusk that never quite goes dark; in late December the same city gets about six hours, with the sun low across the rooftops the whole time. Go north and it stops being a matter of degree. Above the Arctic Circle the sun does not set at all for weeks around midsummer, and does not rise at all for weeks around the solstice in December — which is not pitch black, but a long blue twilight in the middle of the day.

This is the thing to choose on purpose. A June trip to Lapland means reading a newspaper outdoors at midnight and sleeping behind blackout curtains. A December trip means head torches, deep quiet and a landscape lit by snow. Both are remarkable. Neither is what most visitors expect, and a traveller who books Lapland in December hoping to see the mountains in daylight will be disappointed for reasons no hotel can fix.

In the south, plan the shoulder months around the clocks rather than the calendar. From the end of October the evenings go suddenly, and a late-October afternoon in Gothenburg is finished by five.

Planning around the northern lights

The aurora needs three things at once: darkness, solar activity and a clear sky. You can choose the first, you cannot influence the second, and the third is why where you stand matters. The usual advice is to go to Abisko, in the mountains west of Kiruna, and the reason is meteorological rather than romantic: the valley sits in a rain shadow that leaves it clear when the coast and the forest are clouded over. Kiruna itself, Jukkasjärvi, Björkliden and the Luleå river villages all work too.

Give it time. Three or four nights is the sensible minimum, a week is better, and anyone promising you a sighting on a given night is selling something. Get away from street lights, let your eyes adjust for twenty minutes, and know that a strong display looks nothing like the photographs — to the eye it is usually a pale green arc that moves, rather than the saturated curtain a long exposure records.

The season runs from about the third week of September, when the nights are dark enough again, to the end of March, when they stop being. The equinoxes in late September and late March are statistically the most active periods. And the trip is worth taking in daylight too: the same days give you dog sledding, snowmobiles, the ice hotel at Jukkasjärvi and a mountain landscape that earns the fare on its own.

Sweden uses the Swedish krona (SEK, written kr), not the euro. Prices move with the season as much as with the exchange rate: a room in Stockholm in July or in Lapland at New Year can be double its October price, while the last week of August and the whole of September are the best value of the warm half of the year. Sweden is also one of the most nearly cashless countries in the world — a great many cafés, buses and museums take cards only and will politely refuse notes, so bring a card you can tap and do not bother changing money. Wine, spirits and stronger beer are sold only through the state shops, Systembolaget, which keep short hours and are shut on Sundays. Tipping is not expected. Decline any card machine offering to charge you in dollars; pay in kronor and let your own bank convert.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS — the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years — is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today — nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave. Sweden is in both the European Union and the Schengen area, so arriving from another Schengen country there is no passport control at all.

When to visit Sweden — questions

What is the best month to visit Sweden?

Late August is our own answer for a first visit. The sea and lakes are at their warmest after a summer of heating up, the archipelago boats are still running, the northern mosquitoes have gone, the light is still long and the July crowds and prices have eased. June is the most beautiful month but carries Midsummer, which closes a great deal for several days. September is quieter and clearer still, with the trade-off that some island boats and northern lodges have stopped for the year.

When can I see the midnight sun in Sweden?

North of the Arctic Circle, which in practice means Kiruna, Abisko, Gallivare or Jokkmokk, the sun stays above the horizon for several weeks around midsummer: roughly the end of May to the middle of July at Kiruna, with the exact run depending on how far north you go. Further south there is no true midnight sun, but there is something nearly as good - from June to early July, Stockholm and Gothenburg never get properly dark, and the dusk runs into the dawn.

When is the best time to see the northern lights?

Late September to late March, and in Lapland rather than the south. The aurora needs darkness, solar activity and a clear sky at the same time, so give yourself three or four nights at least and treat a sighting as a gift rather than an itinerary item. Abisko is the usual recommendation because its valley sits in a rain shadow and stays clear more often than anywhere else in the region. The weeks around the equinoxes in late September and late March are statistically the most active.

What is Midsummer and will it affect my trip?

Midsummer is the biggest holiday in the Swedish year, falling on a Friday in the second half of June, and it will affect your trip if you arrive into it unprepared. Shops, restaurants, museums and many hotels in small places close for two or three days, public transport runs a holiday timetable and the cities empty out as people head to the countryside. If you are invited to a celebration it is the best day of the year to be in Sweden. If you are not, plan to be in a large city with your meals thought through in advance.

Is Sweden worth visiting in winter?

Yes, if you pick your winter. Lapland between December and March is one of the great winter destinations: aurora, dog sledding, snowmobiles, ice hotels and proper silence, with warm clothing lent by the lodges. The southern cities in winter are a different and much milder proposition - short grey days, Christmas markets, museums, skating and good value. What the south generally does not offer is reliable snow. The one practical warning is ice underfoot in both: buy clip-on studs for your shoes in any pharmacy.

How many days do I need in Sweden?

Five days is enough for Stockholm and a day in the archipelago. A week lets you add Uppsala and either Gothenburg or the south. Ten days gets you Stockholm, the west coast and Skane at a civilised pace, or Stockholm and Lapland, which is a different country again. Sweden is longer than most maps suggest and the commonest mistake is treating Stockholm and Kiruna as a comfortable day apart when the train alone is the better part of a day and a night.
