Larnaca

Where to Stay in Larnaca

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Larnaca is the island’s front door — the main international airport is ten minutes from the seafront — and it is a far better place to spend time than an airport town has any right to be. The centre is low-rise and slightly worn in a way that many visitors come to prefer to the polish of Limassol, and it is built around one excellent thing: Finikoudes, a palm-lined promenade running the length of a town beach with the fishing harbour at one end and the medieval fort at the other. Behind the town lies a large salt lake that dries to a white crust in summer and fills with flamingos in winter, with a Muslim shrine among the palms on its far shore. South and east of the centre the coast runs out through beach strips and villages towards Cape Kiti and the Dhekelia road. It is the easiest Cypriot town to arrive in late and like immediately.

Finikoudes and the centre

Most centralPromenade and town beachWalk everywhere

The palm promenade is the heart of the town and the obvious place to stay. The beach runs alongside it, the fort anchors the southern end, the fishing harbour and the marina the northern, and behind it the lanes hold the church of Saint Lazarus, the Sunday market and most of the restaurants. Everything a visitor wants is within a flat ten-minute walk, including the bus station and the dive shops. It is busy and cheerful rather than smart, the town beach is imported sand and perfectly decent, and the evening passeggiata along the front is the best free entertainment in Larnaca.

Mackenzie Beach

Better sandBeach barsPlanes overhead

A kilometre or two south of the centre, Mackenzie is the strip of beach bars and restaurants that Larnaca goes to at the weekend, with a longer and better stretch of sand than the town beach and a louder, younger evening. It is a flat walk or a very short taxi from the centre. The thing nobody mentions in the brochures is directly overhead: Mackenzie sits on the approach to the airport runway, and aircraft pass low over the beach all day. Plenty of people find it entertaining; if you do not, stay in the centre instead.

Oroklini and the Dhekelia road

Larger hotelsQuiet beachesNeed a car or the bus

North-east of town the coast road towards Dhekelia carries a run of bigger beach hotels spaced along a quieter shore, with the village of Oroklini on the hill behind and another small lake that takes its own share of the winter flamingos. This is the package end of Larnaca: more space, more facilities, less town. You are ten or fifteen minutes from the centre by bus or taxi, and well placed if you are also planning days out to the east towards Ayia Napa.

Pervolia and Cape Kiti

Village paceVillas rather than hotelsCar essential

South past the salt lake, the coast flattens out towards the lighthouse at Cape Kiti through the villages of Pervolia and Kiti, where the accommodation is mostly villas and small apartment blocks and the pace drops sharply. The church of Panagia Angeloktisti at Kiti holds a sixth-century mosaic that is among the finest Byzantine survivals anywhere, and is regularly missed by people staying ten minutes away. You will want a car; the bus exists but is slow.

Pyla and the inland villages

CheapestEveryday CyprusNothing within walking distance

Inland behind the Dhekelia road, Pyla is unusual — one of the very few villages where Greek and Turkish Cypriots have continued to live side by side, sitting partly within the buffer zone and overlooked by a United Nations post on the hill. Along with the other villages behind the coast it offers the cheapest rooms in the district and an entirely ordinary Cypriot setting. There is nothing to walk to and no reason to stay here without a car, but the value is real.

Getting around Larnaca

On foot

Larnaca is flat, small and the most walkable town on the island. The promenade runs the whole length of the centre, the old quarters behind it are level, and you can cross the visitor’s half of the town comfortably in twenty minutes. The path continues south towards Mackenzie, so even the beach strip is reachable on foot.

The airport

Larnaca international is the island’s main airport and sits about ten minutes from the seafront — you can be on the promenade with a drink astonishingly soon after landing. Frequent buses run into town and on to Nicosia, Limassol and Ayia Napa, and they are the cheap way to start a holiday. The proximity is also the one drawback of Mackenzie Beach, which lies under the approach.

Buses

Town buses cover the seafront, the Dhekelia road and the villages, and intercity coaches run from the Finikoudes area to Nicosia, Limassol and the south-east resorts. It is the one Cypriot town where a carless holiday is genuinely practical, as long as you accept thin evening and Sunday services.

Diving and boats

Larnaca’s other reason to exist, for a lot of visitors, is under the water: the wreck of the Zenobia lies on her side just outside the harbour and is rated among the best wreck dives in the world. Shops along the front run trips daily in season for qualified divers, with conditions deciding the day. Boat trips also run east along the coast in summer.

When to come, and what it costs

Larnaca shares the island’s long hot summer but gets more breeze than inland Cyprus, and its flat, walkable centre makes it one of the more bearable places to be in August — though the middle of the day is still the middle of the day. The sea is warmest from late August into October. Winter here has a particular attraction: between about November and March the salt lake fills with water and with flamingos, several thousand of them in a good year, which is a genuine spectacle twenty minutes’ walk from the town beach. The town stays open all year because the airport does, so winter rooms are cheap and restaurants keep trading.

Getting in: Cyprus is in the EU but outside Schengen

The Republic of Cyprus belongs to the European Union and uses the euro, but it is not in the Schengen area. Days you spend in Cyprus do not count against the ninety days in any hundred and eighty that Schengen allows, and days spent in Schengen countries do not count against your Cyprus allowance; the two are tracked separately. Cyprus is also outside the EU’s Entry/Exit System, so there is no biometric registration on arrival. Citizens of the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, Australia and the European Union need no visa for a holiday of up to ninety days. British advice is a passport issued within the last ten years and valid three months beyond departure; United States advice recommends six months of validity. Confirm your own nationality’s terms with your own foreign ministry.

One rule matters more than any other here. Enter the Republic only through its own points of entry — the airports at Larnaca and Paphos and the southern seaports. Arriving through the north and crossing south is treated as illegal entry and can mean a fine or a refusal. Crossing north for a day from the south is the opposite: legal, routine and well worth doing, with your passport in your pocket. Time spent in the north still counts towards your ninety days, there are limits on goods brought back, and many hire companies restrict taking their cars across — check your rental agreement first.

Larnaca — questions

Is Larnaca worth staying in, or just for the airport?

Worth staying in, and a good many returning visitors now prefer it. It is the flattest and most walkable town on the island, the promenade is genuinely pleasant, the restaurants are cheaper than Limassol's and it is well placed for day trips in three directions. What it does not have is a spectacular beach or a resort atmosphere. If your idea of Cyprus is white sand and a swim-up bar, go east to Protaras; if it is a town you can walk around with a church, a fort and a lake full of flamingos, stay here.

How close is the airport, really?

About ten minutes to the seafront, which is unusually convenient and occasionally audible. The runway approach passes over Mackenzie Beach south of the centre, so aircraft noise is a real consideration in that strip and much less of one in the centre or north of it. If you are a light sleeper, ask which way the hotel faces and avoid the Mackenzie end.

When are the flamingos there?

Roughly November to March, depending entirely on the rain. The salt lake is dry and white in summer and fills over the winter, and the birds arrive once it does; a wet winter brings more of them and keeps them longer. There is a footpath and boardwalk around part of the lake from the edge of town, and the Hala Sultan Tekke mosque on the far shore is worth the walk whether or not the birds have arrived.

Can I dive the Zenobia as an ordinary holiday diver?

The upper parts of the wreck, yes, if you are a qualified open-water diver in reasonable practice; she lies on her side with the shallowest sections well within recreational limits, and the shops run guided dives daily in season. The deeper decks and the interior are technical diving and not something to attempt on a holiday ticket. Book with a shop that asks to see your certification and logs, and be prepared for conditions to cancel the day.

Is it a good base for the whole island?

A very good one. Nicosia is about forty minutes, Limassol forty-five, Ayia Napa and Protaras around forty, and Paphos an hour and a half. That makes Larnaca the only town from which every other major destination on the island is a comfortable day trip, and the airport on the doorstep means you lose none of the holiday to transfers.
