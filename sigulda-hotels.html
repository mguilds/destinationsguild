Sigulda

Where to Stay in Sigulda

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Sigulda stands on the lip of the Gauja valley about fifty kilometres north-east of Riga, and it is the one place in Latvia where the ground does something dramatic. The river has cut a wooded sandstone gorge through the middle of Gauja National Park, and three castles stand around it: the ruins of the Livonian Order’s on the town side, the red-brick tower of Turaida on the far slope, and Krimulda manor above it. A small cable car crosses the gap in three minutes. It is Latvia’s walking country, the place the whole of Riga comes to look at the autumn colour, and a straightforward hour or so out by train.

The town on the plateau

Most centralWalk to the cable carMost beds

Sigulda proper sits on the high ground on the south side of the Gauja, and almost everything a visitor books is here: the station, the small centre, the New Castle and the ruins of the Livonian Order’s castle behind it, and the cable-car station at the edge of the plateau. From the middle of town you can walk to the castle ruins, the viewing platforms along the lip of the valley and the cable car without getting into anything. Stay here unless you have a specific reason not to.

The valley floor and the Gauja riverside

Best settingQuietA climb back up

Down in the gorge, along the river between Sigulda and the Turaida side, are guesthouses, a campsite or two and a handful of small places with the Gauja running past them. It is the prettiest ground in the area and the quietest place to wake up. The catch is the valley wall: it is a long climb or a drive back up to the town, the shuttle services are seasonal, and after dark the roads down there have no pavement. Lovely with a car, awkward without one.

Turaida and Krimulda: across the gorge

Museum reserveVery quietCable car or a long loop

The far side of the valley, where the red-brick tower of Turaida castle stands in its museum reserve above Gutmanis Cave, and Krimulda manor sits at the top of the opposite slope with the cable car landing beside it. There is very little lodging over here and what there is is quiet, rural and close to the best of the walking. By cable car it is three minutes from Sigulda; by road it is a loop of several kilometres round through the valley.

Out towards Cesis and the national park

Rural guesthousesCar neededCheapest

Beyond the town the Gauja National Park runs north-east for forty kilometres towards Cesis, and the lodging in it is farm guesthouses, forest cabins and small country hotels on tracks off the main road. It is the cheapest way to stay in this landscape and the most rewarding if you want the river, the sandstone cliffs and the woods rather than a town. Getting around Sigulda

On foot

The town is small and walkable, and the viewing platforms and castle ruins are a few minutes from the centre. The walking that brings people here — down to Gutmanis Cave, along the river, up to Turaida — involves long wooden staircases down the gorge and back, which is real effort rather than a stroll.

The cable car

A small cabin on a cable hangs across the Gauja valley between Sigulda and Krimulda, about forty metres above the river, and it is both the quickest way over the gorge and the best three minutes in the region. It runs to a timetable and seasonally; check at the station rather than assuming.

The train from Riga

Sigulda is on the Riga to Valga line, roughly an hour and a bit from Riga Central, with several services a day. The station is a short walk from the middle of town. In early October the trains out here fill with people coming to look at the autumn colour.

A car, and the valley shuttles

A car makes the Turaida side, the national park and the Cesis road much easier, and parking in Sigulda is straightforward. Without one, the cable car covers the gorge and seasonal shuttle buses run between the main sights; out of season they thin out, so plan the valley around the cable car and your own legs.

When to come

Sigulda has one unmistakable high season and it is not summer. Late September and the first fortnight of October are what this place is famous for: the Gauja valley is beech, birch, aspen and maple over sandstone, and when it turns, Latvians come out from Riga by the trainload to see it. Book ahead for those two weeks. June to August is the walking and adventure season — the bobsleigh track, the wind tunnel, the river, the trails — busy at weekends and calm midweek. Winter is quiet and often beautiful, with the valley under snow, sledging and the bobsleigh track in competition use; some of the smaller attractions close and the staircases into the gorge can be icy. Spring is the one to be wary of: the thaw turns the valley floor to mud and the Gauja can run high.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Latvia is in the European Union, the Schengen area and the euro, so arriving from anywhere else in Schengen there is no check at all. Month-by-month weather and the dates worth planning around are in Best Time to Visit Latvia, and the country guide is The Latvia Travel Guide.

Sigulda — questions

Is Sigulda a day trip from Riga or worth a night?

Most people do it as a day trip, and a day is genuinely enough for the cable car, the castle ruins, Turaida and the cave. A night is better if you want to walk properly in the Gauja National Park, if you are coming for the autumn colour and want the valley in the morning light, or if you have children and the adventure attractions on your list. In the first fortnight of October a night here is worth planning months ahead.

What is actually in Sigulda?

Three castles and a gorge. On the town side are the ruins of the Livonian Order’s thirteenth-century castle and the nineteenth-century New Castle beside it; across the valley are the restored red-brick tower of Turaida in its museum reserve and Krimulda manor on the slope. Below them is Gutmanis Cave, the largest in the Baltics, its sandstone walls carved with graffiti going back centuries and attached to the legend of the Turaida Rose. Add the cable car over the gorge, the Soviet-built bobsleigh track still used for World Cup racing, a vertical wind tunnel and a great deal of walking.

How long does the autumn colour last?

Roughly the last week of September to the middle of October, with the peak usually in the first ten days of October and the whole thing ended by the first serious wind. It is the busiest fortnight of the Sigulda year. If you want it without the crowds, come midweek and walk the river rather than queueing for the cable car.

Can I get here and around without a car?

Getting here, easily: the train from Riga takes a little over an hour and the station is a short walk from the centre. Getting around is more mixed. The cable car crosses the gorge and seasonal shuttle buses link the main sights in summer, but out of season you are on your own legs, and the ground between the town and Turaida is a long staircase down and another back up. Perfectly doable if you are a walker; frustrating if you are not.

Is it suitable for someone who does not want stairs?

Partly, and more than you would expect. The town, the New Castle grounds, the viewing platforms along the lip of the plateau and the cable car are all level or nearly so, and the cable car gives you the whole gorge without a step. The castle ruins are uneven, and Gutmanis Cave and the river paths involve long wooden staircases into the valley and back out. Pick the plateau and the cable car and you will still have seen the best of it.

What about the bobsleigh track?

It was built for the Soviet team and is still in competition use, which means Sigulda hosts international racing in winter. Outside competition weeks it has long offered rides to the public, in summer on a wheeled sled and in winter on the ice, which is a genuinely startling way to spend four minutes. What is running, and when, changes with the season and the racing calendar, so check locally rather than turning up expecting it.
