Denali National Park and Preserve

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

A Visitor's Guide · Interior Alaska · One road, six million acres, North America's highest peak

Denali is seen mostly from the window of a park bus, rolling west on a single gravel road past caribou, grizzlies and the mountain itself when the clouds allow. Here is how the road, the buses and the season work, and what changes in 2027.

Before you go

Denali works differently from most national parks, and knowing how before you book saves a great deal of disappointment.

There is one road into Denali, and for most of the summer you can drive only the first 15 miles of it, to the Savage River check station. Beyond that the road is gravel and narrow, and you travel it on a park bus: either a narrated tour bus or a cheaper transit bus that lets you hop off and back on along the way. The bus is not a compromise. Fewer cars mean more wildlife near the road, and the drivers are very good at spotting it.

Since 2021 a landslide at Pretty Rocks, around mile 45, has stopped buses at East Fork, near mile 43. A 475-foot bridge over the slide was finished in early September 2026, and the park expects public bus service to the west end of the road to resume in phases for the 2027 season. Bus and campground reservations for summer 2027 open December 1, 2026. If you have waited to see Wonder Lake and the Kantishna country, next summer is the time to plan for.

Plan on a full day for any bus trip. Even the shorter tours run four and a half hours or more, and the long ones run twelve to thirteen hours on a gravel road with rest stops about every 90 minutes. Pack layers, rain gear, binoculars, water and food; there is nowhere to buy anything once you leave the entrance area.

As for the mountain: Denali, at 20,310 feet, makes its own weather and hides in clouds much of the summer. Many visitors never see it. Go for the whole place, the tundra, the rivers and the animals, and count the mountain as a gift if it shows.

Park Road and Pretty Rocks: buses have turned around at East Fork, near mile 43, since the 2021 Pretty Rocks landslide. The bridge across the slide was dedicated September 2, 2026. The park says full bus service along the Park Road is expected to resume in 2027, in phases as road work and visitor services come online, with routes added to the reservation system as they are set. Wonder Lake Campground (mile 85) is listed with a June 8 opening.

Eielson Visitor Center: the park reports that Eielson will close for up to two years for roof replacement starting in 2027, so even when buses reach the west end, plan on no visitor center there.

Bus fees and entrance fees: narrated tour bus tickets include the $15 entrance fee; transit bus tickets do not, so pay the entrance fee separately or show your pass. Riders 15 and under ride the transit buses free.

Winter now: the road is plowed only to Park Headquarters (mile 3) until about mid-February, when it typically opens to Mountain Vista (mile 13). The Winter Visitor Center is open daily except Thanksgiving, Christmas and New Year's Day, and Riley Creek is the only campground open.

Sources: the National Park Service website (nps.gov/dena), checked November 2026.

Best things to do in Denali National Park and Preserve

Six ways to spend your days at Denali.

Bus tour

Ride the Park Road

Choose a narrated tour bus (Natural History, Tundra Wilderness and longer trips) or a transit bus you can step on and off. Book ahead for the longer routes, which sell out in summer.

Wildlife

Grizzlies, caribou and Dall sheep

The road west of Savage River is where most visitors see Denali's big animals. Keep binoculars handy and call out anything you spot; the driver will stop.

Sled dogs

The park kennels

Denali keeps working sled dogs that patrol the park in winter. A shuttle runs from the Denali Visitor Center to the kennels, 1.5 miles away.

Drive

Savage River

The 15 miles you can drive yourself in summer end at Savage River, with a picnic area, river views and the Savage Loop Trail, whose first half-mile is wheelchair accessible.

Walk

Entrance-area trails

Short trails start near the visitor center: the McKinley Station Trail, the Spruce Forest Trail and the Horseshoe Lake Trail, a good taste of the boreal forest.

Remote

Wonder Lake and Kantishna

Closed to buses since 2021, the far end of the road, 85 miles in, is set to reopen in phases in 2027. On a clear day, Wonder Lake holds the classic reflection of the mountain.

From the Guild

Guild tip: Book the earliest bus departure you can manage. Animals are most active in the cool of the morning, the light is better, and you are back at the entrance with energy left for supper. If you are 62 or older, bring your Senior Pass: tour bus tickets include the entrance fee, and pass holders can get that $15 refunded at the bus depot or Riley Creek Mercantile with their pass, photo ID and reservation.

When to visit Denali

Denali's season is short. Buses start running about May 20, though the longer tours run a shorter route until the road fully opens in early June. Transit buses run until the second Thursday after Labor Day. June and July bring long daylight, green tundra, wildflowers and the most mosquitoes; late August and early September bring red and gold tundra, cooler nights and the first chance of snow.

After the buses stop in fall, private cars may drive as far as Teklanika River (mile 30) until about October 15 or until snow closes the road at Park Headquarters. In spring, the road is typically plowed and open to cars to about mile 30 by late April, with ranger programs starting around May 15.

Winter is for the hardy: the road closes at Park Headquarters, temperatures fall far below zero, and the park becomes a place for skiing, snowshoeing and dog mushing. Many hotels and businesses near the entrance are seasonal and close for the winter. If you are coming on a cruisetour or a once-in-a-lifetime trip, plan for June through August.

How to get to Denali National Park and Preserve

By car

The park entrance is at milepost 237 of the George Parks Highway (Alaska Route 3), 240 miles north of Anchorage and 120 miles south of Fairbanks. The highway is paved and open year-round, but services are thin between towns, so keep the tank above half. Local businesses give their locations by milepost, with higher numbers to the north.

Inside the park you may drive to Savage River (mile 15) in summer. Free shuttles also link the visitor center, campgrounds and trailheads along that first stretch, no reservation needed.

By air, train and bus

Fly into Anchorage or Fairbanks. From either city, the Alaska Railroad runs to Denali and passes right through the park entrance; the depot is about a five-minute walk from the Denali Visitor Center. Many travelers find the train the most relaxing way to arrive, with dome cars and no highway driving.

Private bus and van services run daily in summer between Anchorage, Fairbanks and Denali. Once there, public transportation outside the park is very limited, so if you are not on a cruisetour, think carefully about whether you will want your own car.

Where to stay near Denali National Park and Preserve

There are no park-run hotels at Denali. Most visitors stay along the Parks Highway near the entrance or in Healy, and many add a night in Talkeetna or Anchorage on the way north.

Lodges inside the park

The National Park Service operates no hotels at Denali; inside the park it runs only campgrounds. The few lodges inside the boundary are privately owned wilderness lodges on old mining land in the Kantishna area at the far end of the Park Road: Camp Denali, Denali Backcountry Lodge, Kantishna Roadhouse and Skyline Lodge. While the Pretty Rocks closure lasted, they could be reached only by air. With the road expected to reopen in 2027, ask each lodge directly how its guests will travel and what its 2027 season looks like. Book through the lodges themselves.

Most visitors stay in the hotels, lodges and cabins along the Parks Highway near the entrance, in Healy, or about 30 miles south in Cantwell. Many of the hotels near the entrance are seasonal and close in winter; book early for June through August, especially if you need a specific bus date.

RV travel & camping at Denali

Denali is a classic RV destination, but there are no hookups in the park and a hard 40-foot limit.

Three park campgrounds take RVs: Riley Creek, just inside the entrance and open year-round; Savage River, at about mile 14, open mid-May to mid-September; and Teklanika River, at mile 29, open from May 20 into September. No campground in Denali can fit an RV over 40 feet, and none has electric or water hookups. Sanctuary River, Igloo Creek and Wonder Lake are tent-only and reached by the camper bus.

Teklanika is the one place you may drive your own rig past Savage River in summer, with a three-night minimum stay if you drive in. Campsites cost about $25 to $60 a night, with 50 percent off for Senior and Access Pass holders, and are booked through reservedenali.com starting December 1 for the following summer. Camping is free in winter, roughly late September to early May.

The dump station, showers, laundry, potable water and a small store are at the Riley Creek Mercantile near the entrance. Water at Riley Creek is turned on around May 15, weather permitting. Private RV parks with hookups operate along the Parks Highway near the entrance and in Healy; book them early for summer.

Fishing in Denali

Fishing in Denali is more of a side pleasure than a reason to come. Most streams and lakes near the road run gray with glacial silt and hold few fish; clear creeks and ponds deep enough not to freeze solid in winter may hold a few fish. In the original part of the park, the old Mount McKinley National Park, no Alaska licence is required. In the newer park additions and the preserve, including Kantishna, you need a current Alaska sport fishing licence and state rules apply.

In the old park area the limit is ten fish per person per day, not to exceed ten pounds and one fish, and only two lake trout. Fishing is by hook and line only, and all bait, live or dead, is prohibited. For serious Alaska fishing, look south and east to the salmon rivers, and see our guide to fly fishing British Columbia and Alaska.

Accessible travel at Denali

What is available for visitors with limited mobility, and the passes that help.

Many tour and transit buses have wheelchair lifts. Ask for a lift bus when you book or when you buy tickets at the Denali Bus Depot. Buses stop about every 90 minutes, and every rest stop on the Park Road has at least one accessible restroom. If the buses cannot meet your needs, the park offers a road travel permit to drive some or all of the Park Road in your own vehicle; contact the park several weeks ahead for the application.

The Denali Visitor Center has accessible parking, a paved approach with a slight grade, a push-button front door, an elevator and accessible restrooms, and lends one wheelchair free at the desk. The kennels shuttle has at least one lift-equipped bus. Level, compacted-gravel trails include the Spruce Forest Trail (flat, 5 feet wide), the Mountain Vista Loop (5 percent maximum grade), the first half-mile of the Savage Loop and the McKinley Station Trail (up to 8.5 percent).

At Riley Creek Campground a few sites near the Mercantile are designated accessible; request one when you reserve. The Mercantile has accessible restrooms and showers. The other campgrounds have no designated accessible sites, though many are level.

Park passes that save you money. U.S. citizens and permanent residents with a permanent disability can get the free lifetime Access Pass. Visitors 62 and over can buy the Senior Pass ($80 for life, or $20 a year). Veterans and Gold Star families get a free Military Lifetime Pass, and current service members a free annual pass. Each covers entrance fees at every national park.

Traveling on dialysis? The nearest dialysis clinic is in Fairbanks, about 120 miles north of the park entrance; to the south there are clinics in Wasilla and Anchorage. Book your treatment at a clinic there several weeks before you go; the large dialysis companies run travel desks that will arrange it with your home unit.

Denali by cruise ship

Denali is the most popular land extension on an Alaska cruise. Many lines sell cruisetours that pair a one-way Gulf of Alaska sailing with several nights on land, typically traveling between the ship and Denali by motorcoach or the Alaska Railroad and staying at the lines' own lodges near the park entrance. The bus trip into the park is usually included or offered as an add-on.

If you are building a cruisetour, choose one with at least two nights at Denali so a cloudy day does not cost you the whole visit, and with the Pretty Rocks bridge open, ask whether 2027 packages include a longer trip down the Park Road. Denali National Park and Preserve FAQ

How much does it cost to enter Denali National Park?

$15 per person age 16 and over, good for seven days. Children 15 and under are free. A Denali annual pass costs $45. The park takes cards only, no cash. Narrated tour bus tickets include the entrance fee; transit bus tickets do not.

Can you drive your own car in Denali National Park?

Only the first 15 miles, to Savage River, in summer. Beyond that you travel by park bus, bicycle or on foot. Campers with a Teklanika River reservation may drive to mile 29.

Is the Denali Park Road open past mile 43?

Not yet for buses. The Pretty Rocks bridge was completed in September 2026, and the park expects bus service to the west end of the road to resume in phases in 2027.

When do Denali bus reservations open for 2027?

December 1, 2026, through reservedenali.com, for both buses and campgrounds.

Is Eielson Visitor Center open?

The park says Eielson will close for up to two years for roof replacement starting in 2027.

When is the best time to visit Denali?

June through August for the full bus schedule and long days; late August and early September for fall color and fewer mosquitoes. Buses run from about May 20 to mid-September.

Where should I stay near Denali National Park?

Along the Parks Highway near the entrance, in Healy 11 miles north, or in Cantwell 30 miles south. Inside the park there are only campgrounds and four private wilderness lodges at Kantishna.

Can I see Denali on an Alaska cruise?

Yes. Many lines sell cruisetours that add several nights at Denali to a one-way Gulf of Alaska sailing, traveling by motorcoach or the Alaska Railroad.

Plan your Denali trip

Book your bus seats on December 1, line up a room near the entrance, and give the mountain two days to show itself.
