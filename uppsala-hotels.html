Uppsala

Where to Stay in Uppsala

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Uppsala is a small city with a very large past: the seat of the Swedish church, the home of the oldest university in the Nordic countries, and before either of those the centre of pagan Sweden, whose kings are buried under grass mounds four kilometres north of the cathedral. It is also forty minutes by train from Stockholm and eighteen from Arlanda airport, which makes it either a day trip, a quieter and cheaper alternative to staying in the capital, or a sensible first and last night either side of a flight. The city is small enough to cross on foot in twenty-five minutes, and the choice of where to sleep comes down to four areas.

Where to stay in Uppsala, area by area

Four parts of a small city, what each is actually like, and who each one suits.

Fjärdingen — the cathedral and the castle quarter

Most atmosphericThe sightsSlopes

The high western bank of the river, where the cathedral — the largest church in Scandinavia, with twin towers you can see from the train — stands above the old university buildings, the Gustavianum with its anatomical theatre, the Carolina Rediviva library and the botanical garden below the castle. Staying here puts everything you came to see within five minutes’ walk and gives you the quietest evenings in the city. There are only a handful of places to sleep in these streets, they are small, and the ground rises steadily from the river, which is worth knowing if a hill is a consideration.

The centre and the station side

Most convenientFlatBest value

The eastern bank, between the river and the railway, is the working city: the shops, the market square at Stora torget, the main run of restaurants, and almost all of the hotels. It is flat, it is five minutes’ walk across the bridge from the cathedral, and the station on its edge puts you forty minutes from Stockholm and eighteen from the airport. This is where the chains are and where the value is, particularly at weekends and in July when the business and conference trade disappears. It is unremarkable to look at and entirely practical to stay in.

Luthagen and the university side

ResidentialQuietGood for longer stays

North-west of the cathedral, behind the university buildings, Luthagen is the handsome residential Uppsala of early twentieth-century apartment blocks, bakeries, and students on bicycles. There is not much in the way of hotels, but there are apartments and small guesthouses, and it is the right choice for a stay of several days or for anyone who would rather be among people getting on with their lives than among visitors. The river path into the centre is flat and takes ten minutes.

Gamla Uppsala — the burial mounds

HistoryFour kilometres outVisit, do not stay

The original Uppsala, four kilometres north of the modern one: three enormous royal burial mounds from the sixth century, a medieval church built on the site of a pagan temple, and a good museum explaining what is and is not known about either. It is the most remarkable thing in the district and we would not suggest sleeping out here — there is almost nothing to sleep in, and nothing open in the evening. Stay in town and come out by city bus, which takes about fifteen minutes from Stora torget.

Getting around

Uppsala is a walking and cycling city and it is almost entirely flat east of the river. The cathedral, the castle, the museums, the market square and the station are all inside a twenty-five-minute walk of one another, and the riverside path through the middle is the pleasantest way between any two of them. Bicycles are the local transport of choice and there is a cheap hire scheme; in a student city of this size you will be in good company.

City buses cover the rest, including Gamla Uppsala, and take contactless cards or the regional transport app rather than cash. For anything beyond the city, the train is the answer: Stockholm Central is about forty minutes away with several departures an hour, and Arlanda airport is eighteen minutes in the other direction — which is why a first or last night in Uppsala is often more restful and less expensive than one in central Stockholm.

One practical note for the airport run. Trains to Arlanda from Uppsala are quick and frequent, but the station sits under the terminal and a supplementary passage charge applies on top of the ordinary fare; buy the through ticket in the regional app or at the machine and it is included, rather than discovering it at the barrier.

When to come, and what it costs

Uppsala’s calendar is a student calendar, and it has two peaks that will affect what you pay and what you find open. The last day of April, Valborg, is the biggest student celebration in Sweden: the city fills, rooms are booked months ahead and the river is lined with people watching rafts go by. Graduation in early June and the start of term in late August are the other busy weeks. Outside those, the city is quiet and rooms are reasonable all year.

The best months to visit are May to September, with the botanical garden and the riverbanks at their best in late spring and the light long into the evening through midsummer. July is the one month to think twice about: the students are gone, a good many restaurants take their holiday, and the city is sleepier than its size suggests. Autumn is handsome and brief; winter is cold, dark and genuinely pretty under snow, with the cathedral lit against it.

On cost, Uppsala is cheaper than Stockholm for an equivalent room and markedly cheaper at weekends, when the business trade leaves. The weekday fixed-price lunch is served by almost every restaurant in the centre and is the best-value meal in the city. And the great majority of what there is to see here — the cathedral, the burial mounds, the botanical garden, the riverside — costs nothing at all.

Sweden uses the Swedish krona (SEK, written kr), not the euro, and is one of the most nearly cashless countries in the world — a great many cafés, buses and museums take cards only and will politely refuse notes, so bring a card you can tap and do not bother changing money. Wine, spirits and stronger beer are sold only through the state shops, Systembolaget, which keep short hours and are shut on Sundays; weaker beer is in the supermarkets. Tipping is not expected and never chased — rounding up a bill is plenty. Decline any card machine that offers to charge you in dollars; pay in kronor and let your own bank convert.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS — the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years — is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today — nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave. Sweden is in both the European Union and the Schengen area, so arriving from another Schengen country there is no passport control at all.

Staying in Uppsala — questions

Is Uppsala worth visiting?

For a day, certainly, and for a night or two if you like old university towns. The cathedral is the largest church in Scandinavia, the Gustavianum holds a seventeenth-century anatomical theatre, the Carolina Rediviva library keeps the Codex Argenteus, and the royal burial mounds at Gamla Uppsala are the most significant pre-Christian site in the country. All of it sits in a small, flat, walkable city with a river through the middle. What it is not is a substitute for Stockholm; it is a quieter, older and much cheaper complement to it.

Should I stay in Uppsala instead of Stockholm?

It is a reasonable choice in two situations. If you are arriving at or departing from Arlanda, Uppsala is eighteen minutes from the airport against twenty to the centre of Stockholm, and the rooms cost less - so a first or last night here is often more restful and cheaper. And if you want a base for the Malaren valley and the old churches north of the capital, Uppsala is better placed. For a holiday whose centre of gravity is Stockholm itself, stay in Stockholm: forty minutes each way adds up over several days.

How far is Uppsala from Stockholm and from Arlanda airport?

Trains run from Uppsala Central to Stockholm Central in about forty minutes, several times an hour, and to Arlanda airport in about eighteen minutes. Both are straightforward and step-free. For the airport, buy the through ticket in the regional transport app or at a machine - a supplementary station charge applies at Arlanda on top of the ordinary fare, and the through ticket includes it rather than leaving you to find out at the barrier.

What is Valborg and should I avoid it?

Valborg, on the last day of April, is the largest student celebration in Sweden and Uppsala is where it is taken most seriously: rafts on the river, a champagne breakfast, crowds along the banks and a city given over to it entirely. Whether to avoid it depends on what you want. If you would enjoy being in the middle of a very large and very good-humoured party, book months in advance. If you want to look at a cathedral in peace, choose another week - rooms are scarce and dear, and the quiet Uppsala you came for is not there that day.

How long do I need in Uppsala?

Half a day covers the cathedral, the Gustavianum and a walk along the river. A full day lets you add the castle, the botanical garden and the bus out to the burial mounds at Gamla Uppsala, which is the part most people are glad they did not skip. Two nights is plenty unless you are using the city as a base for the surrounding valley, in which case three or four with a regional transport ticket works well.
