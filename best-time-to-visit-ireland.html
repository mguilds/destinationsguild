Best Time to Visit Ireland

Best Time to Visit Ireland — Month by Month

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated August 2026

Ireland has one of the mildest climates in Europe and one of the greyest. The Gulf Stream keeps it from ever getting properly hot or properly cold — a summer afternoon in Galway and a winter afternoon in Galway are barely fifteen degrees apart — so the Irish calendar is not really about temperature at all. It is about rain, daylight and crowds, and those three things change enormously between January and July. Here is the whole year, honestly: what the weather actually does, how much darker December is than you are imagining, which weeks in Dublin and Galway double in price, and what shuts along the Wild Atlantic Way when the season ends.

The short answer

Come in May or September. May is the driest and sunniest month of the Irish year, the countryside is at the peak of its green, everything seasonal has just reopened, and the evenings already run to nearly ten o’clock. September gives you the warmest sea, empty roads in Kerry and Connemara, and room rates that fall the moment the Irish schools go back in the first week. June is a close third and owns the longest evenings of the year, at a slightly higher price. July and August are the warmest and by a distance the busiest and dearest — Killarney, Dingle and Galway are genuinely full, and the weather is not meaningfully better than in May. November to February is cheap, quiet and dark, superb for Dublin, Galway, Cork and Belfast, and a poor bet for a driving holiday.

Season by season

Four seasons that differ far more in light and crowds than in temperature.

Spring — March to May

The season that improves fastest. March is raw and windy and the west only half awake, but by late April the hedgerows are out and the evenings stretch past eight. May is the best single month in Ireland: driest and sunniest on the long-term records, the greenest the country ever looks, every seasonal ferry and mountain road open again, and rates still below summer. Only St Patrick’s weekend and Easter break the calm.

Summer — June to August

Warmest, brightest, busiest, dearest. Highs sit around 18–20°C (65–68°F) and rarely pass 25°C, so summer here means long light rather than real heat. Early June is glorious and not yet crowded; from the last week of June the school holidays begin and Kerry, Dingle, Connemara and the Cliffs of Moher fill with coaches, while Galway sells out for its July festival fortnight.

Autumn — September to October

Our other favourite. September behaves like a quieter, cheaper August: the sea at its warmest, the roads clear the week the schools return, and light until half past eight. October is softer and wetter, with real colour in the Wicklow and Killarney woods and the first Atlantic storms — excellent value, but the seasonal west starts closing at the end of it, so drive the coast early.

Winter — November to February

Cheap, quiet, green and dark. Ireland barely freezes — Dublin averages about 8°C (46°F) in January — but daylight shrinks to seven and a half hours and the wind and rain are at their most persistent. The cities are the answer: Dublin, Galway, Cork, Kilkenny and Belfast run at full strength, with Christmas markets from mid-November and the lowest rates of the year.

Month by month

Typical daytime highs for Dublin; the west runs slightly milder in winter, cooler in summer and much wetter all year. Daylight is Dublin sunrise to sunset.

January — 8°C / 46°F · 8 hrs light

The cheapest month, and with February the wettest and windiest. Nothing freezes for long, but Atlantic depressions queue up and the light is gone by half past four. A city month and an excellent one: rock-bottom rates in Dublin and Galway, empty museums, and music sessions full of locals rather than tour groups.

February — 8°C / 47°F · 9.5 hrs light

Still cheap, still stormy, but the light is returning — by month’s end the afternoons stretch past six. The St Brigid’s Day bank holiday on the first Monday gives a long weekend and a modest bump in city rates. Fine for Dublin, Cork or Kilkenny at half the summer price; poor for cliffs and boats.

March — 10°C / 50°F · 12 hrs light

Two identities in one month. Most of it is a bargain shoulder month with rapidly lengthening days and a blustery edge. Then St Patrick’s Day on the 17th lands and Dublin doubles in price for the weekend around it. Travel a fortnight either side and you get Ireland cheaply, with the west just reopening.

April — 12°C / 54°F · 14 hrs light

Spring properly arrives — showery and changeable, the classic four-seasons-in-an-afternoon month, but bright between the showers and greener by the day. The gardens of Wicklow, Kerry and Cork are at their best, the Wild Atlantic Way is reopening, and evenings run to half past eight by the end. Shoulder prices, except over Easter.

May — 15°C / 59°F · 16 hrs light

The best month in Ireland. Statistically the driest and sunniest, with the countryside at the peak of its green and the fuchsia starting in Kerry. Everything seasonal is open, the coach tours have not yet arrived in force, and the light lasts until nearly ten. The May bank holiday is the only busy weekend.

June — 18°C / 64°F · 17 hrs light

The longest days of the year: around the solstice the sun sets close to ten and the western sky keeps enough light to walk by until nearly eleven, which changes what fits into a day. Warm, green and reasonably quiet for three weeks; from about the 20th the schools break up and prices climb into the peak.

July — 20°C / 68°F · 16.5 hrs light

The warmest month, the wettest of the summer three, and the busiest. Kerry, Dingle, Connemara and Clare are at capacity and hire cars are scarce and dear. Galway is a special case: the Arts Festival takes the middle of the month and the Races the last week, and between them the city has no rooms at any price.

August — 20°C / 68°F · 15 hrs light

As warm as July, as busy, and with the sea at last worth swimming in at around 15–16°C. The August bank holiday on the first Monday is the heaviest domestic travel weekend of the year. If you are locked to school holidays this works — book far ahead, start early, and expect peak rates on everything.

September — 17°C / 63°F · 13 hrs light

The other best month. The Irish schools go back in the first week and the roads empty almost overnight, yet the weather holds and the sea is at its warmest of the year. Rates fall from the first weekend, everything seasonal is still open, and the light runs to half past eight early on.

October — 14°C / 57°F · 11 hrs light

Soft, wet and beautifully quiet, with autumn colour in Killarney National Park and the Wicklow woods and low-season rates from mid-month. The first serious Atlantic storms arrive now, and the clocks go back on the last Sunday, taking the evenings away suddenly. Halloween, which Ireland invented, is celebrated properly.

November — 10°C / 51°F · 9 hrs light

The quietest, cheapest month before the Christmas trade begins: grey, wet, dark by half past four, with much of the seasonal west closed. The compensation is price and peace, plus the Christmas markets opening in Galway’s Eyre Square and at Belfast City Hall from mid-month. A city-and-pub month, not a driving one.

December — 8°C / 47°F · 7.5 hrs light

The darkest month by a wide margin: near the solstice the sun rises about twenty to nine and sets just after four, and under low cloud the afternoon can feel finished by three. The cities are excellent value until about the 20th — after which the holiday fortnight is busy and dear and many businesses close until the 2nd of January.

Rain, wind and daylight — plainly

The three things that actually decide what an Irish trip feels like.

The west is far wetter than the east

The most useful weather fact about Ireland, and almost nobody arrives knowing it. Dublin sits in the rain shadow of the Wicklow Mountains and takes roughly 730 mm across about 130 wet days a year — less than Rome. The Atlantic side is another climate: parts of Kerry, Connemara and Donegal take well over 1,400 mm across 200 to 225 wet days. A week in Dublin and Kilkenny is a very different gamble from a week in Dingle.

What Irish rain is actually like

Rarely the day-long downpour visitors picture. The usual pattern is short, sharp showers pushed in off the Atlantic with bright, washed spells between them — which is why the light is so good and why locals set out walking under a black sky. Never cancel a day for the forecast. The exception is a named Atlantic storm, most likely from October to February, when the sensible thing is to stay off exposed cliffs and coast roads.

Daylight is the real difference

Ireland lies as far north as Labrador, so the swing is enormous. In mid-December Dublin gets about seven and a half hours of light, and Donegal less; you will drive in the dark at both ends of the day. In mid-June it is roughly seventeen hours, with a long dusk that keeps the west workable until nearly eleven. May and September give thirteen to sixteen — enough to drive the Ring of Kerry, stop often, and still eat dinner in daylight.

Rain gear beats an umbrella

Leave the umbrella at home. Irish rain arrives on an Atlantic wind, and on any clifftop or western beach an umbrella is inverted and finished inside a minute; note how few locals carry one. What works is a hooded waterproof that packs small, shoes or boots with real grip for wet cobbles and wet limestone, and layers rather than one heavy coat — plus gloves and a hat from November to March.

Best time for…

The same country rewards very different months depending on what you have come to do.

A Wild Atlantic Way road trip

Best: mid-May – late SeptSweet spot: SeptemberAvoid: Nov – Feb$$ Shoulder value

A driving holiday needs three things Ireland only supplies for part of the year: long daylight, open businesses, and roads you can enjoy. September delivers all three — thirteen hours of light, everything still trading, the coach traffic gone home — and mid-May to June is its equal with more daylight still. Steer away from July and August unless you must: the Ring of Kerry, the Slea Head loop and the Cliffs of Moher approaches are genuinely congested, and Killarney and Dingle sell out. In winter, short days and wet, narrow, unlit roads make the same drive a chore.

Hiking and the hills

Best: May, June, SeptemberDriest: MayLongest days: JuneBring waterproofs

Irish walking is at its best in May, the driest month, and June, the brightest: the Wicklow Way, the Kerry Way, Connemara and the Mournes are comfortable then, with firmer ground and light enough to start late and finish late. September is the autumn equal — drier underfoot than October, quieter than August. Two cautions: Irish uplands are boggy and can be wet even in a dry month, so an ankle-high waterproof boot is not optional; and hill weather turns fast off the Atlantic, so check the forecast in the morning, not the night before.

The tightest budget

Cheapest: January & FebruaryClose behind: NovemberAvoid: St Patrick’s weekend$ Rock bottom

Transatlantic airfares and Irish hotel rates both bottom out in the dark months. January and February are the cheapest weeks of the year on both counts, with November and early December close behind — the carve-outs being the Christmas fortnight and, in Dublin, St Patrick’s weekend. For decent weather at a decent price, take the shoulder edges: late April and the second half of September into early October, which give near-summer conditions at roughly a third off peak. Midweek beats weekends, and a hire car booked months out costs a fraction of one booked on arrival.

Avoiding the crowds

Best: late Sept – OctAlso: late April – mid-MaySkip the bank holidaysGo early in the day

Ireland’s crowd map is small and predictable. The pressure is on the south-west and the west — Killarney, Dingle, the Ring of Kerry, the Cliffs of Moher, Connemara and the Aran Islands — and only from late June to late August, plus the bank holiday Mondays. It eases the moment the Irish schools return in the first week of September. Two habits do the rest: see the famous sites before ten in the morning or after five in the afternoon, and take one base rather than five.

The dates that move prices

Ireland is small enough that one event can empty a city of rooms. Check these before you book anything.

St Patrick’s Day — 17 March

A public holiday across the island and, in Dublin, a multi-day festival rather than a single parade. The city fills from the Friday before: central rooms that go for about €150 on an ordinary March night commonly ask €300 and upward, minimum-night stays appear, and good restaurants book out weeks ahead. Cork, Galway, Limerick and Belfast parade at a smaller premium. Book six months out, or move a fortnight and pay half.

Galway in July — the Arts Festival and the Races

Two events back to back make one city the most expensive bed in Ireland. The Galway International Arts Festival takes roughly the middle two weeks of July, filling the town with theatre, street spectacle and big-name music. The Galway Races at Ballybrit follow from the last Monday for about a week, and are as much a national social occasion as a race meeting. Between them Galway has effectively no rooms at sane prices, and availability tightens as far out as Ennis and Athlone.

The All-Ireland finals at Croke Park

The hurling and football finals are the two biggest days in the Irish sporting year, filling Dublin completely with 82,000 in the ground and a whole county celebrating afterwards. Worth knowing: since the Gaelic Athletic Association rearranged its calendar the finals now land in July rather than in September as they did for generations, so check the fixtures for your year rather than assuming late summer.

Christmas markets and the holiday fortnight

Ireland’s Christmas season starts early. The Galway Christmas Market in Eyre Square and the market at Belfast City Hall open around mid-November and run to just before Christmas, with festive programmes in Dublin, Cork and Waterford too. It is a lovely, inexpensive time to visit up to about the 20th of December; after that the fortnight is busy and dear and many businesses close until the 2nd of January. Belfast charges in pounds sterling.

Easter, bank holidays and rugby weekends

Easter moves each year and empties the hotels wherever it lands. The Republic also has bank holiday Mondays at the start of February, on 17 March, at the start of May, June and August and at the end of October, each adding a surcharge in the coastal towns; Northern Ireland keeps a different list. One quirk catches visitors out: when Ireland play a Six Nations home match at the Aviva on a February or March Saturday, Dublin rates spike for that weekend alone — the difference between €150 and €350 for the same room.

What it costs, by season — 2026 in euro

Realistic 2026 planning figures for the Republic of Ireland, in euro. Northern Ireland charges in pounds sterling and generally runs a little lower. Typical ranges, not quotes — confirm the total on the booking page.

Low season — November to February

A comfortable three-star double in central Dublin runs about €90–€150 a night, and in Galway, Cork or Limerick about €80–€130. Country houses and four-stars discount heavily, often to €150–€200 with dinner, and a compact hire car is at its cheapest at roughly €180–€280 a week. The exception is the Christmas fortnight, when city rates climb back toward summer levels.

Shoulder — March to May, and October

The best value-for-weather in the Irish year: €130–€190 for a good central Dublin double, €110–€170 in Galway, Cork or Killarney, and €250–€400 a week for a hire car. Guesthouses and B&Bs, still the soul of Irish travel, sit around €100–€140 with a proper breakfast. The one spike is St Patrick’s weekend in Dublin, when the same room doubles.

Peak — late June to August, and the September drop

Central Dublin runs €200–€300 for a three-star double and more on event weekends; Killarney, Dingle and Galway ask €180–€280 and sell out early. Hire cars are the real shock at €450–€700 a week — book the car before the hotel. Allow €35–€55 a head for a pub dinner without wine. From the first weekend of September coastal rates fall 25 to 35 percent while the weather barely changes.

The extras that catch people out

Irish car hire quotes look low until the counter, where the collision-damage excess is high and excess insurance is pushed hard; many US credit-card rental waivers specifically exclude the Republic of Ireland, so check your card’s terms before declining anything. Tolls are modest, but the M50 around Dublin is barrier-free and must be paid online by 8pm the next day. Dublin levies no per-night tourist tax, so the quoted rate is close to the real one.

Common questions about timing an Ireland trip

What is the single best time to visit Ireland?

May and September, and of the two we would take May. It is statistically the driest and sunniest month of the Irish year, the country is at its greenest, everything seasonal on the Wild Atlantic Way has reopened, and the light lasts until nearly ten at night. September is its mirror image: the warmest sea of the year, roads emptied of July traffic, and rates that fall the week the Irish schools go back. June is a close third and owns the longest evenings, at a higher price. July and August are the warmest months and by a distance the busiest and dearest, and the weather is not better enough to justify the gap.

Does it really rain every day in Ireland?

No, but on more days than you are used to, and how many depends entirely on which coast you are standing on. Dublin, in the rain shadow of the Wicklow Mountains, takes about 730 millimetres a year across roughly 130 wet days — less than Rome. The far west is another climate: parts of Kerry, Connemara and Donegal take well over 1,400 millimetres across 200 to 225 wet days. The character matters more than the totals. Irish rain usually comes as short showers pushed in on an Atlantic wind with bright spells between them, not as a day-long downpour.

How dark is Ireland in December, and how long are the June evenings?

The swing is dramatic, because Ireland lies as far north as Labrador. Around the winter solstice Dublin gets about seven and a half hours between a sunrise near 8.40 and a sunset near 4.10, and Donegal is darker still; under low cloud a December afternoon can feel finished by three, so plan short driving days. June is the reward: roughly seventeen hours of daylight, the sun setting close to ten, and a long northern dusk that keeps the sky over Galway or Sligo walkable until nearly eleven. It is the strongest practical argument for a late-spring or early-summer trip.

What does St Patrick’s Day do to prices in Dublin?

It roughly doubles them, and for a long weekend rather than a single day. The 17th of March is a public holiday across the island and Dublin runs a multi-day festival around it, so the city fills from the Friday before: central rooms that sell for about €150 on an ordinary March night commonly ask €300 and upward, minimum-night stays appear, and good restaurants book out weeks ahead. Book six months out if the parade is the point. If it is not, travel a fortnight either side and pay half for a far quieter Dublin.

Is the Wild Atlantic Way worth doing in winter?

It is spectacular and it is half closed. From November to March many seasonal businesses along the western seaboard shut: small-town cafes and craft shops keep short hours or close, some guesthouses close for the winter, and boat trips to the Aran Islands and Skellig Michael are reduced or suspended — the Skellig landings run only from about mid-May to the end of September in any year. Add seven and a half hours of daylight and wet, narrow, unlit roads. Drive the coast between May and early October, and save winter for the cities, where nothing closes at all.

What should I actually pack, and do I need an umbrella?

Leave the umbrella at home and bring a proper waterproof jacket with a hood. Irish rain arrives on an Atlantic wind, and on any exposed clifftop, promenade or western beach an umbrella is inverted and finished within a minute — which is why you will see so few locals carrying one. Bring a rain shell that packs small, waterproof shoes or boots with real grip for wet cobbles and wet limestone, and layers rather than one heavy coat: the temperature range across a whole Irish year is narrower than a single American summer week.
