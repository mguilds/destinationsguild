Brunei Travel Guide

Researched and compiled by the Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

The Omar Ali Saifuddien Mosque, Bandar Seri Begawan. Photo: Wikimedia Commons.

Brunei Darussalam is a sultanate on the north coast of Borneo roughly the size of Delaware, wrapped on three sides by Malaysia and with about 450,000 people in it. It is wealthy, orderly, quiet and almost entirely forested — a far higher share of Brunei is still under primary rainforest than anywhere else on the island — and it sells no alcohol, which more than anything else sets the tone of an evening here. Most of what a visitor comes for is in or near Bandar Seri Begawan: two of the most striking mosques in Southeast Asia, the stilt village of Kampong Ayer out over the river, and a short boat ride to proboscis monkeys in the mangroves. Beyond that there is Jerudong on the coast, the oil towns of the Belait district in the west, and the rainforest of Temburong across the bay. This guide covers the three places where our booking partner holds a real choice of rooms, and it is straight with you about the parts of the country where it holds none.

The four districts of Brunei

Brunei is divided into four districts, and they are genuinely different places rather than administrative lines on a map. Where our partner lists rooms we link them; where it lists none we say so and leave the button off.

Brunei-Muara — the capital districtDISTRICT I

The smallest district and the one that holds almost everything: Bandar Seri Begawan itself, the Omar Ali Saifuddien Mosque on its lagoon with the replica royal barge in front of it, the larger and more ornate Jame’ Asr Hassanil Bolkiah Mosque out at Kiarong, the Royal Regalia building with the coronation chariot inside, and the water village of Kampong Ayer — several thousand people living on stilts over the river, reached by water taxi from the embankment for a couple of dollars. Take a boat upriver in the late afternoon for the proboscis monkeys. North of the city the district runs out to the beaches and the ferry terminal at Muara.

Tutong — the middle, and the big lakeDISTRICT II

The quiet district between the capital and the oil coast, and the one most visitors drive straight through. Its reason to stop is Tasek Merimbun, the largest lake in Brunei and an ASEAN Heritage Park: black water, a boardwalk out to an island in the middle, and birds. Tutong town itself sits where the river meets the sea. Our partner lists one hotel here — The Lanes Hotel, on Simpang 14 — and we checked it, but one property is not a chapter, so we have given the district an honest browse link rather than a page of its own.

Belait — oil country in the westDISTRICT III

The largest district and the one that paid for modern Brunei. Kuala Belait is the country’s second town, with an old market and a long beach; Seria, ten miles east, has the Billionth Barrel Monument standing over the field that made the sultanate rich. Inland the district runs up to Labi and the longhouses at the forest edge. The Sungai Tujoh crossing into Sarawak is a few miles west of Kuala Belait, which makes this the natural overnight stop for anyone driving to or from Miri. A note on Seria: our booking partner does not file it as a town of its own — ask it for Seria and it offers you the capital — so there is no Seria list for us to point you at, and the Kuala Belait page covers the coast instead.

Temburong — the rainforest exclaveDISTRICT IV

The eastern piece, cut off from the rest of Brunei by a wedge of Malaysian Sarawak, and since 2020 reached directly by the Sultan Haji Omar ‘Ali Saifuddien Bridge across the bay — which turned a half-day with two border posts into a drive of about an hour. What is over there is Ulu Temburong National Park: a longboat up the Temburong and Belalong rivers, then a climb to a canopy walkway high in primary rainforest. It is the best day in the country. We are leaving the booking button off this one on purpose — our partner holds nothing in Temburong that we could verify, and the park is normally done as a day trip from the capital or through an operator who arranges the boats and the longhouse stay together. Book it that way, and sleep in Bandar Seri Begawan.

Before you book

Visas — and why we are not printing the rule

Brunei lets a good many nationalities in without a visa for a set number of days, and others can apply in advance; the lengths differ by country and they are not all the same number. What we are not going to do is print the list or the day counts. We reached the Immigration and National Registration Department’s own website from here today, but could not open the page that carries the exemption list, and an entry rule copied out of a travel article and left to go stale is exactly the kind of thing that leaves someone standing at a border. Go to the department’s site at immigration.gov.bn, or to the Brunei embassy or high commission for your country, and check close to the date you travel. Their site also links an e-Arrival Card; check whether you are expected to complete one before you fly. If your trip crosses into Sarawak or Sabah by road, check Malaysia’s rules separately — and note that a drive to Temburong no longer needs them, now the bridge is open.

No alcohol — the thing to know before you land

Brunei sells no alcohol. Not in hotels, not in restaurants, not in shops; there are no bars, and a hotel ‘bar’ here means coffee. Non-Muslim visitors have long been allowed to bring in a personal quantity for their own consumption and must declare it to customs on arrival. The allowance is set by the Royal Customs and Excise Department, and we are not printing a figure because we could not read the current one off the department’s own site today — check it before you fly rather than trusting a number from a forum. None of this is a reason not to come; it is a reason to expect a different kind of evening. Dinner is the event here, the night markets at Gadong and Jerudong are where people actually go, and the country is quiet after dark.

Customs and courtesy

Brunei is a Muslim country and it is sincere about it, in a way that costs a visitor nothing but a little attention. Dress modestly, and cover arms and legs at the mosques — both of the great ones admit non-Muslim visitors outside prayer times, and both lend robes at the door. Take your shoes off where you are asked to. Use your right hand to give and receive. During the fasting month of Ramadan, visitors are expected not to eat, drink or smoke in public during daylight hours; hotels go on serving their own guests. Public displays of affection and casual swearing both land badly. In return you get a country where almost nothing is hurried and almost nobody is rude.

Money

The currency is the Brunei dollar. Under a currency interchangeability agreement that has run since 1967 it is held at par with the Singapore dollar, and Singapore notes are accepted in Brunei in everyday use — a useful thing to know if you are arriving from Changi with leftover cash. Cards work in hotels, malls and the larger restaurants; carry some cash for water taxis, the night markets and the smaller places west of the capital. We do not quote exchange rates on these pages, because by the time you read it any figure we printed would be wrong.

Getting about

There is no railway anywhere in Brunei. Flights come into Brunei International Airport at Berakas, just north of the capital, and Royal Brunei Airlines is the national carrier; the airport is close enough to town that an ‘airport hotel’ here is an ordinary city hotel. By land, the coastal highway runs west through Tutong to Seria and Kuala Belait and on to the Sarawak border at Sungai Tujoh for Miri; eastward, the bridge now carries you to Temburong in about an hour. Buses exist but are thin, taxis are few and ride-hailing is patchy, so beyond the central blocks of the capital plan on a hire car or a driver arranged through your hotel. Distances are small: nothing in the country is more than about two hours from anything else.

Fishing here

Brunei is a coastal country with good offshore and river fishing, and the Belait and Temburong rivers both hold fish worth a day. We have not written a Brunei fishing guide, and rather than invent one we will point you at the shelf: the whole of it is in The Guild Fishing Library. If you want to fish while you are here, arrange it locally through a charter at Muara or through your hotel, and ask about licences and closed seasons when you book rather than when you arrive.

Brunei questions, answered

How many days do you need in Brunei?

Two full days covers the country’s headline sights comfortably; three lets you add a day up the river into Temburong, which is the one that people remember. A single night is enough for the two great mosques, the Royal Regalia building and a water taxi across to Kampong Ayer, and a great many visitors arrive with exactly that — a stopover between Southeast Asian flights. Four or five days, with a night out at Jerudong and a drive west to the Belait coast, is a proper and very unhurried holiday.

Can you buy alcohol in Brunei?

No. Brunei does not sell alcohol — not in hotels, not in restaurants, not in shops, and there are no bars. Non-Muslim visitors have long been permitted to bring in a personal quantity for their own consumption, declared on arrival; the allowance is set by the Royal Customs and Excise Department, and we are not printing a figure here because we could not read the current one off the department’s own site today. Check it before you fly. This single fact shapes an evening in Brunei more than anything else on this page: dinner is the event, the night markets are where people go, and the country is quiet after dark in a way that some visitors find a relief and others find long.

Is Brunei worth visiting on its own, or only as a stopover?

Honestly, both work, and we would rather say which is which. As a stopover — a night or two between flights — it is excellent value for the time: the capital’s sights are close together, nothing is crowded, and it is one of the easiest and safest places in the region to arrive in cold. As a holiday in its own right it asks you to want what it has: rainforest, a river, two extraordinary mosques, a famously grand hotel, and very little else by design. If you need nightlife or a long list of attractions, go next door. If you want somewhere genuinely unhurried, this is it.

Do you need a car in Brunei?

Outside central Bandar Seri Begawan, effectively yes. Public transport is thin, taxis are few, and ride-hailing is patchy, so almost every visitor who gets beyond the capital either hires a car or arranges a driver through their hotel. Driving is easy — good roads, light traffic, on the left. Within the central blocks of the capital you will walk, and the water taxis across to Kampong Ayer are part of the pleasure of the place.
