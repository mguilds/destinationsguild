Why visit Germany

Few countries balance big-city culture, deep history and storybook countryside so effortlessly — or make it all so easy to reach.

Living history

The Brandenburg Gate and the remains of the Wall, Cold War Berlin, medieval old towns and grand cathedrals — Germany wears its whole twentieth century, and the centuries before it, in plain sight.

Fairy-tale landscapes

Neuschwanstein above its gorge, castles strung along the Rhine, the Bavarian Alps and the deep Black Forest — the scenery that inspired the Brothers Grimm.

Beer, bread & markets

Beer gardens and wheat beer, a thousand kinds of bread and sausage, hearty regional cooking — and the glowing Christmas markets that light every town square in December.

Effortless to travel

Fast, comfortable ICE trains link the cities in a few hours, the autobahn runs everywhere, and the country is famously clean, orderly and safe.

How the country divides up

Berlin & the east

Most first trips start in Berlin, and it rewards the time — the Brandenburg Gate and the Reichstag, Museum Island, the East Side Gallery and Checkpoint Charlie, and a nightlife and art scene with few equals. Nearby Potsdam keeps the palaces of Sanssouci, while further south Dresden has risen again in Baroque splendour and Leipzig pairs Bach with a buzzing creative edge.

Bavaria & the south

Munich is the prosperous, good-humoured gateway to the south — beer halls, grand squares and the launch pad for the Alps. From here you reach Neuschwanstein, the castle that inspired Disney, near Füssen; the medieval walls of Nuremberg; and the peaks around Garmisch-Partenkirchen and the Zugspitze.

The Rhine & the west

The Rhine Valley between Rüdesheim and Koblenz is the Germany of postcards — vineyards, riverboats and a castle on every crag. Frankfurt brings a rare European skyline and the country's busiest airport; Cologne is crowned by its vast Gothic cathedral; and Heidelberg keeps its romantic ruined castle above the Neckar.

Hamburg & the north

The north runs to water and red brick. Hamburg is a handsome harbour city of canals, warehouses and the new Elbphilharmonie; Lübeck and Bremen preserve their Hanseatic old towns; and the Baltic and North Sea coasts draw Germans to their own beaches and islands each summer.

Top things to do in Germany

The experiences worth building a trip around.

Cold War Berlin

The Brandenburg Gate and the Reichstag dome, the East Side Gallery painted onto the Wall, Checkpoint Charlie and the treasures of Museum Island.

Neuschwanstein Castle

Ludwig II's fairy-tale palace, floating above an Alpine gorge near Füssen — the model for Disney's castle. Book timed tickets well ahead.

Bavaria & Oktoberfest

Munich's beer halls and gardens, the Marienplatz glockenspiel, and — if you time it right — the roar of Oktoberfest in late September.

The Romantic Rhine

Cruise the gorge between Rüdesheim and Koblenz past terraced vineyards, the Lorelei rock and a castle on nearly every hilltop.

Christmas markets

From Nuremberg and Dresden to Cologne, December fills the squares with mulled wine, lights and gingerbread — Europe's finest.

Forest & Alps

Hike or drive the Black Forest and the Bavarian Alps, ride to the Zugspitze summit, and soak in the spa towns of the south.

Best time to visit & getting there

When to go

May–September is the main season: long days, warm weather, beer gardens and open-air festivals, with July and August the busiest and priciest.

May–June and September–October are the shoulder-season sweet spots — mild, bright and less crowded, ideal for cities, castles and the countryside alike.

Late November–December is cold and dark but magical, as the famous Christmas markets fill every town square, and the Alps open for skiing into March.

Getting there

Frankfurt (FRA) and Munich (MUC) are the two big long-haul hubs, with Berlin Brandenburg (BER) the capital's gateway and direct flights from across the world.

Hamburg (HAM), Cologne/Bonn (CGN) and Düsseldorf (DUS) also take international flights, and Germany's fast ICE trains link the major cities so smoothly that many visitors skip domestic flying altogether.

The currency is the euro; Germany is still notably cash-friendly, so carry some for bakeries and small cafés. Always check your current visa and passport-validity requirements before booking.

Germany travel FAQ

When is the best time to visit Germany?

May to September is the main season — long days, warm weather, beer gardens and open-air festivals, with July and August the busiest and priciest. Late spring and early autumn (May–June and September–October) are the sweet spots for mild weather and thinner crowds. Winter is cold and dark but magical from late November to Christmas, when the country's famous Christmas markets fill every town square, and the Alps open for skiing.

What currency is used in Germany?

The euro (EUR, symbol €). Germany remains more cash-friendly than many of its neighbours, so carry some notes and coins for bakeries, markets, small cafés and older Gaststätten. Cards and contactless are widely accepted in hotels, supermarkets and larger restaurants, though not universal. ATMs (Geldautomaten) are everywhere; if a machine offers to charge you in your home currency, decline and pay in euros to avoid a poor built-in rate.

Berlin or Munich — which should I choose?

Berlin is the edgy, creative capital — modern history at the Wall and the Reichstag, world-class museums, and a nightlife and art scene with few rivals; it is big, spread out and rewards several days. Munich is more compact, prosperous and traditional, the gateway to Bavaria, the Alps and the castles, and the home of Oktoberfest and the classic beer-hall Germany many visitors picture. On a first trip they pair well, linked in under four hours by fast ICE train.

How many days do you need in Germany?

For a first trip, ten to fourteen days lets you combine Berlin, Munich and a stretch of the Rhine or Bavaria, all linked by fast trains. With only a week, pick two anchors — Berlin and Munich, or Munich with the Bavarian castles and Alps — rather than trying to cross the whole country. Germany's ICE network is quick and comfortable, so many visitors skip the car unless they want to drive the Romantic Road or the Black Forest.

Is Germany safe for travellers?

Germany is one of the safest countries in Europe for travellers, with low violent crime and excellent infrastructure. The usual big-city caution applies — watch for pickpockets around major stations, festivals and crowded transit in Berlin, Munich and Cologne. Public transport is clean, punctual and reliable; jaywalking is frowned upon and often fined. As always, check your government's current travel advisory before you go.
