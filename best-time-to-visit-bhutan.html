Best Time to Visit Bhutan

Researched and compiled by the Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Punakha Dzong in autumn light. Photo: Wikimedia Commons.

Bhutan has one outstanding season and one difficult one, and the gap between them is mostly about cloud rather than cold. October and November are the clearest weeks of the year and the ones to aim for; March to May is the other good window; late June to late September is the Indian monsoon. What complicates the picture is altitude: the country runs from subtropical lowland on the Indian border to glaciers on the Tibetan one, so “Bhutan in January” means two quite different trips depending on which valley you are standing in. The tables below give four of them.

Season by season

Autumn — late September to late November. The best of it.

The Department of Tourism’s own description is “bright, sunny days and some early snowfall at higher elevations”, and that is exactly right. The rain stops, the dust settles, the high peaks come out from behind cloud for the first time since spring, and the harvest festivals run through the valleys. Thimphu’s October rainfall averages about 208 mm against 633 mm in July. The cost is crowding: this is the season every operator sells, and the valley hotels in Paro and Punakha are the first to go. Book the autumn months well ahead.

Winter — late November to early March. Clear, cold, and cheap.

Frost through much of the country and snow common above about 3,000 metres, with gale-force winds through the high passes — the winter wind is where the name Drukyul, Land of the Thunder Dragon, comes from. The skies are often the clearest of the year and the country is quiet. A winter itinerary normally stays west and drops into Punakha, where afternoons average around 61°F (16°C) in January, for the warm nights. Phobjikha is the winter prize: the black-necked cranes are in. High passes and trekking routes may be closed.

Spring — March to May. The second window.

The Department calls the early spring “a botanist’s delight”, and the rhododendron on the passes is the reason. It is drier than summer without being as crowded as autumn, the trekking routes open, and the valleys warm quickly — Punakha afternoons are already near 77°F (25°C) by May. Showers build through late April and May as the summer weather comes on, and the mountain views are less reliable than in October.

Summer — late June to late September. The monsoon.

Wet, warm, green and cloudy. July is the wettest month in every valley we measured: about 633 mm in Thimphu, 592 mm in Paro, 471 mm in Punakha, 510 mm in Bumthang. The southern hills take the worst of it. Expect cloud over the peaks, slower driving, landslide risk on the east road and leeches on forest paths; expect also an empty country, the lowest prices of the year and the farming valleys at their greenest. The Department warns plainly that monsoon rain can affect transport and services between June and September, so build slack into a summer itinerary.

Festivals — and why we are not printing dates

The tsechus are the reason many people choose their month, and the big ones at Paro and Thimphu draw the largest crowds of the year. But the dates follow the Bhutanese lunar calendar and move from year to year, and a festival date printed on a travel page is out of date the moment the calendar shifts. We are not going to guess one. Check the Department of Tourism’s own calendar at bhutan.travel, or ask the operator or hotel arranging your guide, and book early if a tsechu is the point of your trip — the valley fills.

What this means for the fee

One practical note that is easy to miss. The Sustainable Development Fee is charged per night, not per trip, so the length of your stay — not the season — is the biggest single lever on what Bhutan costs. A week in the quiet monsoon and a week in the crowded autumn carry exactly the same USD 700 per adult in fees. If you are choosing between a longer cheap-season trip and a shorter peak-season one, work that number first. Full detail is on the Bhutan guide.

Bhutan weather questions, answered

What is the best month to visit Bhutan?

October, with November close behind. The monsoon has finished, the air is as clear as it gets, the mountains are out, and most of the best known festivals fall in the autumn. Our ten-year figures put Thimphu's October afternoons near 64F (18C) with about 208 mm of rain across the month, against 633 mm in July. March to May is the other genuinely good window, drier than summer with rhododendron on the passes. The trade in autumn is company: it is the busiest season, and the valley hotels in Paro and Punakha fill early.

Is the monsoon a reason not to go?

Not a reason to cancel, but a reason to know what you are buying. The Indian monsoon runs from late June or July to late September and hits the southern hills hardest. Thimphu's wettest month in our ten-year figures is July at about 633 mm; Paro's is also July at about 592 mm. What that means on the ground is cloud over the high peaks, slower and less certain mountain roads, leeches on forest paths and landslide risk on the drives east. What you get in exchange is an empty, intensely green country and the lowest prices of the year. Several Bhutanese festivals fall in the summer too.

How cold does Bhutan get in winter?

It depends entirely which valley you are in, which is the single most useful thing to know about Bhutanese weather. Ten-year figures put January afternoons near 48F (9C) in Thimphu, 52F (11C) in Paro, 61F (16C) down in Punakha and 49F (10C) up in Bumthang, with Bumthang nights near 25F (-4C). Snow is common above about 3,000 metres and the high passes can close, so a winter itinerary normally stays in the western valleys and drops down to Punakha for the warm nights. Winter skies are often the clearest of the year.

When can you see the black-necked cranes?

In the Phobjikha valley in the winter half of the year. The cranes fly in from Tibet in the late autumn and leave again in early spring, and the valley - a wide, treeless bowl at around 3,000 metres below the Gangtey monastery - is the place they are most reliably seen. It is the one strong argument for a winter trip to Bhutan, and it pairs naturally with the warm nights down in Punakha. Accommodation up there is very limited, so it needs booking rather than improvising.

When is trekking season in Bhutan?

Spring and autumn, for the same reasons everywhere else in the Himalaya: the monsoon makes the trails wet and the views useless, and winter closes the high passes. March to May and late September to November are the windows, with the higher routes safest at the back end of the autumn. Remember that the rules are not optional here - the Department of Tourism requires every trek to be run by an accredited operator or guide, so this is a trip to arrange in advance rather than to improvise from a hotel in Paro.
