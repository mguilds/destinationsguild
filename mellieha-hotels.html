Mellieha

Where to Stay in Mellieha

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Mellieha is the north of Malta, and it is the part of the country that looks least like the rest of it. The island thins out above St Paul’s Bay into ridges and valleys with actual open ground between the villages, and Mellieha sits on top of one of those ridges with the largest sandy beach in Malta at the bottom of the hill. It is the usual choice for a beach holiday, for families, and for anyone who wants Gozo close at hand - the ferry at Cirkewwa is ten minutes up the road. The thing to understand before booking is the hill: the village and the bay are the same place on a map and a steep twenty-minute walk apart on the ground, and which of the two you book changes the shape of your days completely.

The village on the ridge

Real Maltese townBest viewsSteep walk to the beach

The old village runs along the top of the ridge around its parish church, with a view north over the bay to Gozo and south across half the island. This is where Mellieha’s own life is: grocers, bakeries, band clubs, a weekly market, restaurants that are not aimed at holidaymakers, and the air-raid shelters and the shrine cut into the rock beneath the church. Rooms up here are mostly small hotels and apartments and they are cheaper than the bay. The trade is the descent - it is a genuine hill with a bus running up and down it, and few people walk back up in August.

Mellieha Bay and Ghadira

The big sandy beachBest for familiesBusy in summer

Ghadira, at the bottom of the hill, is the longest stretch of sand in Malta and the one beach on the island where you can walk into shallow water for a long way, which is why families come. Hotels on and above the bay road put you a few minutes from the sand and the water sports, with buses to the village and to the Gozo ferry passing the door. In July and August it is crowded and the bay road is busy, and there is a nature reserve of salt marsh behind the beach that is worth an early walk. Out of season it empties completely.

Marfa Ridge and the far north

QuietestWalking countryCar or bus needed

The last ridge before the channel is open, rocky, windy country with small bays cut into it - Paradise Bay, Armier, Ramla tal-Qortin - a few seasonal restaurants and almost nothing else. The views across to Comino and Gozo are the best in Malta and the walking is the only proper hill walking the island offers. Accommodation here is thin and mostly self-catering. Come for the quiet and the coastline and accept that you will be using buses or a car for everything, and that a good deal of what is open in July is closed in January.

Around Cirkewwa and the Gozo ferry

Gozo on the doorstepDiving baseLittle else nearby

The ferry terminal at the top of the island is twenty-four hours and runs to Mgarr on Gozo in about twenty-five minutes, several times an hour through the day. The handful of large hotels out here are built for that convenience and for the diving - the reefs, the wrecks and the arch off this coast are the best known dive sites in the country. It is a practical address for a trip that treats Malta and Gozo as one holiday. It is not a place to walk out into: there is no village, and you are a bus ride from a shop.

St Paul’s Bay, Bugibba and Qawra, just south

Most roomsMost restaurantsBuilt-up

The connected towns on the next bay down hold far more beds and far more cheap eating than Mellieha, with a long rocky promenade, a national aquarium and buses in every direction. It is denser and plainer than Mellieha and there is no real beach, but it is noticeably cheaper, it has life out of season when Mellieha has little, and the big sandy bay is fifteen minutes up the road by bus. For a budget stay in the north, or for a longer visit where you want shops and choice, this is the practical alternative.

Getting around

Two things shape getting about in Mellieha: the hill, and the fact that you are at the far end of the island. Buses are frequent and cover the village, the bay, Cirkewwa and south to St Paul’s Bay and Bugibba, but the journey into Valletta takes about an hour and routes to the rest of the island often mean changing. This is the one part of Malta where hiring a car genuinely earns its keep, particularly for Marfa Ridge and the small northern bays, which the buses reach poorly or seasonally; parking is far easier here than anywhere in the south. Remember that Malta drives on the left. The Gozo ferry at Cirkewwa runs around the clock and takes about twenty-five minutes, and foot passengers pay on the return leg from Gozo rather than on the way out. Malta International Airport is about forty-five minutes away by road, longer in summer traffic.

When to come, and what it costs

Mellieha is a summer address and it behaves like one. From June to September the bay is warm, busy and fully open, with the sea swimmable from roughly June into November. July and August are hot - mid thirties - and Ghadira is crowded through the middle of the day, so swim early and late. May, early June and late September are the best weeks of all: warm water, open restaurants and room on the sand. From November the north goes quiet in a way the towns around Valletta do not: some hotels, restaurants and beach concessions close for the winter, the buses run less often, and what you have instead is excellent walking weather on Marfa Ridge and an empty beach. Check directly that your hotel and the places you want to eat are actually open if you are coming between November and March.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Malta is as straightforward as Europe gets for an American or Canadian visitor. It has been in the European Union since 2004, in the Schengen area since 2007 and on the euro since 2008, so an arrival from another Schengen country has no passport control at all, and an arrival from outside it is a single check at Malta International Airport. There is no land border to cross. The ferry between Malta and Sicily is an internal Schengen crossing; the one to Tunisia is not, and is checked.

Staying in Mellieha — questions

Village or bay - which should I book?

Book the bay if the beach is the point of the holiday, if you have small children, or if a steep twenty-minute hill is a problem; you will be minutes from the sand. Book the village if you want a real Maltese town around you in the evening, better views and lower prices, and you do not mind taking the bus down to the beach and back up again. The bus up the hill is frequent and cheap, so the village is not impractical - but almost nobody walks back up it in August, and it is worth being honest with yourself about that before you book on the view.

Is Mellieha Bay really the best beach in Malta?

It is the largest, and it is the best for families because the sand is wide and the water stays shallow a long way out. Whether it is the nicest is another matter: Golden Bay and Ghajn Tuffieha, round on the west coast, are smaller, prettier and backed by cliffs rather than a road, and Ramla Bay on Gozo has red sand and far fewer people. Malta’s coast is mostly rock, so all of these are genuinely busy in high summer. The practical answer is that Mellieha is the one you can walk to.

How easy is the day trip to Gozo from here?

Very easy, and it is the main reason to stay in the north. The Cirkewwa terminal is about ten minutes up the road, the crossing to Mgarr takes about twenty-five minutes and sailings run several times an hour through the day and right through the night. On Gozo, buses meet the ferry and run to Victoria, from where the rest of the island connects. As a day trip it works, although Gozo rewards a night or two: the island empties when the last of the day visitors go back.

Do I need a car in the north?

More than anywhere else in Malta, yes - though it is still optional. The buses serve Mellieha village, the bay, Bugibba and the ferry perfectly well. What they do not serve well is Marfa Ridge, the small northern bays and the west-coast beaches, several of which have thin or seasonal services. If your week is built around those, hire a car; parking in the north is much easier than in the south. If it is built around the one big beach and a Gozo trip, you do not need one.

What is there to do here when it rains?

Less than around Valletta, and that is the honest weakness of a northern base in winter. Within reach there is the national aquarium at Qawra, the Popeye Village film set above Anchor Bay, which is aimed squarely at children, the shelters and the rock-cut shrine under the Mellieha parish church, and the Red Tower on Marfa Ridge. For museums, temples and galleries you are taking the bus towards Valletta or Mdina, about an hour either way. In a wet week, stay nearer the capital.
