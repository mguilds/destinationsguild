Colombo

Where to Stay in Colombo

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Colombo is a working port city rather than a resort, and most visitors give it a night at the start or the end of a trip. Give it two and it repays them: a long sea-facing promenade, the best eating on the island, Dutch and British bones under a fast-rising skyline, and markets that have been trading for centuries. The practical thing to know first is that the airport is not here — it is 35 kilometres north, next to Negombo — so a late arrival need not mean a drive into town.

Fort and Galle Face — the old core and the sea wall

Landmark hotelsSeafront walkMost central

Fort is the colonial heart: the lighthouse clock tower, the old Dutch hospital turned restaurant arcade, banks and shipping offices in grand stone, and the president’s house behind the barriers. South of it the Galle Face Green runs along the sea, a kilometre of lawn where half the city walks at dusk and the kite sellers and prawn-fritter carts set up. The grandest hotels on the island look onto it, and the Port City reclamation is rising immediately offshore, which is either interesting or a building site depending on your mood. Stay here for the sunset walk and for being able to reach everything, and accept that Fort itself empties after office hours.

Kollupitiya and Colombo 3 — the practical middle

Best valueShops and restaurantsEasy tuk-tuks

Immediately south of Galle Face, Kollupitiya holds most of the city’s mid-range and business hotels, strung along the Galle Road with the sea on one side and the embassies and old houses on the other. It has the shopping, a dense run of restaurants from rice-and-curry shops to the smart places, two large malls and a halt on the coastal railway. Nothing here is picturesque and everything is convenient, which for a one-night stop is usually the right trade. Reckon ten minutes to Fort and fifteen to Cinnamon Gardens by tuk-tuk outside the rush.

Cinnamon Gardens, Colombo 7 — the quiet green one

QuietestBoutique housesMuseums

Colombo 7 is the leafy quarter the British laid out on old cinnamon plantations, and it is still the city’s most agreeable address: wide streets under big trees, the National Museum, Viharamahadevi Park, the Independence Memorial, and a scatter of small hotels and guest houses in converted family houses. It is the best choice if you want to sleep quietly and wake somewhere pleasant to walk, and the worst if you want to be in the middle of things, because the restaurants are spread out and you will take a tuk-tuk most evenings.

Mount Lavinia and the southern suburbs — a beach, at a price in time

SwimmingSunsets30-45 min out

Eleven kilometres south along the coast, Mount Lavinia has the nearest proper beach to the city, a headland with a view back up the shoreline and a long tradition of Sunday lunch and sunset drinks. It is cheaper than the centre, the sea breeze is real, and the coastal railway runs there from Fort in about half an hour for a few rupees — one of the better cheap pleasures in Sri Lanka. The honest trade-off is the road: by car at the wrong hour the same trip can take an hour, so it suits a traveller with time rather than one with an early flight. Swimming is for the dry months, December to April; the monsoon surf from May is strong and the beach narrows.

Getting around

Colombo has no metro, and the thing that shapes a stay here is traffic. The Galle Road corridor and the approaches to Fort are slow from about half past seven to ten in the morning and from four to seven in the evening, and a journey that takes twelve minutes at nine at night can take fifty at six. Plan around it rather than through it.

Tuk-tuks are the default, and the single most useful piece of advice for the city is to use an app — PickMe is the local one and Uber also operates — or to insist on the meter before you set off. A metered or app-hailed ride across the middle of town costs very little; a negotiated one costs several times that. Air-conditioned cars are easy to book through the same apps and through any hotel.

The coastal railway is the hidden convenience. It runs from Fort south through Kollupitiya, Bambalapitiya and Wellawatte to Mount Lavinia and on down the coast to Galle, it is absurdly cheap, it ignores the traffic entirely and it runs along the sea for much of the way. Carriages are crowded at commuter times and there is no reserved seat on the short local hops. For the airport, the fixed-price taxi desks in arrivals and pre-booked cars are the sensible choices; allow an hour and a half in the morning peak for those 35 kilometres.

When to come

Colombo sits on the wet western side of the island, so the southwest monsoon governs its year. December to March is the dry season and the best time to be here: sunny, hot, with the sea breeze along Galle Face and the lowest chance of an afternoon soaking. February is often the driest month of all.

May to September is the southwest monsoon. It rarely rains all day — the pattern is bright mornings and heavy afternoon downpours — but the sea gets rough, the humidity is constant and the streets flood briefly in the worst of it. October and November are the inter-monsoon weeks and the most unsettled of the year here, with thunderstorms arriving from either direction.

Heat and humidity are the constant rather than the rain: Colombo lies a few degrees off the equator and barely varies through the year, so do the walking early and late and keep the middle of the day for lunch and shade. Getting in

Most visitors need an Electronic Travel Authorisation, the ETA, arranged before you fly, and airlines check it. Sri Lanka has revised its entry rules, its fees and the list of nationalities more than once in recent years, including spells when some passports were admitted free of charge, so we deliberately do not print a figure or a list here — a number that is out of date is worse than no number. Read it off the Department of Immigration and Emigration’s own portal at immigration.gov.lk at the moment you book, and apply there rather than through one of the look-alike sites that charge a mark-up for filling in the same form. Your passport should have at least six months left on it and the officer may ask to see an onward ticket.

Two practical notes for the first hour. Draw rupees or change a little money at the airport, because tuk-tuks, markets and small restaurants want cash and small notes are useful. And remember the airport is not in Colombo: Bandaranaike International is at Katunayake, about 35 kilometres north of the capital and some ten minutes from Negombo, which is why so many itineraries begin and end there. Colombo hotels FAQ

How long is worth giving Colombo?

One night if it is a staging post, two if you want the city itself. Two gives you Fort and the old Dutch hospital, the Pettah markets, the National Museum, a walk on Galle Face at sunset and a proper dinner, which is the part most people wish they had allowed for. Sri Lanka's best eating is here by a comfortable margin, and almost nobody budgets an evening for it.

Is the airport in Colombo?

No, and this catches people out. Bandaranaike International is at Katunayake, about 35 kilometres north of the city and roughly ten minutes from Negombo. Allow an hour in light traffic and ninety minutes in the morning peak. If you land late or fly out early, a night in Negombo is the easier plan than a night in the city.

Which area should I pick for a single night?

Kollupitiya, or the Galle Face end of Fort. Both put you within a short tuk-tuk of everything, both have a wide choice of rooms at every price, and both let you walk the sea front in the evening without arranging transport. Cinnamon Gardens is nicer to wake up in and less convenient; Mount Lavinia is a beach visit rather than a city one.

How do I get around without being overcharged?

Use PickMe or Uber for tuk-tuks and cars, or insist on the meter before you set off. The apps settle the fare in advance, they work in English and they remove the whole negotiation. For journeys down the coast, the train from Fort is cheaper and faster than anything on the road.

Is Colombo safe to walk around?

Broadly yes, with the usual city sense: petty theft happens in crowds, and the Pettah bazaar is dense enough to keep a hand on your bag. Traffic is the real hazard rather than crime. Pavements are uneven and frequently missing, scooters use them, and crossings are advisory, so cross with locals rather than alone.
