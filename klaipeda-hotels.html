Klaipeda

Where to Stay in Klaipeda

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Klaipeda is Lithuania’s only seaport and its third city, on the narrow strait where the Curonian Lagoon opens into the Baltic. For most of its history it was Memel, the northernmost city of East Prussia, and the half-timbered warehouses along the river Dane and the merchants’ street grid behind them are what is left of that. It is flat, small enough to walk, and the one place in the country where a visitor can be in an old town in the morning and on a sand dune in a national park by lunchtime — the passenger ferry to the Curonian Spit leaves from the end of the main street.

The Old Town and the Theatre Square

Most centralHalf-timbered warehousesWalk to the ferry

Klaipeda’s old town is small, low and unmistakably German: half-timbered warehouses along the Dane, a grid of streets laid out for merchants, and the Theatre Square with its little fountain at the middle of it. This was Memel, the northernmost city of East Prussia, until 1923, and the architecture is the honest remainder of that. Most of the restaurants and the smaller hotels are here, the passenger ferry for the Curonian Spit leaves from the quay at the end of the street, and everything a short visit wants is within ten minutes on foot. It is flat.

The Dane riverside and the quays

Cruise quayBest for a short stopLevel

The river Dane runs through the middle of the city to the strait, with tall ships and small boats moored along it, the castle museum and the old moat on the seaward side, and the cruise quay a short walk further. For anybody arriving by ship or passing through for a night this is the obvious place to be: level, central, a few minutes from the Old Town and from the ferry. Rooms range from converted warehouses to plain modern hotels, and the water makes up for the architecture where the architecture is ordinary.

The new town, south and east

Best valueLarger hotelsBuses in

South and east of the river the city turns twentieth-century: wide streets, apartment blocks, the bigger hotels and shopping centres, and the bus and railway stations. This is where the value is, and where a visitor arriving with a car will find somewhere to put it. The walk into the Old Town is ten to twenty minutes depending where you land, and buses are frequent. Plain rather than pretty, and perfectly sensible for a family or a longer stay.

Melnrage and Giruliai: the northern beaches

BeachQuietBus or bike in

A few kilometres north of the centre the city runs out into pine woods and a long sand beach, with the northern pier at Melnrage and a quieter stretch again at Giruliai. It is a proper swimming and walking beach rather than a resort strip, the water is Baltic-cold, and the accommodation is guesthouses and apartments among the trees. Buses and a cycle path run in to town in a quarter of an hour. Smiltyne and the spit: sleep here, cross for the day

Smiltyne, directly across the strait, is the northern tip of the Curonian Spit: the Sea Museum in an old fort, the beaches, the pine forest and the start of the national park. A passenger ferry crosses from the Old Town quay in a few minutes and runs all day. There is almost nothing to book on Smiltyne itself, and we will not send you to try. Sleep in Klaipeda and cross for the day, or go properly down the spit and stay at Nida — our guide to that is Where to Stay in Nida and the Curonian Spit.

Getting around Klaipeda

On foot

The Old Town, the riverside and the quays are flat and tightly packed, and you will walk the lot without thinking about it. The distances only get awkward if you stay out at the beaches or south of the stations.

The ferries to the spit

Two of them. The passenger ferry from the Old Town quay crosses to Smiltyne in a few minutes and is the one most visitors want. The vehicle ferry leaves from a separate terminal south of the city and lands you on the spit road for Nida. Sailings are frequent in summer and thinner out of season, and the vehicle ferry queues can be long on a July weekend, so go early.

Buses

City buses cover Klaipeda and reach the northern beaches, Palanga up the coast and the villages inland. Tickets come from kiosks, machines or the app. Services to the beaches thin out in the evening, which is worth knowing before you plan a late dinner in town.

Airports, trains and coaches

The nearest airport is at Palanga, about thirty kilometres up the coast, with buses and taxis in. Vilnius and Kaunas airports carry more routes and are a long way off. Trains and coaches from Vilnius take roughly four hours either way, which is why the coast is better treated as a separate stay than as a day out.

Taxis and apps

Ride-hailing apps work in the city and out to the beaches and the airport, and are the simplest way to get a fair price. For street taxis use a marked rank and look for the fare card.

When to come

The coast has a shorter season than the rest of Lithuania, and the difference is sharper. June is long-lit, warm enough and still quiet, and is the best single month here. July and August are the only time the sea is swimmable and the only time the city is genuinely full; the Sea Festival in late July takes every room. September is warm, empty and our own pick for the spit. October to April is wind, rain and a grey sea, which is bleak if you came for a beach and magnificent if you came for a long walk and an empty hotel. The amber is easiest to find on the sand after a winter storm, which is the reason local people walk the beaches in February.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Lithuania is in the European Union, the Schengen area and the euro, so arriving from anywhere else in Schengen there is no check at all. Month-by-month weather and the dates worth planning around are in Best Time to Visit Lithuania, and the country guide is The Lithuania Travel Guide.

Klaipeda — questions

Which part of Klaipeda should I stay in?

The Old Town or the Dane riverside for almost everybody: both are flat, central and a few minutes from the ferry. The new town south and east if you want value, space or somewhere to park. Melnrage if the beach is the point and you do not mind a bus. There is effectively nothing to book across the water at Smiltyne.

Is Klaipeda worth staying in, or should I go straight to Nida?

Both, in that order, if you have the days. Klaipeda deserves a night: the German old town, the castle museum, the ships and the sea festival atmosphere are all genuinely worth it, and the ferry means you can see Smiltyne without moving hotels. Nida is a different thing altogether — quieter, stranger and better for two or three nights than for one. A short trip that takes only one of the two should usually take Nida.

What is left of German Memel?

More than the guidebooks let on. The street plan of the Old Town, the half-timbered warehouses along the river, a good deal of nineteenth-century brick, the little fountain on the Theatre Square and the Lutheran churches are all from the city’s long centuries as the northernmost town of East Prussia. The Second World War and the Soviet decades that followed took much of the rest. The museums in the castle and in the town are straightforward about all of it.

Can I swim here?

Yes, in high summer, and you should be ready for it to be cold. The beaches at Melnrage and Giruliai and the sand at Smiltyne are clean, broad and backed by pine, and the Baltic here warms up only in July and August, and only for a swimmer who commits. Out of season it is a walking beach, which is arguably better.

How long do I need?

One full day and a night covers the Old Town, the castle museum, the quays and a crossing to Smiltyne. Two lets you add the northern beaches, the sculpture park and a slower look at the museums. Longer than that and you should be spending the extra nights down the spit at Nida instead.

When is the city busiest?

Late July, when the Sea Festival fills the whole waterfront and every room in the city, and through August while the beaches are usable. Dates shift from year to year, so check before you fix travel: it is a wonderful weekend to be here and an impossible one to find a bed in. June and September are warm enough and far quieter.

Is the ferry included in anything, or do I pay?

You pay, on the day, and it is a small fare for the passenger crossing and a larger one for a vehicle. Fares and timetables change by season, so check them at the quay rather than taking a figure off an old page — including ours, which is why we do not print one.
