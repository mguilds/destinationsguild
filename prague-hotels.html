Where to Stay in Prague

Where to Stay in Prague — Best Hotels & Areas

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated August 2026

Prague is a small city pretending to be a large one. Almost everything a visitor comes for sits inside a walkable oval either side of the Vltava, which means the choice of district matters less here than in most capitals — and the choice of building matters a great deal more. What separates a good Prague stay from a trying one is usually stairs, noise and cobbles rather than location. Choosing Your District

Five neighbourhoods carry almost all the good addresses. Here is the honest lay of the land.

Old Town, castle side, or the quiet residential quarters?

OrientationWalkabilityNoiseStairs

The Old Town (Staré Město) puts you inside the picture — the Astronomical Clock, the square, the bridge — and it is flat. The costs are noise, crowds and a great many protected buildings with no lift. Malá Strana, across the river beneath the castle, is the prettiest place to sleep in the city and the most demanding underfoot: baroque lanes on a slope, all of it cobbled. The New Town (Nové Město) around Wenceslas Square is flat, central, better served by trams and metro, and generally better value; parts of it are scruffy after dark. Josefov, the old Jewish quarter, is quiet, elegant, expensive and beautifully placed between the Old Town and the river. Vinohrady, ten minutes out by tram, is where Prague residents actually live — leafy, level, full of good restaurants, and the choice we would make for a stay of a week or more.

Luxury

Prague’s top tier is small, riverside and mostly housed in historic buildings — which is the pleasure and occasionally the problem.

Four Seasons Hotel Prague

$$$$ LuxuryOld Town riversideRiver and castle viewsFull accessibility

The address most people mean when they say the best hotel in Prague — a set of linked historic buildings directly on the Vltava, with the castle framed across the water from the better rooms. It is the rare Prague luxury hotel that is genuinely modern behind the facade, with proper lifts and level access, which matters more here than the star rating. Ask specifically for a river-facing room; the difference between the two aspects is the whole reason to stay.

Boutique & Character

Small, particular hotels — where Prague is at its strongest and where the stairs question matters most.

Golden Well (U Zlaté Studně)

$$$ UpperMalá Strana, below the castleTerrace viewSmall

A former Renaissance house tucked into the hillside directly beneath the castle gardens, with a rooftop terrace that has one of the finest views in the city. It is small, personal and very quiet. Two honest cautions: the approach is up a steep cobbled lane, and the building is historic and compact. If you are steady on your feet it is a wonderful address; if you are not, choose something flat instead.

Budget & Value

Prague still offers real value, and the honest bargains are in the New Town rather than the Old.

Grandium Hotel Prague

$$ ValueNew Town, near the main stationLargeLift and level access

A big, comfortable, unfussy hotel close to the main railway station and a short flat walk or one tram stop from Wenceslas Square. It is the practical choice: proper lifts, luggage handling, a decent breakfast and a rate well below anything comparable in the Old Town. Not characterful, and does not claim to be. Adults-only — and an honest word

Prague has essentially no formally adults-only hotels. Rather than invent one, here are two genuinely grown-up, quiet addresses.

Aria Hotel Prague

$$$$ LuxuryMalá StranaMusic themeGarden access

A small music-themed luxury hotel beside the Vrtba Garden in Malá Strana, with a rooftop terrace looking to the castle. It is emphatically an adult sort of place — hushed, indulgent and built around evenings rather than mornings — though it is not formally adults-only and does accept children. If you want quiet and polish rather than a policy, this is the address.

Prague Hotel FAQ

Which district should I stay in for a first visit to Prague?

The Old Town or the New Town near Wenceslas Square. Both are flat, both put you within a ten-minute walk of nearly everything, and the New Town is materially cheaper. Choose Malá Strana instead only if the beauty of the setting outweighs a cobbled uphill walk each time you come home.

Is Prague walkable, and how bad are the cobbles?

Very walkable and genuinely cobbled. The whole historic core is laid with rounded granite setts that are uneven and slippery in the wet. This is the single most common cause of trouble for visitors. Bring shoes with a real sole and grip, and if you use a stick at home, bring it.

Do Prague hotels have lifts?

Many historic ones do not, or have a small lift that starts on the first floor after a flight of stairs. Protected buildings often cannot be altered. This is rarely stated plainly in a listing, so ask the hotel directly: is there a lift, and how many steps are there from the street to it?

How do I get from the airport into the city?

Václav Havel Airport is about half an hour west of the centre. The Airport Express bus runs to the main railway station and is inexpensive and straightforward; public buses connect to the metro; taxis and ride-hailing are readily available. There is no rail link direct from the terminal.

Should I worry about noise?

In parts of the Old Town and around Wenceslas Square, yes — Prague has a lively and sometimes raucous nightlife and sound carries in narrow streets. Ask for a courtyard-facing room, a higher floor, or choose Josefov or Vinohrady, which are quiet after dark.

Is it cheaper to stay outside the centre?

Meaningfully, yes, and Prague is one of the few capitals where doing so costs you almost nothing in convenience. Vinohrady, Žižkov and Karlín are all within ten to fifteen minutes of the centre by tram or metro, cost considerably less, and are where you will eat better.

What currency will I be charged in?

Czech koruna. Cards are accepted almost everywhere. If a hotel or card machine offers to charge you in US dollars instead of koruna, decline and pay in koruna — the built-in conversion rate is almost always worse than your own bank’s.
