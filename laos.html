Laos Travel Guide

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

The quiet one. Laos has no coastline, no mass-market resort strip and no intention of acquiring either, and travellers who come here from Thailand or Vietnam almost always say the same thing afterwards — that it was the part of the trip where they slowed down. The Mekong runs the length of it. Luang Prabang, a whole town of gilded temples and shuttered shophouses between two rivers, is the reason most people come. Beyond it there are limestone towers at Vang Vieng, the quietest capital in Southeast Asia at Vientiane, coffee country on the Bolaven Plateau, and a stretch in the far south where the river spreads out into islands. This is the Guild’s chapter on it.

Why visit Laos

Four reasons the Guild sends travellers here, in the order people tell us about them afterwards.

Luang Prabang

A small town of thirty-odd working monasteries on a peninsula between the Mekong and the Nam Khan, listed by UNESCO in 1995 and still a place where monks sweep the courtyards at six in the morning rather than a museum of itself.

The pace

Nothing here is in a hurry, including you after about three days. It is the quality visitors name first, and the reason people who have done the Southeast Asia circuit often rate Laos the best part of it.

Rivers and mountains

The Mekong the length of the country, the Nam Ou through gorges in the north, limestone towers at Vang Vieng and Nong Khiaw, the tiered turquoise pools at Kuang Si, and waterfalls off the edge of the Bolaven Plateau.

Very good value

One of the least expensive countries in the region for a comfortable trip: good rooms, excellent French-Lao cooking in the old towns, and a car with a driver for the day at a price that makes the mountain roads worth taking slowly.

The regions of Laos

Laos is long and thin and mostly mountain, and the country changes completely from one end of it to the other. Towns with a Guild guide are marked; the rest link straight to hotels through our Trip.com partner while their chapters are written.

How the country divides up

Luang Prabang & the north

Luang Prabang sits on a tongue of land where the Nam Khan joins the Mekong: gilded roofs, French shophouses, the royal palace, and Wat Xieng Thong at the tip of the peninsula. Out of town are the tiered pools of Kuang Si and the caves at Pak Ou. North of it the country climbs into mountains — Nong Khiaw on the Nam Ou under limestone cliffs, trekking country around Luang Namtha, and Huay Xai on the Thai border where the Mekong slow boat starts.

Vientiane, Vang Vieng & the centre

The capital is low-rise, French-planned and startlingly calm: the golden stupa of Pha That Luang, the Patuxai arch, a long Mekong promenade facing Thailand, and the COPE centre explaining the bombing and its clearance. North on the river road, Vang Vieng sits under a wall of karst towers. East in the highlands, the Plain of Jars around Phonsavan — thousands of carved stone vessels of uncertain purpose — became the country’s third World Heritage site in 2019.

Savannakhet & the Mekong centre-south

Savannakhet is the country’s second city and the most under-visited good thing in it: a square of crumbling French shophouses around a church, almost no other travellers, and a bridge straight over to Thailand. Inland from Thakhek, the limestone is riddled with caves and river passages, and a well-known motorbike loop runs out through them — including the 7.5-kilometre river cave at Kong Lor.

Pakse, the Bolaven & the Four Thousand Islands

Pakse is the junction of the south and the base for everything around it. Up on the Bolaven Plateau it is cool and green and planted with coffee, with waterfalls dropping off the rim. South of town, Vat Phou is a pre-Angkorian Khmer temple climbing a hillside, listed by UNESCO in 2001. Further down, the Mekong widens and breaks into Si Phan Don, the Four Thousand Islands, which is where travellers go to stop moving altogether.

Top things to do in Laos

The experiences worth building a trip around.

Wat Xieng Thong at opening time

The finest temple in Luang Prabang, with its roofs sweeping almost to the ground and a tree of life in coloured glass on the back wall. Go at eight, before the tour groups come up from the river.

Kuang Si Falls

Tiered limestone pools of an improbable turquoise in forest half an hour outside Luang Prabang. Walk up to the top fall, and come early or late — the middle of the day is busy and the light is flat.

The Mekong slow boat

Two days downriver from Huay Xai on the Thai border to Luang Prabang, with a night at Pakbeng in between. It is long, it is wooden, and it is the best way anyone arrives in Laos.

Balloons over Vang Vieng

Up at dawn over the Nam Song with the karst towers coming out of the mist below. Vang Vieng’s wild river-bar years ended in 2012; ballooning, caving and kayaking are what the town is about now.

The Plain of Jars

Thousands of carved stone jars scattered across the uplands around Phonsavan, nobody entirely certain why. Cleared paths are marked — walk between the markers and nowhere else.

Coffee on the Bolaven Plateau

A cool green plateau above Pakse, planted with arabica and robusta, with Tad Fane and other falls dropping off its edge. Several farms will show you the whole process and then make you a cup of it.

Best time to visit & getting there

When to go

November to February is the cool dry season and the best of the year: warm days, genuinely cool mornings in the north, and clear air. March to May is dry and hot — and carries the one seasonal problem worth planning around, the agricultural burning haze that can settle over the northern valleys from roughly March into April and take the mountains out of the view for weeks. June to October is the green season: heavy afternoon rain, waterfalls at full volume, brilliant country and the lowest prices.

Two festivals are worth knowing before you fix dates. Pi Mai, Lao New Year, falls in mid-April and turns into a nationwide water fight; much of the country stops. Boun That Luang fills Vientiane around the November full moon. Both follow the lunar calendar and move from year to year.

See our month-by-month guide to the best time to visit Laos for weather, haze, crowds and the festivals through the year.

Getting there

Three international gateways: Wattay International Airport (VTE) at Vientiane, Luang Prabang International Airport (LPQ) and Pakse International Airport (PKZ). There are no direct flights from North America; almost everyone connects through Bangkok, Hanoi, Singapore, Seoul or Kuala Lumpur. Many visitors also arrive overland from Thailand across one of the Friendship Bridges, or by slow boat from Huay Xai.

The Laos–China Railway opened on 3 December 2021 and changed travel inside the country more than anything else in a generation. It runs north from Vientiane through Vang Vieng and Luang Prabang to Boten on the Chinese border, turning long days on mountain roads into a short ride. Seats are limited and popular departures sell out, so book ahead rather than turning up; the Vientiane terminus for the line is well outside the city centre, so allow for the transfer at both ends.

United States passport holders need a visa, applied for through the official Lao government eVisa service or obtained on arrival. The eVisa is accepted at nine designated ports only, and arriving anywhere else means paying again for a visa on arrival — the FAQ below lists the nine. Use only the official government site.

Laos travel FAQ

When is the best time to visit Laos?

November to February is the cool dry season and the best weather of the year: warm days, cool mornings, clear air and low river mist on the Mekong. It is also the busiest stretch. March to May is dry and very hot, and from roughly March into April the farming haze and field burning across the north can grey out the views around Luang Prabang and Vang Vieng for weeks — the one thing most likely to disappoint a first visit. The green season from June to October brings heavy afternoon rain, full waterfalls, brilliant countryside and the lowest prices.

Do Americans need a visa for Laos?

Yes. United States passport holders need a tourist visa. It is issued online through the Lao government's own eVisa service at laoevisa.gov.la, or on arrival at designated checkpoints. The eVisa is single entry, allows a stay of 30 days, is normally processed within three working days, and the approval letter stays valid for 60 days after it is issued. Your passport must be valid for more than 180 days from the date of arrival. You must print the approval letter — immigration does not accept it on a phone screen. Confirm the current rules with the official Lao authority before you book, and apply only through the government site.

Can I use my eVisa at any border?

No, and this is the detail that strands people. The Lao eVisa is accepted at nine designated ports only: Wattay International Airport and Lao-Thai Friendship Bridge I at Vientiane, Luang Prabang International Airport, Lao-Thai Friendship Bridge II at Savannakhet, Pakse International Airport, Lao-Thai Friendship Bridge IV in Bokeo, Boten International Checkpoint and Boten Railway Station in Luang Namtha, and Khamsavath Railway Station in Vientiane. Arrive anywhere else and you must apply for a visa on arrival instead, and the eVisa fee you already paid is not refunded. If you are crossing at a small land border, check the current list before you pay for anything.

How many days do you need in Laos?

Ten days is the comfortable shape: four nights in Luang Prabang, two in Vang Vieng, two in Vientiane, and two more either in the far north or flown south to Pakse. With a week, do Luang Prabang properly and add Vang Vieng, and leave the south for another trip. Laos rewards going slowly more than almost anywhere in Southeast Asia, and the commonest mistake is trying to see the whole country in the time it takes to see a third of it.

Is Laos safe, and what about the unexploded bombs?

Laos is a calm, low-crime country and ordinary tourism is straightforward. The unexploded ordnance is real but it is a rural hazard, not a town one. Laos was the most heavily bombed country in history relative to its population, around eighty million submunitions failed to explode, and clearance work continues across much of the countryside; unexploded ordnance still kills or injures roughly fifty people a year, almost all of them Lao people farming or scavenging metal. For a visitor the rule is simple and sufficient: stay on marked paths and well-used tracks, do not walk into fields or dig anywhere off the trail, and never pick up or move a metal object you find on the ground. The COPE visitor centre in Vientiane explains the history and the clearance work honestly and is worth an hour.
