United Arab Emirates

United Arab Emirates Travel Guide

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Seven emirates, federated in 1971, occupying a corner of Arabia about the size of Portugal — and almost all of the travel writing about them is really about one of them. Dubai is the reason most visitors come, and it earns the attention: the tallest building on earth, a creek still worked by wooden dhows, and a hotel industry that invented several categories of its own. But Abu Dhabi holds the capital, the Grand Mosque and the museums; Sharjah, ten minutes from Dubai’s airport, is the cultural emirate and a dry one; Ras Al Khaimah has the country’s mountains; and Fujairah faces the Gulf of Oman on the other coast entirely, with reefs instead of skylines. This guide covers where to sleep in each of them, what the seasons genuinely do to a trip here, and the practical and legal matters that catch visitors out.

Why visit the United Arab Emirates

A city built in one lifetime

Dubai is the most-photographed new city on earth for a reason. The Burj Khalifa stands 828 metres, nearly twice the height of the Empire State Building, and the view from it takes in desert on one side and an artificial archipelago on the other. Within living memory this was a pearling and trading port of a few thousand people.

The old Gulf is still there

It takes finding, but it survives: the wind-tower houses of Al Fahidi, the wooden abra boats that still ferry people across Dubai Creek for a few fils, the gold and spice souks, the dhow wharfage piled with cargo for Iran and East Africa, and the restored heart of Sharjah.

Desert and mountains, within the hour

The dunes begin where the suburbs stop, and an evening in them — the light, the quiet, the cold after dark — is the part of the trip most people remember. Inland and north the Hajar mountains rise to almost two thousand metres at Jebel Jais in Ras Al Khaimah.

Two coasts, two different seas

Most of the country faces the Arabian Gulf, warm and shallow and lined with resorts. But Fujairah sits on the far side of the mountains on the Gulf of Oman, where the water is deeper and clearer and the diving is the reason to go rather than an amenity.

The UAE at a glance

Capital

Abu Dhabi, which is also the largest emirate by far — some four-fifths of the country’s land area and the great majority of its oil. Dubai is the larger city and the one most visitors mean when they say they are going to the UAE.

Money

The UAE dirham, written AED or Dhs and divided into 100 fils. It has been pegged to the US dollar for decades at a rate of about 3.67 dirhams to the dollar, which makes American budgeting unusually easy. Cards are taken almost everywhere; decline any terminal offering to charge you in your home currency.

Language

Arabic is the official language, but the country is largely made up of residents from elsewhere and English is the everyday language of business, hotels, taxis and signage. Hindi, Urdu, Malayalam, Tagalog and Farsi are all widely spoken. No visitor needs any Arabic.

The working week

Federal government and schools work Monday to Friday with a short Friday, and the weekend is Saturday and Sunday. Sharjah’s public sector takes a three-day weekend from Friday. Private businesses vary, and Friday midday prayers quieten everything for an hour or so wherever you are.

Getting about

Driving is on the right, the motorways are excellent and the traffic is quick; speed and lane discipline are camera-enforced and the fines reach the hire company. Taxis are metered and cheap, Dubai’s metro is driverless and useful, and intercity coaches connect all seven emirates for a few dirhams.

Shape of it

About 83,600 square kilometres — a little smaller than Portugal — with roughly 1,300 kilometres of coast. Desert over most of it, salt flats along the Gulf, the Hajar mountains in the east and north, and the Liwa oases on the edge of the Empty Quarter in the south.

The seven emirates

All seven are within a few hours of one another, and the differences between them are larger than the distances suggest. Where the Guild has written a full guide the link goes to it; otherwise it opens that town’s hotels through our Trip.com partner.

Dubai

The one everybody means. A ninety-kilometre strip of coast with the Marina and Jumeirah beaches at one end, the Burj Khalifa and the old Creek quarters in the middle, and the desert conservation reserve behind. Hatta, a mountain enclave with a dam and walking trails, belongs to Dubai but sits two hours inland.

Sharjah, Ajman and Umm Al Quwain

The three northern emirates that run straight on from Dubai, and the reason a cheap room in “Dubai” sometimes turns out to be in another emirate entirely. Sharjah is the cultural capital of the federation, with the restored Heart of Sharjah and a serious set of museums — and no alcohol anywhere in it. Ajman and Umm Al Quwain are small, quiet and considerably cheaper.

Things to do in the UAE

Go up the Burj Khalifa

The observation decks are on the 124th, 125th and 148th floors, and the queue is much shorter and the light much better if you book a timed slot online rather than turning up. Sunset slots cost more and sell out first. The tower sits above the Dubai Mall and the fountain, so the evening arranges itself.

The Sheikh Zayed Grand Mosque

Abu Dhabi’s white marble mosque holds tens of thousands of worshippers and is open to non-Muslim visitors outside prayer times, at no charge. Dress is strictly enforced and the mosque lends what is needed. Go late in the day, when the stone turns from white to gold to blue.

Cross Dubai Creek by abra

The best-value hour in the country. A wooden abra across the Creek costs small change, lands you between the gold souk on the Deira side and the spice souk and old quarter on the Bur Dubai side, and shows you the city the skyline replaced.

A night in the desert

Dune drives, camel rides and a dinner camp are sold on every hotel desk and vary wildly in quality and in how they treat the animals and the dunes. The quieter alternative is a stay inside one of the desert conservation reserves, where the oryx and gazelle are the point rather than the backdrop.

Louvre Abu Dhabi and Saadiyat

Jean Nouvel’s domed museum on Saadiyat Island opened in 2017 and is worth a half-day on its own; the dome’s “rain of light” is the building’s real exhibit. Saadiyat’s beaches, where turtles nest, are a short drive from it.

Dive the east coast

Fujairah and Khor Fakkan face the Gulf of Oman, where the water is clearer and cooler than the Gulf side and the reefs and wrecks are genuinely good. Snoopy Island off Al Aqah is the easy shore dive and snorkel that everyone starts with.

Best time to visit & getting there

November to March is the season, and the gap between it and the rest of the year is wider here than almost anywhere the Guild writes about. In those months the days are warm and dry, the evenings are pleasant enough to eat outside, and the beaches, deserts and mountains are all usable. December to February is the peak, with the highest room rates of the year at the beach resorts and a scramble over the Christmas and New Year weeks. April and October are hot but manageable if you plan around the middle of the day. From June to September the heat is the trip: temperatures in the forties Celsius, humidity on the coast that makes it feel worse, and sightseeing reduced to short moves between air-conditioned places. Hotel rates collapse accordingly, which is a real offer if a pool and a mall are what you came for. Rain is rare and falls mostly between December and March, but when it comes it can be heavy enough to flood roads. There is a fuller month-by-month account on the best time to visit page.

Dubai International (DXB) is one of the busiest airports in the world and the easiest way in from almost anywhere, with the emirate’s own airline flying nonstop to every continent. Zayed International (AUH) serves Abu Dhabi and is the quieter, calmer arrival; its new terminal opened in 2023 and the airport was renamed in 2024, so older signage and guidebooks still call it Abu Dhabi International. Sharjah (SHJ) and Ras Al Khaimah (RKT) take the low-cost traffic, and a cheap fare into either is often worth it — but check the transfer distance to your hotel before booking, because they are in different emirates from the one you may think you are staying in. The two big airports are about ninety minutes apart by road, and flying between them makes no sense; take a taxi or a coach.

Fishing here

The UAE fishes both of its coasts, and they are different propositions. The Arabian Gulf side, off Dubai and Abu Dhabi, is shallow and warm, and the charter trade out of the marinas runs for queenfish, cobia, barracuda and the prized local hamour, a grouper. The Gulf of Oman side at Fujairah drops into deeper water much closer to shore, which brings sailfish, tuna and kingfish within reach of a morning charter — this is the serious sport-fishing coast of the two. Fishing from the shore and from boats is regulated: recreational fishing requires a permit from the local authority, there are closed seasons and size limits on several species, and the hamour in particular is heavily managed because the Gulf stock has been overfished. The practical answer for a visitor is to go out with a licensed charter, which carries the paperwork as part of the trip. Travelling in your later years

The heat is the whole question

Everything else here is easy; the temperature is not. From June to September the heat is a genuine health risk for anyone with a heart or respiratory condition, and even in the shoulder months the middle of the day is best spent indoors. Come between November and March if you have the choice, carry water everywhere, and plan the day around early mornings and evenings.

Newer than anywhere, and it shows

Because most of the country was built in the last forty years, step-free access is the norm rather than the exception: hotels, malls, museums, the Dubai metro and the airports are all designed for lifts and ramps, and staff are plentiful and quick to help. Wheelchairs are easy to hire. This is one of the more straightforward destinations in the world for limited mobility.

The old quarters and the dunes are not

The exceptions are the parts worth seeing: the souks and the wind-tower lanes are uneven underfoot and often without shade, the abra boats are a step down into a rocking hull with no handrail, and a dune drive is a violent ride that no one with a back, neck or heart complaint should be talked into. Ask for a sedate desert tour, which exists.

Medicines need checking first

This matters more here than in most places. A number of medicines sold freely elsewhere, including some painkillers, sleeping tablets and psychiatric drugs, are controlled in the UAE and need prior approval to bring in; travellers have been detained over quantities in a suitcase. Carry everything in its original labelled packaging with a copy of the prescription, and check your specific medicines against the official UAE list well before you fly.

Getting in: what to expect at the border

For most Western visitors entry is unusually quick. Citizens of the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, the European Union and a long list of other countries are admitted on arrival without applying for anything in advance, with the permitted length of stay depending on nationality; there is no fee and no form to fill in beforehand. Passport control at Dubai and Abu Dhabi is increasingly done by camera at a gate rather than by an officer at a desk, and the queues move fast. Your passport should be valid for at least six months beyond your arrival date, and the airline will check that before it lets you board.

Everyone else needs a visa arranged before travel, normally sponsored by an airline, a hotel or a relative. The categories, the costs and the eligible nationalities all change, and so does the length of the stay granted on arrival, so confirm the current rules for your own passport with the official UAE authority or your nearest UAE embassy or consulate before you book anything. Do not rely on a guide, including this one, for a rule that decides whether you are allowed on the aircraft.

Two practical points that are not visa matters but belong here. First, customs: the UAE is strict about what comes in, and the list of prohibited items is longer than most travellers expect — it includes some medicines, e-cigarettes of certain kinds, and material considered offensive. Second, alcohol allowances apply only to arrivals in emirates that permit it, and there is no duty-free allowance into Sharjah. If you are driving between emirates, remember that transporting alcohol through Sharjah is an offence even if you bought it legally in Dubai.

The United Arab Emirates — questions

When is the best time to visit the United Arab Emirates?

November to March, without much argument. Those months bring warm, dry days, cool evenings and sea temperatures that are pleasant rather than bathwater, and they are when the outdoor life of the country actually happens. December through February is the peak, and rates at the beach resorts reflect it. April and October are shoulder months, hot but workable. June to September is genuinely severe: daytime temperatures in the forties Celsius with high humidity on the coast, which turns sightseeing into a series of dashes between air-conditioned buildings. Hotel prices fall a long way in those months, which is the trade on offer. There is a fuller account on our best time to visit page.

Do I need a visa for the United Arab Emirates?

Many nationalities, including citizens of the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom and the European Union, are granted entry on arrival without applying in advance, with the length of stay depending on nationality. Others must arrange a visa before travelling, usually through an airline, hotel or sponsor. Entry rules change and the details differ by passport, so confirm the current position for your own nationality with the official UAE authority or your nearest UAE mission before you book, rather than relying on any guide including this one.

Dubai or Abu Dhabi — where should I base myself?

Dubai if you want the spectacle, the shopping, the beach clubs and the broadest choice of hotels and restaurants; Abu Dhabi if you want museums, the Grand Mosque, a calmer pace and rather better value for the same standard of room. They are about ninety minutes apart by road, so a first trip can reasonably do both, and many visitors split a week between them. If you only have three or four nights, pick one and take a day trip to the other rather than moving hotel.

What do I need to know about alcohol, dress and Ramadan?

Alcohol is served in licensed hotels, restaurants and bars in most of the country, but it is not sold or served in Sharjah at all, which catches people out when they book a cheaper room there. Public drunkenness is an offence. Dress is relaxed on hotel beaches and at pools and more conservative elsewhere: covered shoulders and knees are the safe standard in malls, souks and public buildings, and mosques require considerably more, with the Sheikh Zayed Grand Mosque providing the necessary garments. During Ramadan, eating, drinking and smoking in public during daylight hours is restricted, and hours change at restaurants and attractions. The dates move each year.

How do you travel between the emirates?

By road, for the most part. The emirates are strung along a good motorway network and a hire car is the most flexible way to see more than one; driving is on the right and the standard of road is high, though the traffic is fast and the fines are automated. Taxis are metered, plentiful and inexpensive by European or American standards, and ride-hailing works in the cities. Dubai has a driverless metro that is genuinely useful along the Sheikh Zayed Road corridor. Intercity coaches link Dubai with Abu Dhabi, Sharjah and the northern emirates cheaply and reliably.

Is the United Arab Emirates safe, and which laws catch visitors out?

Street crime is very rare and most visitors find the country exceptionally safe to walk around at any hour. The risk here is legal rather than criminal, and it is worth taking seriously. There is zero tolerance of illegal drugs, including trace amounts and substances that are legal or sold over the counter elsewhere, and some prescription medicines require prior approval to bring in. Public displays of affection, swearing and rude gestures, drinking outside licensed premises and photographing people without consent are all offences that have led to arrests of visitors. Same-sex relations are criminalised. Debt and unpaid cheques are treated as criminal matters. None of this makes the country dangerous to visit, and millions go every year without incident, but the margin for error is smaller than at home. Read your own government's current travel advice before you go.
