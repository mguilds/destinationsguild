Before you go

The Everglades is close to Miami but nothing like it, and a little planning makes the difference between a sweaty disappointment and a wonderful day.

Everglades National Park has three separate entrances, and they do not connect. The main entrance near Homestead leads to the Ernest F. Coe Visitor Center, the Anhinga Trail and, at the end of the Main Park Road, Flamingo on Florida Bay. Shark Valley sits on the Tamiami Trail (U.S. 41) west of Miami. The Gulf Coast area at Everglades City, on the southwest side, is the gateway to the Ten Thousand Islands. The park says the three entrances and Flamingo are each at least an hour's drive apart, so pick one or two for a day, not all three.

Season matters more here than in almost any other park. The dry season, typically December through April, brings mild temperatures, fewer mosquitoes, a full schedule of ranger programs and wildlife gathered around the remaining water. The wet season, May through November, is hot and humid, with daily storms and mosquitoes that can make a trail walk miserable. Even in late November the concessioner at Flamingo was warning of extremely high mosquito levels, so pack repellent, long sleeves and a head net whatever the month.

Inside the park the choices for an overnight stay are few: the Flamingo Lodge and its campground and houseboats, plus a second campground at Long Pine Key. Most visitors sleep in Homestead, Florida City or Everglades City, or drive down for the day from Miami or Naples.

Carry a card, not cash: the park takes only credit and debit cards or digital passes at its fee stations. And arrange your ride back before you go. The park warns that rideshare drivers who drop you off may not come back, and there is no public transportation to or within the park.

Gulf Coast visitor center reopened: the new Marjory Stoneman Douglas Visitor Center at the Gulf Coast area opened in 2026, replacing buildings lost to Hurricanes Irma (2017) and Ian (2022). It is open daily 9 a.m. to 5 p.m. Entrance passes are not sold there; buy one online in advance or at the Homestead or Shark Valley entrances.

Current closures (park alerts, September 2026): the East Everglades Day Use Area and SW 237 Avenue are closed during heavy construction. Several wilderness campsites (Johnson Key Chickee, Shark Point Chickee and Little Rabbit Key) are closed for maintenance, parts of Hog Key and Pavilion Key are closed, and the Halfway Creek paddling trail is impassable between markers L 3 and L 1. The Pa-hay-okee Overlook reopened in winter 2026 after a full renovation.

Mosquitoes and heat: wet-season highs average around 90°F with a heat index over 100°F. Carry water, start early, and take mosquito protection seriously in any month.

Visitors from outside the U.S.: since January 1, 2026, Everglades is one of 11 national parks that charge non-U.S. residents aged 16 and older an extra $100 per person on top of the entrance fee. The America the Beautiful pass costs $80 for U.S. residents and $250 for nonresidents, and a pass covers that extra fee.

Best things to do in Everglades National Park

Six good ways to spend a day in the river of grass.

Boardwalk

Anhinga Trail

A flat, wheelchair-accessible 0.8-mile loop from the Royal Palm area, about four miles inside the main entrance. In the dry season it is one of the surest places in Florida to see alligators, turtles, herons and anhingas up close.

Tram

Shark Valley

A narrated two-hour tram ride on a 15-mile loop road, with a stop at a 70-foot observation tower above the sawgrass. You may also bike or walk the loop; bikes are rented on site.

Overlook

Pa-hay-okee Overlook

A short, accessible boardwalk off the Main Park Road to a raised platform over the open sawgrass prairie, freshly renovated and reopened in 2026.

Florida Bay

Flamingo

The end of the road, on Florida Bay. The Guy Bradley Visitor Center, a marina, the restaurant and 90-minute boat tours into the backcountry or, November through April, out on the bay.

Islands

Gulf Coast and the Ten Thousand Islands

Mangrove islands and quiet water reachable only by boat. Boat tours into the park leave from Everglades City, and the new visitor center has a kayak launch.

Paddle

Canoe and kayak trails

Paddling the mangrove creeks is the most intimate way to see the park. Do it in the dry season, and for overnight trips get a wilderness permit; wilderness campsites are now reserved ahead on recreation.gov.

From the Guild

Guild tip: If you have one day and a car, enter at Homestead early in the morning, walk the Anhinga Trail before the crowds, stop at Pa-hay-okee on the way down, and have lunch at Flamingo before a late-afternoon boat tour. If you have only a half-day from Miami, Shark Valley is closer, and the tram does the work for you. Book the tram ahead from November through April.

When to visit Everglades

The dry season, typically December through April, is the Everglades at its best and busiest. Days are mild, humidity drops, mosquitoes ease off (though they still come out at dawn and dusk), and as the marsh dries, birds and alligators gather around the remaining water where they are easy to see. Ranger programs are offered widely, and visitor centers keep their longer hours, roughly 8 a.m. to 5 p.m.

The wet season, May through November, brings average highs around 90°F, humidity over 90 percent, afternoon thunderstorms and swarms of mosquitoes and biting flies. Ranger programs are fewer, and some facilities may be closed or only partly running. It is also hurricane season, June through November; watch the forecast and follow park closures.

For most older travelers and anyone coming from Arizona's dry heat, January through March is the sweet spot. Book Flamingo Lodge and the campgrounds early for those months, and reserve the Shark Valley tram, which fills on winter weekends.

How to get to Everglades National Park

By car

For the main entrance, take Florida's Turnpike south to its end at U.S. 1 in Florida City, turn right on Palm Drive (State Road 9336) and follow the signs to the Ernest F. Coe Visitor Center. The Main Park Road continues past Royal Palm and Pa-hay-okee to Flamingo; it has narrow shoulders and speed limits of 45 to 55 mph, so allow plenty of time.

Shark Valley is on the Tamiami Trail (U.S. 41), about 30 miles west of Miami. The Gulf Coast area is at Everglades City on the southwest side, reached from Naples or from U.S. 41. Plan at least an hour's drive between any two of the park's areas.

By air and on tour

Miami International and Fort Lauderdale-Hollywood International serve the Homestead and Shark Valley side; Southwest Florida International in Fort Myers is handier for Everglades City. Rent a car: there is no public transportation to or inside the park, and rideshare drivers may not return to pick you up.

If you would rather not drive, many day tours run from Miami and Fort Lauderdale. Check that a tour actually enters the national park; many airboat rides take place on private or state land outside its boundary.

Lodges inside the park

The only lodging inside the park is at Flamingo. The Flamingo Lodge opened in late 2023, replacing the old lodge lost to hurricanes in 2005. Built of elevated shipping containers and painted in the old Mission 66 pink, it has 24 rooms: two-bedroom suites, one-bedroom suites and studios, every one with a balcony, and four ADA-accessible rooms reached by elevator. A restaurant and bar sit alongside.

Flamingo is run by the park's authorized concessioner, which does business as Flamingo Adventures. It also rents 42-foot houseboats for trips into Whitewater Bay, offers glamping tents, runs the boat tours and the marina store, and handles campground reservations at Flamingo and Long Pine Key. Book through the concessioner's own website. Rooms for the January-to-March high season go early, and stays are limited to 14 nights.

Everyone else stays outside the park, in Homestead, Florida City, Miami, Everglades City or Chokoloskee. Flamingo is a long day's round trip from the hotels in Homestead, so a night or two at the lodge or campground is well worth it if you want sunrise and sunset on Florida Bay.

RV travel & camping at Everglades

The Everglades is a fine winter RV stop, with two campgrounds off the Main Park Road and a 35-foot limit at both.

Flamingo Campground, at the end of the Main Park Road, is the larger of the two, with 274 sites including large pull-through RV sites. It is the only place in the park with hookups: 41 sites in the T-Loop have electricity, and generators are allowed there 8 a.m. to 8 p.m. It has a dump station, showers and potable water. Part of the campground closes in the summer wet season; more sites open around mid-November, and all are open by mid-January. The RV limit is 35 feet.

Long Pine Key Campground, a few miles inside the main entrance, has 108 sites with no hookups, a dump station, showers and water, and a 35-foot RV limit. It is open seasonally, roughly November through April or May, and closed in summer. Its restrooms are wheelchair accessible.

Both are run by the concessioner rather than recreation.gov. In late 2026 the park's listings showed $33 a night for tent or non-electric RV sites on weeknights and $50 to $60 for electric sites at Flamingo, with a 10 percent discount for seniors, military and Access Pass holders. Reserve early for December through April. Private RV parks with full hookups operate around Homestead and Florida City for longer winter stays.

Fishing in Everglades

Fishing in the Everglades follows Florida's licence rules: you need a Florida freshwater licence for fresh water and a Florida saltwater licence for salt water, and anglers under 16 need none. Exceptions apply, so check with the Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission. Florida Bay and the backcountry around Flamingo and the Ten Thousand Islands are famous shallow-water fishing grounds, best reached by boat.

Fishing is prohibited in several popular spots, including the Royal Palm area and its trails, the lakes at the Ernest F. Coe Visitor Center, the Shark Valley Tram Road, Eco, Mrazek and Coot Bay ponds, and the Flamingo and Gulf Coast marinas and boat ramps from sunrise to sunset. Lobster and queen conch may not be taken. The park posts mercury advisories: do not eat bass caught north of the Main Park Road. Accessible travel at Everglades

What is available for visitors with limited mobility, and the passes that help.

Several of the park's best short walks are wheelchair accessible. The Anhinga Trail is a flat 0.8-mile loop of paved path and boardwalk from the Royal Palm area. The Pa-hay-okee Overlook (0.16 mile round trip) was renovated with accessibility in mind and reopened in winter 2026. At Shark Valley, one of the two short walking trails is accessible, and the tram lets you see the loop without walking it.

At Flamingo, four of the lodge's 24 rooms are ADA accessible and reached by elevator and walkway. Long Pine Key's restrooms are wheelchair accessible. The park also lists boat tours and frontcountry camping among its accessible services; call ahead to the concessioner to ask what a given tour can accommodate. For visitors who are deaf or hard of hearing, the park's TDD line is 305-242-7740.

Park passes that save you money. U.S. citizens and permanent residents with a permanent disability can get the free lifetime Access Pass. Visitors 62 and over can buy the Senior Pass ($80 for life, or $20 a year). Veterans and Gold Star families get a free Military Lifetime Pass, and current service members a free annual pass. Each covers entrance fees at every national park.

Traveling on dialysis? Homestead, just outside the main entrance, has several dialysis clinics, and Miami has many more. Book your treatment at a clinic there several weeks before you go; the large dialysis companies run travel desks that will arrange it with your home unit.

Everglades by cruise ship

PortMiami and Port Everglades in Fort Lauderdale are two of the busiest cruise ports in the world, and the Everglades is an easy pre- or post-cruise outing from either. Shark Valley is about 30 miles west of Miami, and the main entrance near Homestead is about an hour south of the city. Spend a night in Homestead or Florida City before or after your sailing and give the park a full day.

Because the park's areas are far apart, choose one: Shark Valley for an easy half-day with the tram, or the main entrance for the Anhinga Trail and the road to Flamingo. Everglades National Park FAQ

How much does it cost to get into Everglades National Park?

$35 per private vehicle, $30 per motorcycle, or $20 per person on foot or bicycle, good for seven days. An Everglades annual pass costs $70. The park accepts only cards or digital passes, no cash.

Do I need a reservation for Everglades National Park?

No reservation is needed to enter. Reserve the Shark Valley tram, Flamingo Lodge, houseboats and campsites ahead, especially from December through April.

Is Flamingo Lodge open?

Yes. The new Flamingo Lodge opened in late 2023 with 24 rooms and a restaurant, run by the park concessioner. Book through the concessioner's website.

Are there hotels inside Everglades National Park?

Only the Flamingo Lodge at the far end of the Main Park Road. Most visitors stay in Homestead, Florida City or Everglades City.

When is the best time to visit the Everglades?

December through April, the dry season, when it is cooler, mosquitoes are fewer and wildlife gathers near water. May through November is hot, humid and buggy.

How long is the Shark Valley tram tour?

About two hours on a 15-mile loop road, with a stop at a 70-foot observation tower. Reservations are strongly recommended November through April.

Do I need a fishing license in Everglades National Park?

Yes. A Florida freshwater licence for fresh water and a Florida saltwater licence for salt water. Anglers under 16 are exempt.

Can I visit the Everglades before or after a cruise?

Yes. Shark Valley is about 30 miles west of Miami, and the main entrance is about an hour from PortMiami or Port Everglades. Allow a full day and plenty of time to get back.

Plan your Everglades trip

Come in the dry season, start early, bring the bug spray, and let the alligators come to you.
