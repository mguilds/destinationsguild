Where to Stay in Aguas Calientes — Machu Picchu Hotels

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated August 2026

Aguas Calientes — officially Machu Picchu Pueblo — is a town of hotels and restaurants wedged into a river gorge at the foot of the mountain, reached only by train. It is not beautiful, and nobody comes for the town. It is worth a night for one reason: it puts you on the first bus of the morning, when the cloud is still lifting off the ruins and the circuits are almost empty. There is exactly one hotel at the citadel gate itself, and everything else is down here in the gorge. Two useful facts: at about 6,700 feet this is the lowest and easiest place you will sleep on a southern Peru trip, and the town is built on slopes, so “five minutes from the square” often means five minutes uphill with a suitcase. Below are the hotels we would send our own readers to, each with a Book Me link straight to Trip.com.

A word for travellers taking it gently. Book three separate things and confirm all three: your Machu Picchu entrance ticket (a timed slot on a numbered circuit, with a licensed guide required and no re-entry), your train from Ollantaytambo or Poroy, and your shuttle bus up the mountain. None of them includes the others. Ask your hotel to hold your main luggage and travel up with a small bag; the trains have strict limits. And enjoy the altitude here — at 6,700 feet, this is the easiest breathing on the whole itinerary.

Luxury

Sanctuary Lodge, A Belmond Hotel, Machu Picchu

$$$$$ LuxuryAt the citadel gateNot in the townFull board

The only hotel at the entrance to Machu Picchu itself, at the top of the mountain rather than down in the gorge — a few dozen paces from the turnstiles. What you are buying is time: you can walk in as the site opens and come back for lunch without the bus, and be there again in the late afternoon when the day-trippers have gone. It is extremely expensive and the rooms are comfortable rather than palatial. For a traveller for whom the climb and the queue are the hard part, it can be the single best-spent night in Peru.

Inkaterra Machu Picchu Pueblo Hotel

$$$$ LuxuryCasitas in a gardenCloud-forest reserveSpa

White adobe casitas scattered through twelve acres of cloud-forest garden on the edge of town, with hundreds of orchid species, spectacled bears in a rescue enclosure and the river loud beyond the trees. Easily the loveliest place to stay in Aguas Calientes and the only one that makes the town feel like somewhere rather than a waiting room. The casitas are spread across a hillside on garden paths and steps, so ask for one near reception if walking is difficult.

Spa & Hot Springs

The town is named for its hot springs; the hotel spas are the better bet.

Inkaterra Machu Picchu Pueblo Hotel — the Unu spa

$$$$ LuxurySpaGarden settingThermal-style baths

The best place in the gorge to put your legs up after the citadel: a small spa set in the gardens, with treatments and warm baths among the orchids and the sound of the river. Aguas Calientes takes its name from the public hot springs above the town, which are cheap and cheerful and, in honesty, often crowded and cloudy; if you want the soak rather than the anecdote, have it here.

Machu Picchu Hotels — Frequently Asked Questions

Should I stay overnight at Aguas Calientes or day-trip from Cusco?

Stay the night, if you possibly can. A day trip from Cusco means about four hours of travel each way and puts you at the citadel in the middle of the day with everyone else. A night at Aguas Calientes buys you the first bus up the mountain, when the cloud is still moving through the ruins and the circuits are quiet. It is also far less tiring, which matters more than most itineraries admit.

Is there a hotel at Machu Picchu itself?

One, and only one: the Sanctuary Lodge stands beside the citadel entrance gate, at the top of the mountain rather than down in the town. It is very expensive, and its advantage is entirely about time — you can walk in at opening and return for lunch without the bus. Everything else described as a “Machu Picchu hotel” is down in Aguas Calientes, a twenty-five-minute bus ride below.

How do I get from Aguas Calientes up to Machu Picchu?

By the shuttle bus, which climbs the switchback road in about twenty-five minutes and runs from early morning. Bus tickets are sold separately from your entrance ticket and can be bought in advance online or at the office in town; queues for the first departures form well before dawn in high season. The alternative is a steep stone stairway of well over a thousand steps — a serious hour and a half of climbing, and not something we would suggest to most of our readers.

How high is Aguas Calientes?

About 6,700 feet — a good deal lower than Cusco at 11,150, and lower even than the citadel above it at roughly 7,970 feet. Most people find they sleep better and breathe more easily here than anywhere else on a southern Peru itinerary. The town is in a narrow river gorge, so it is warm, humid and often wet, and the noise of the Urubamba carries into every room.

Is Aguas Calientes walkable?

The centre is small and there are no cars, which is a genuine pleasure, but it is built on the sides of a gorge and almost every street either climbs or descends. Hotels advertised as “a few minutes from the square” can mean a few minutes uphill, with luggage, on uneven stone. If stairs are difficult, say so when you book and ask specifically about the number of steps between the street and reception.
