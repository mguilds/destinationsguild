Trinidad and Tobago Travel Guide

By the Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Two islands, twenty-one miles apart, that have almost nothing in common. Trinidad is the big one: oil and gas, a capital that works for a living, the best street food in the Caribbean and the carnival every other carnival on earth is copying. Tobago is the small one: reef, rainforest, fishing villages and a beach you can usually have most of. The country sits further south than the rest of the Caribbean, off the Venezuelan coast, which is why it feels like South America in places and why hurricanes mostly pass it by. This is the Guild’s plain guide to both halves — where to sleep, when to come, what it costs and what not to miss.

Why visit Trinidad and Tobago

Carnival, where it actually comes from

Trinidad Carnival is not a tourist event that grew. It is the parent of the Caribbean carnivals, of Notting Hill and of Labour Day in Brooklyn, and it is where the steelpan was invented. Two days of mas bands crossing the stage, a pre-dawn start on the Monday in paint and mud called J’Ouvert, and a soca season that has been building since Christmas. In 2027 it falls on Monday 8 and Tuesday 9 February. If that is why you are coming, book a year out and do not expect a quiet island holiday.

The food is the reason to stay a day longer

Doubles — two soft fried breads around curried chickpeas, eaten standing up before nine in the morning — are the national breakfast and cost small change. Then roti, bake and shark at Maracas, pholourie, callaloo, pelau, Chinese and Creole and South Indian cooking that arrived with indentured labour in the nineteenth century and never left. For a country this size the range is absurd.

Tobago is a different holiday on the same passport

Twenty-one miles across the water, everything slows down. Tobago has reef you can snorkel from the beach, a rainforest reserve that has been protected since 1776, fishing villages on the north coast where nothing much happens, and the kind of quiet beach that the better-known islands charge a great deal for. It has far fewer rooms than Barbados or Saint Lucia, which is part of the point.

The wildlife, and the ibis in particular

The islands sit close enough to South America to have taken its wildlife rather than the thinner island version: hummingbirds, howler-free forest full of oilbirds and manakins, leatherback turtles coming ashore to nest on the north coast in the first half of the year, and the scarlet ibis flying in to roost over the Caroni mangroves at dusk, which is the thing most visitors remember longest.

Trinidad and Tobago at a glance

Money

The Trinidad and Tobago dollar (TT$), which has traded near TT$6.80 to the US dollar for years — a guide, not a fixed peg. Hotels and car hire take US dollars; street food, maxi-taxis and markets want local cash.

Language

English, with a Trinidadian Creole in everyday speech that takes a day or two of ear. Spanish, Hindi and French words run through it.

Getting around

Drive on the left. Trinidad is a car or a route-taxi island and traffic into Port of Spain is heavy; Tobago is small enough to hire a car for a week and see all of it. Maxi-taxis are the cheap local buses.

Flying in

Piarco International (POS) serves Trinidad, about forty minutes east of Port of Spain. A N R Robinson International (TAB) serves Tobago and sits at Crown Point, a few minutes from the beach hotels. The inter-island hop is about twenty minutes.

Weather

Hot all year, around the high twenties Celsius by day. Dry season January to May, wet season June to December with afternoon downpours and a drier spell some years in September. Rarely hit directly by hurricanes.

How long to stay

A week is the sensible minimum if you want both islands: two or three nights in Trinidad, the rest in Tobago. Carnival alone will take a week.

The two islands — which part is which

Port of Spain and the west

The capital and its western suburbs — St Clair, Woodbrook, Newtown, the Queen’s Park Savannah — hold almost every hotel on Trinidad, the restaurants, the Carnival route and the cruise berth. It is a working city of about half a million in its wider area, not a resort, and it is more interesting for that. Our Port of Spain guide has the detail.

Chaguaramas and the north-west peninsula

West of the city the land runs out into a green peninsula of marinas, boatyards, zip lines and day-trip islands. It is where Trinidadians go at the weekend, where yachts sit out hurricane season because the risk here is low, and the nearest thing to countryside within half an hour of town.

The north coast and Maracas

Over the Northern Range on a winding road is the Atlantic-facing north coast: Maracas Bay with its bake and shark huts, Las Cuevas, Blanchisseuse, and beyond them Grande Riviere, where leatherback turtles nest. Spectacular, and a day out rather than a base.

Central and south Trinidad

Sugar country turned to rice and vegetables, the Caroni Swamp and its ibis on the western edge, San Fernando as the second city, the Pitch Lake at La Brea, and the oil and gas towns that pay for the country. Few visitors stay here; the Caroni trip is the exception and it is worth it.

Crown Point and south-west Tobago

The busy end of the small island: the airport, Store Bay, Pigeon Point, Buccoo and its reef, most of the hotels, the dive shops and the Sunday night party at Buccoo. Flat, walkable in parts, and the easiest base if you are not hiring a car. Our Crown Point guide covers it.

Scarborough, Bacolet and the leeward coast

Tobago’s capital climbs a hill behind its harbour, with Fort King George at the top and the island’s best market on a Friday. East of town the coast turns to small headlands and bays at Bacolet. North along the Caribbean side are Castara, Englishman’s Bay and Parlatuvier — fishing villages with beaches most countries would have built on. Our Scarborough guide starts there.

The windward side and Speyside

The Atlantic side runs through Roxborough to Speyside and Charlotteville at the far north-east tip: big water, serious diving around Little Tobago, and the Main Ridge Forest Reserve inland, protected since 1776 and generally called the oldest legally protected forest reserve in the western hemisphere. Three hours from the airport on slow roads, and the quietest corner of the country.

Things to do in Trinidad and Tobago

Carnival, or the season around it

If you cannot come in February, the calypso tents, panyards and soca fetes run for weeks beforehand, and a panyard rehearsal in January — a hundred steel drums in a yard, free to stand and listen — is one of the great free experiences anywhere.

The scarlet ibis at Caroni

A flat-bottomed boat into the Caroni mangroves in the late afternoon, and then the national bird coming in to roost in hundreds, red against green, as the light goes. Boats leave in the afternoon and you want to book.

Maracas Bay and bake and shark

The drive over the Northern Range, the view from the lookout, and then a fried bake stuffed with shark and loaded from a table of sauces. It is the national day out and it deserves to be.

Buccoo Reef and the Nylon Pool

Glass-bottom boats run from Buccoo and Store Bay over the reef to the Nylon Pool, a waist-deep sandbar in the middle of the sea. Go with an operator who keeps people off the coral; the reef has taken a beating from decades of boats.

Argyle Falls and the Main Ridge

Tobago’s tallest waterfall, a guided walk in, and three tiers you can swim in. Pair it with a birding walk in the Main Ridge Forest Reserve on the way.

Diving at Speyside

Drift diving in nutrient-rich water around Little Tobago and Goat Island, with big sponges and the chance of manta rays. Not beginner water on the big-current days; go with a shop that will tell you so.

Fort King George and Scarborough market

The old British fort above Scarborough, restored, free to walk and with the best view on the island, and a Friday market below it that is for the town rather than for visitors.

When to go

January to May — the dry season

The reliable months: lighter rain, steadier sun, best underwater visibility in Tobago and the whole Carnival season in the middle of it. It is also the busiest and dearest stretch, and Carnival week is in a price class of its own.

June to December — the wet season

Heavier afternoon rain, greener everything, lower rates and far fewer people. Rain here tends to come hard and move on rather than settle in for a day. The islands sit south of the usual hurricane track, which is a real advantage over most of the Caribbean, but tropical storms are not impossible.

The weeks we would pick

Late January for the panyards and the build-up without Carnival prices; mid-July for the Tobago Heritage Festival; or the second half of May, when the dry season is ending, the crowds have gone and rates have not yet risen again. Our month-by-month guide goes through all twelve.

Arriving by ship

Both islands take cruise ships, and both put you ashore beside the capital rather than at an industrial terminal miles out. In Port of Spain the berth is on the waterfront at the bottom of the city, so the Brian Lara Promenade, Independence Square and the downtown markets are a walk away; the Savannah and the museums are a short taxi. In Scarborough the berth is at the edge of the small town, and Fort King George is a stiff but short climb above it.

Calls are concentrated in the dry season, roughly December to April, and Trinidad is a Southern Caribbean port rather than a fixture on the standard eastern and western itineraries, so look for it on longer southern loops out of Barbados, San Juan or Florida. If your ship is in Port of Spain for a day, the Caroni ibis trip is usually too late in the afternoon to make; Maracas and the city itself are the realistic choices. In Tobago a day is enough for Buccoo Reef and a beach, or the fort and the market.

A night either side of a sailing is worth booking rather than risking a same-day flight. Our Caribbean cruises guide sets out the wider routes, and the Guild’s cruise desk covers planning in general.

Travelling in your later years

Honest notes for anyone who does not want surprises. Port of Spain is hot, hilly in places and not a walking city after dark; take booked taxis rather than finding your way, and ask your hotel to call them. The drive over the Northern Range to Maracas is beautiful and relentlessly winding — sit in front if you are prone to car sickness, and it is about an hour each way.

Tobago is the easier island by a wide margin: short distances, flat ground at the Crown Point end, and several hotels where you can be on sand within a few minutes of your room. Scarborough itself is built on a hill and the fort is a real climb. Glass-bottom boats to the reef mean stepping down into a boat from a beach or jetty; ask the operator plainly how you board before you pay.

Health care is better in Trinidad than in Tobago, and serious cases in Tobago are flown across. Bring your own prescription medicines in their boxes with a copy of the prescription, and take out travel insurance that will move you if it has to.

Getting in

The UK Foreign Office advice we read for this page says British passport holders can visit Trinidad and Tobago without a visa for up to ninety days, and that your passport should have at least six months left after the day you arrive. You may be asked for an onward or return ticket and for proof that you can pay your way. Make sure your passport is actually stamped at the airport: it is the stamp that records how long you may stay, and the ninety days can be extended afterwards through the immigration department. Cash above TT$20,000 must be declared.

Rules differ by nationality and they change without notice, so check what your own government currently says before you book. We deliberately print no web address for an arrival form here: the arrangements in this region have been re-issued more than once, and a stale link is worse than none.

Fishing here

Trinidad and Tobago is not a flats destination in the Bahamian sense — there is little of the hard white sand bonefish want, and the water off Trinidad is coloured by the Orinoco. What it has is blue water close in off Tobago’s north-east, where the drop-off comes near the shore, and a Caribbean saltwater scene of tarpon, kingfish and wahoo rather than fly-rod flats. Questions travellers ask about Trinidad and Tobago

When is Trinidad Carnival in 2027?

Carnival Monday and Carnival Tuesday fall on 8 and 9 February 2027. The Trinidad and Tobago tourism board gives those dates, and so does the holiday calendar we checked them against. Neither is a public holiday in law, but almost everything closes anyway, and J’Ouvert begins at four in the morning on the Monday. Book flights and rooms many months ahead: Carnival week is the one time of year when Port of Spain genuinely runs out of beds.

Is Trinidad and Tobago in the hurricane belt?

The islands sit at about ten and a half degrees north, which is further south than almost every other Caribbean destination, and that is why direct hurricane strikes here are rare rather than routine. Rare is not never. Tropical storms and the heavy rain on their edges do reach the islands in the wet season, so travel insurance that covers weather is still worth having between June and November.

Should I split my time between both islands?

If you have a week, yes. They are not two halves of the same place. Trinidad is busy, industrial, musical and food-obsessed, with very few beach hotels. Tobago is small, slow and has the sand and the reef. Most visitors give Trinidad two or three nights for Port of Spain and the Caroni swamp, then fly or sail across for the rest. The hop between the two airports takes about twenty minutes in the air.

Do I need a visa for Trinidad and Tobago?

British passport holders can visit without a visa for up to ninety days, according to the UK Foreign Office advice we read for this page. Your passport should have at least six months left when you arrive, and you may be asked for an onward or return ticket and proof of funds. Make sure your passport is actually stamped on arrival. Rules differ by nationality and they change, so check your own government’s current advice before you book.

Is Trinidad safe for visitors?

We will not pretend otherwise: Trinidad has a serious violent-crime problem, concentrated in particular districts of greater Port of Spain that visitors have no reason to enter. The tourist side of the island — the western suburbs, Maracas, the north coast, Chaguaramas — is a different experience, and Tobago is quieter again. Take ordinary city sense, use booked taxis after dark rather than walking, and read your own government’s current travel advice before you go.

What is the money, and will US dollars work?

The currency is the Trinidad and Tobago dollar, which has traded near TT$6.80 to the US dollar for years. It is not a fixed peg, so treat that as a guide and not a promise. Hotels, car hire and dive shops will quote and take US dollars; markets, maxi-taxis, doubles stands and small restaurants want local money. Cash is still king outside the hotels, and ATMs in town are the easiest way to get it.

Which island has the beaches?

Tobago, overwhelmingly. Store Bay, Pigeon Point, Castara, Englishman’s Bay and the sheltered water behind Buccoo Reef are what people picture when they picture this country. Trinidad does have Maracas Bay on the north coast, which is a fine beach and a national institution for its bake and shark, but it is a day out from Port of Spain over a mountain road, not a place most people stay.

Do cruise ships call at Trinidad and Tobago?

Both islands take ships, and both berth beside their capital rather than out of town, so you can step off and walk into Port of Spain or Scarborough. Calls are concentrated in the dry season from about December to April, and Trinidad in particular is a Southern Caribbean port rather than a staple of the standard eastern and western routes.
