Vacation Homes — Whole Houses, Room to Spread Out

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated August 2026

There comes a point in a family's life when three hotel rooms and a lobby will no longer do. A whole house gives you a kitchen table, a porch, a washing machine and a door that closes — and for a week with the grandchildren, that is worth more than a turndown service. Here is how the four main kinds of vacation home differ, what to check before you commit, and what tends to go wrong.

Why a Whole House Beats a Hotel for a Family Gathering

The argument for a house is not really about the beds. It is about the hours between the outings. Book three hotel rooms for a family reunion and what you have bought is three separate boxes and a lobby with an espresso machine; everybody retreats after dinner and the visiting you came for never quite happens. Book one house and there is a kitchen table, which is where families actually talk, a sitting room big enough for everyone at once, and a porch or a deck where somebody will end up at eleven at night with the last of the wine.

The practical advantages stack up quickly too. A full kitchen means the early risers can make coffee at six without waiting for a dining room to open, and anyone on a particular diet — low salt, gluten-free, diabetic — can eat properly without a negotiation at every meal. A washing machine turns a ten-day trip into a five-day packing job. Doors that close mean a grandchild can nap while the adults keep talking. And a yard or a deck is somewhere small children can be let loose without anybody minding.

The money usually favours the house as well, though not always. Once you are at four or more people staying four nights or longer, a house is frequently cheaper per person than the equivalent hotel rooms, and considerably cheaper once you count breakfasts and a few meals eaten in. Below that threshold the arithmetic flips: for one or two people on a three-night city break, a hotel generally wins on convenience, daily housekeeping and the plain fact that somebody is at the desk at two in the morning if the heating fails.

The honest summary. A house buys you space, a kitchen and time together. A hotel buys you service and somebody to complain to. Choose the house when the group is large, the stay is long, and being together is the point; choose the hotel when the stay is short and you would rather not be responsible for the bins.

Beach Houses

The classic family week — and the category where the photographs flatter most.

Who it suits

Multi-generational weeksSat-to-Sat staysWatch the stairs

Beach houses are built for the trip most American families picture when they picture a holiday: everybody in one place for a week, near enough to the water that the children can be sent to it, with a big deck and a table that seats twelve. They suit groups of six to fourteen, they suit stays of a full week rather than a weekend, and they suit families who want the day to have no particular schedule. They are the easiest of the four categories to be happy in and the easiest to get wrong on the details.

What to check before you book

  • The stairs. A great many coastal houses are built on pilings for flood protection, which means a full exterior flight up to the front door before you have set a suitcase down. Ask how many steps from the car to the door and whether there is a handrail. Ask whether there is a bedroom and a full bathroom on the entrance level.
  • The real distance to the sand. Ask for it in minutes on foot, and ask whether the walk crosses a road, a dune crossover with steps, or soft sand. Beachfront, beach view, ocean view and steps to the beach are marketing terms with a great deal of stretch in them.
  • Air conditioning, room by room. Older coastal houses sometimes cool the main floor well and the upstairs bedrooms poorly. In July in the Carolinas or the Gulf, that decides who sleeps.
  • What is provided. Beach chairs, umbrellas, a cart for hauling things over the sand, towels, and starter supplies. Some houses supply all of it and some supply nothing but a bare mattress.
  • Parking. Count the spaces against the number of cars your group will actually arrive in. Barrier-island streets often prohibit parking on the verge.
  • Hurricane season. On the Atlantic and Gulf coasts, June through November. Ask the owner directly what happens if a mandatory evacuation is ordered, and read that answer alongside the cancellation policy.

What typically goes wrong

The commonest disappointment is distance: a house advertised as steps from the beach turns out to be a ten-minute walk with a road crossing, which is fine for adults and a genuine obstacle with a toddler, a cooler and somebody using a stick. The second is stairs, discovered on arrival. The third is a wide-angle lens: a lens can make a modest sitting room look like a hall, so look for photographs that show a whole room from a corner and be suspicious of a listing with no picture of the kitchen. Fourth, sand and salt are hard on houses, and a property that photographs beautifully can be tired up close; recent reviews are the tell. Finally, on a beach week the cleaning fee is spread over seven nights and rarely stings — but check whether a mid-week clean or a linen change costs extra, because on some houses it does.

Cabins & Lodges

Quiet, woodsmoke and a porch — with a few rural realities worth knowing in advance.

Who it suits

Couples & small groupsAutumn colourCheck the road

Cabins suit people who want the holiday to be quiet. Two couples in the Smokies in October, a family of six in a lodge near a national park, a week of reading on a screened porch with a lake in front of it. They are usually the best value of the four categories, they are at their finest in autumn and in ski country in winter, and they are the type most likely to deliver an evening you remember for the rest of the year. They are also the type where the practical details bite hardest, because you are further from help.

What to check before you book

  • The access road. This is the big one. Ask whether the final approach is paved, gravel or dirt; how steep it is; whether an ordinary car can manage it; and what happens in rain, snow or ice. Mountain cabins in Tennessee, Colorado and the Sierra regularly require four-wheel drive in winter, and a rental sedan on a steep gravel switchback is a bad afternoon.
  • Sleeping arrangements, honestly. Many cabins reach their advertised capacity by way of a loft up a steep open staircase or something close to a ladder, and by counting sofa beds. Ask how many proper bedrooms there are and how many are on the ground floor.
  • Mobile signal and internet. Ask which carrier works there, not whether there is signal. If anyone in the party needs to be reachable — a family member unwell at home, a business matter — establish this before you book, and note that no signal also means no ride-hailing and no delivery.
  • Water and heat. Well water is common and perfectly safe but tastes different and may run out if the group showers all at once. Ask what heats the cabin: a heat pump, propane, or a wood stove that somebody has to feed at three in the morning.
  • How far to a shop and a hospital. Forty minutes to the nearest grocery changes how you plan the week. Ask, and stock up on the way in.
  • Wildlife and the hot tub. Bear country has rules about food and bins; follow them. And if the hot tub is the reason you booked, ask when it was last serviced and whether it is cleaned between guests.

What typically goes wrong

Arrival in the dark down an unlit gravel road, with no signal to reach the owner and a door code written only on a phone that has just died. That single scenario accounts for a remarkable share of unhappy cabin stays, and all of it is preventable: get the directions and the code on paper, and aim to arrive in daylight on the first night. After that, the recurring complaints are heat that struggles in a cold snap, a loft nobody in the party can comfortably use, water pressure that disappears when three people shower in a row, and the gap between rustic as an aesthetic and rustic as a description of the plumbing. Read the three-star reviews; that is where cabins tell the truth about themselves.

Condos & Apartments

The city option, and often the most sensible choice for two people who want a kitchen.

Who it suits

City staysLifts matterAsk about the building

A condominium or an apartment is the right answer when the trip is urban, the party is small, and the appeal of a rental is a kitchen and a sitting room rather than acreage. It is also the category that most often solves the accessibility problem, because a lift-served building on a flat street beats almost any house for anyone who does not want to meet a staircase twice a day. Beach and ski resorts are full of condos too, and there they often come with the pool, the lift and the on-site parking that a standalone house lacks.

What to check before you book

  • Which floor, and is there a lift? Ask explicitly. A charming third-floor walk-up in Rome or Lisbon is a delight at thirty-five and a serious daily undertaking at seventy-five. Ask also whether there are steps into the building before the lift.
  • Lift reliability and size. Older European buildings have small, slow lifts that hold two people and one suitcase, and they are occasionally out of service. If that would be a real problem, ask.
  • Noise. Which way do the bedrooms face — the street or a courtyard? Is there a bar, a tram line or a rubbish collection point below? Light sleepers should ask before booking, not after.
  • Building rules. Some associations restrict short-term rentals, amenity access or the hours the pool is open. Confirm you will actually be allowed to use the facilities in the photographs.
  • Parking and entry. City parking is often a separate garage a few streets away at a daily charge. Gated buildings frequently need a fob or a second code; make sure you have both before you arrive.
  • Air conditioning in Europe. Far from universal, and a Mediterranean summer without it is punishing. If the listing does not say it has it, assume it does not and ask.

What typically goes wrong

Stairs where the listing implied none, a lift that is smaller or more temperamental than expected, and noise. Beyond those, the recurring city-apartment problems are check-in logistics — a key in a lockbox around a corner, an entry code that does not work, a manager who is asleep in a different time zone — and parking that turns out to cost thirty or forty dollars a day nobody budgeted for. In resort condominiums, the specific trap is a mandatory resort or amenity fee that appears late in the booking process, and a pool or gym that belongs to the association rather than the unit, with rules to match. Take the booking to the final price screen before you decide.

Villas & Large Homes

For the reunion, the milestone birthday and the wedding weekend — where the money and the logistics are both largest.

Who it suits

10–20 peopleReunions & weddingsSplit the cost

This is the category for the trip that has a reason: a fiftieth anniversary, a seventieth birthday, a wedding weekend, the first time the whole family has been in one place since before the pandemic. A villa in Tuscany, a large house on a lake, an estate outside Charleston. Divided among four or five couples, the nightly figure that looks alarming on the listing often works out at less than a decent hotel room each, with a pool and a cook's kitchen thrown in. These are also the bookings where a mistake costs the most, so they deserve the most care.

What to check before you book

  • Sleeps sixteen is not the same as suits sixteen. Ask how many proper bedrooms there are, how many have their own bathroom, and how many people are being counted on sofa beds and daybeds. Then ask how many seats there are at the dining table, because that is the real limit on a group holiday.
  • Bathrooms. The ratio matters more than the bedroom count. Twelve people and two bathrooms is a difficult week regardless of how handsome the house is.
  • The ground-floor bedroom. In a large multi-storey house, confirm at least one bedroom and full bathroom are on the entrance level for anyone who should not be doing stairs.
  • What staff, if any. Many villas include a weekly clean, a gardener or a pool technician who comes on set days; some include a cook or a concierge, and some charge for all of it. Establish who will be on the property and when.
  • Events and extra guests. If you are planning a party, a wedding or even a large dinner with outside guests, get written permission first. Many contracts forbid events outright and the penalty can be forfeiting the whole booking.
  • The payment schedule. Large rentals usually take a substantial deposit at booking with the balance due thirty to sixty days out, plus a damage deposit. Agree in advance who is collecting from whom, and give everyone the cancellation deadline in writing.
  • Getting there. Rural villas in Italy, France and Spain often sit at the end of a long unpaved lane, and the nearest town may be a fifteen-minute drive. Establish how many cars the group needs.

What typically goes wrong

The failure is usually organisational rather than architectural. Somebody has to be the one who books, chases the balance payment, collects money from six households and answers the owner's messages, and if nobody volunteers, the trip drifts until the house is gone. Beyond that: capacity oversold on paper, too few bathrooms, a pool that is not heated in April when everybody assumed it would be, an events clause discovered after the invitations went out, and a cancellation policy that turns a family emergency into a five-figure loss. For a booking of this size, read the cancellation terms twice, take the more flexible option even at a premium, and give serious thought to travel insurance.

Before You Book Anything

Five things that apply to every vacation home, whatever the category.

1. Ask about stairs and accessibility in writing

The word accessible is used very loosely on rental platforms, and photographs are taken to flatter. Ask the specific questions and get the answers in the platform's message thread rather than by telephone, so there is a record if the house turns out otherwise. How many steps from the parking space to the front door, and is there a handrail? Is there a bedroom and a full bathroom on the entrance level? How many steps in the internal staircase, and is there a rail on both sides? Does the shower have a step over a tub edge, a lip, or a level entry, and is there a grab bar? How wide are the bathroom and bedroom doorways if a walker is involved? Is the path gravel, sand, or something firm? An owner who answers these plainly and quickly is very often a good owner in every other respect too, and one who is vague is telling you something.

2. Read the cancellation policy before you pay, not after

Vacation rental cancellation terms are set by the individual owner or manager, not by the platform, and they are markedly stricter than a hotel's. A typical arrangement gives a full refund up to a stated number of days before arrival, a partial refund after that, and nothing at all inside a short final window; some peak-season and discounted bookings are non-refundable from the moment you pay. Read the exact wording on the listing, and read it again on the second house if you are comparing two. Two habits protect older travellers particularly well. Prefer the more flexible policy when the trip is months off or somebody's health is uncertain, even if it costs a little more — the premium is cheap insurance. And for large multi-family bookings where several households have paid in, consider proper travel insurance, because that is where a single cancellation does real financial damage.

3. Judge the total price, not the nightly rate

The advertised nightly figure is rarely what you pay. Expect a cleaning fee charged once per stay, a service fee taken by the platform, local lodging or occupancy taxes, and often a refundable damage deposit or a damage-protection charge. Pet fees, resort or community fees, pool-heating charges and linen fees appear on some listings and not others. Because the cleaning fee is a flat amount, it lands very differently depending on length of stay: spread over seven nights it is usually trivial, while over two nights it can add a third to the bill. Amounts vary enormously by property and region, so confirm the figures on the listing itself rather than trusting any rule of thumb. The reliable method is to advance the booking to the final price screen — the last step before payment — because that is the first place the true total appears, and then compare houses on that number alone.

4. Understand what “self check-in” means

It means nobody meets you. Instead of a front desk there is a door code, a key in a lockbox, or a smart lock, and the details usually arrive by message a day or two before you travel. It is genuinely convenient — a nine o'clock arrival is not a problem, and there is no wait for a manager to drive over — and the great majority of the time it works exactly as described. Four things make it reliable. Get the code and the exact street address before you leave home and write them on paper, because a flat phone battery or no signal at the door is the classic failure. Confirm the earliest hour you may enter, since houses often have later check-in than hotels. Ask what the parking arrangement is, as gated communities and city buildings frequently need a second code or a permit. And make sure you have a telephone number for a real person, in the same time zone, who can come out if the lock refuses to open.

5. Be careful with listings that have very few reviews

This is the single biggest risk on any rental platform. Reviews are the only independent evidence that the house exists as described, that the owner answers the telephone and that the photographs are honest. A property with no reviews may simply be new and perfectly good — but it may also be a listing whose earlier reviews were unflattering, or an outright fabrication. Favour houses with a solid number of recent reviews spread over months rather than a burst of five posted in a single week. Read the three-star reviews rather than the five-star ones; that is where the useful information sits, and it is where you learn about the stairs, the noise and the water pressure. Look at how the manager responds to criticism, because a reasonable reply to a complaint tells you more than any amount of praise. If you do decide on a thinly reviewed house, ask two or three specific questions first and judge the answers, keep every message and every payment inside the platform where the protection lives, and never agree to pay by bank transfer, wire, gift card or any other method that moves the transaction off the site. That request is the clearest warning sign there is.

Frequently Asked Questions

What readers ask us most about renting a whole house.

Why rent a whole house instead of booking hotel rooms?

Because of what happens between the outings. Three hotel rooms give a family three separate boxes and a lobby; one house gives them a kitchen table, a porch and a sitting room, which is where the actual visiting happens. A house also has a washing machine, a full kitchen for the early risers and anyone on a particular diet, and doors that close so grandchildren can nap while the adults talk. For four or more people staying four nights or longer, a house is very often cheaper per person than the equivalent rooms once you count breakfasts and a few meals in. For one or two people on a short city break, a hotel usually still wins on convenience, daily housekeeping and a front desk that is staffed at two in the morning.

What should I ask about stairs and accessibility before booking?

Ask in writing, and ask specifically, because listing photographs are taken to flatter and the word accessible is used very loosely. How many steps from the parking space or the street to the front door, and is there a handrail? Is there a bedroom and a full bathroom on the entrance level, or is every bedroom upstairs? How many steps in the internal staircase, and does it have a rail on both sides? Does the shower have a step over into a tub, a lip, or a level entry, and is there a grab bar? Beach houses in particular are often built on stilts with a full flight of exterior stairs, and cabins frequently have a sleeping loft reached by something close to a ladder. Get the answers in the platform's message thread rather than by telephone, so there is a record.

How do cancellation policies work on vacation rentals?

They are set by the individual owner or manager, not by the platform, and they are stricter than hotels. Also check the policy on the deposit and the final payment, since many rentals take a deposit at booking and the balance thirty to sixty days out.

Is it risky to book a house with very few reviews?

It is the biggest single risk on any rental platform, and it is worth taking seriously. Which type of vacation home suits which kind of trip?

A beach house suits a multi-generational week where the point is to be together near the water, and its main hazards are exterior stairs, the true distance to the sand and the difference between an ocean view and an ocean glimpse. A cabin or lodge suits couples and small groups who want quiet, woodsmoke and a porch, and its hazards are the access road in bad weather, well water, patchy mobile signal and sleeping lofts reached by steep steps. A condominium or apartment suits city trips and shorter stays where a lift, a doorman and a walkable street matter more than square footage, and its hazards are building rules, lift reliability and street noise. A villa or large home suits a reunion, a milestone birthday or a wedding party, and its hazards are the gap between advertised capacity and comfortable capacity, and the fact that a house sleeping sixteen needs one person willing to organise everybody.
