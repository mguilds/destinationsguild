Limassol

Where to Stay in Limassol

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Limassol is the island’s working city and the one that has changed most in the last decade. It is a real port with a real economy rather than a resort, and the visitor’s Limassol is a long thin thing strung out along the coast road for about fifteen kilometres. At the western end is the restored old town around the castle, with the Molos seafront park beside it, and this is where the city has become genuinely good to walk in. East of that, past the new towers, the hotel strip runs through Potamos Germasogeias and Agios Tychonas out towards the ruins at Amathus, which is where most package visitors actually sleep. The marina sits between the two. Be clear about one thing before booking: the beaches here are dark sand and shingle rather than the postcard white of the east coast, and Limassol’s pleasures are urban ones.

The old town, the castle and Molos

Best for walkingRestaurants and barsCity rather than beach

The old quarter around the medieval castle has been quietly and well restored: lanes of bars and small restaurants, the old carob warehouses turned into galleries, and the Molos seafront park running beside it — a long landscaped promenade with sculptures, lawns and a path that carries you for kilometres with the sea on one side. This is the best part of the city to be based in if you want to eat well and walk home, and it is the only part that feels like a town centre rather than a strip. The beach immediately here is unremarkable; come for the city, not the swimming.

Potamos Germasogeias: the hotel strip

Most hotelsOn the beachBusy road behind

Four or five kilometres east of the old town, this is the main tourist quarter: a dense run of hotels, apartments, tavernas and tourist shops along the coast road, with the longest stretch of usable beach in the city in front of it. It is convenient, well served by buses into town, and entirely unmysterious — if you want a week on a sunbed with the city available when you fancy it, this is the practical choice. The coast road behind is busy and noisy, so a room on the sea side is worth the difference.

Limassol Marina and the new front

SmartestRestaurants on the waterDearest

The marina development beside the old town put berths, apartments, restaurants and a small beach where the old port edge used to be, and the cluster of tall towers along the front nearby is the most expensive address in Cyprus. It is polished, it is pleasant to walk round in the evening, and it is handy for the old town, which is five minutes away on foot. It is also the least Cypriot-feeling corner of the city, and prices follow the yachts.

Agios Tychonas and Amathus

Quieter endLarger resortsRuins next door

Further east again, past the main strip, the hotels get larger and further apart and the surroundings get calmer. The ancient city of Amathus — a Greek and Roman site running from the shore up the hillside, with part of the harbour still visible under the water — is right among them. This end suits people who want a resort holiday with the city as an occasional trip rather than a nightly one; you will use the bus or a taxi for anything in town, and it is a twenty-minute ride.

Pissouri: the headland west of the city

Half an hour west towards Paphos, Pissouri is a hillside village with a separate bay below it, a long shingle beach and a handful of hotels and villas. It is the answer for people who want the south coast without a city attached, and it is well placed for Kourion, the wine villages and the drive up into the Troodos. Getting around Limassol

On foot

The old town and the Molos promenade are flat, pleasant and genuinely walkable, and the seafront path runs a long way east. What you cannot do is walk between the old town and the hotel strip as a matter of course — it is a good hour and mostly beside a main road. Think of Limassol as two or three walkable neighbourhoods joined by a bus ride rather than one walkable city.

Buses and taxis

The coastal bus route along the front is frequent and cheap and is the normal way visitors move between the strip, the marina and the old town. Intercity coaches run from Limassol to Nicosia, Larnaca and Paphos and are quick on the motorway. Taxis are plentiful; agree the fare or insist on the meter before setting off.

Driving and parking

Limassol is the easiest Cypriot city to drive in, with the A1 and A6 motorways meeting just behind it, but the seafront road is slow in the evening rush and parking in the old town is metered and scarce. Driving is on the left. A car pays for itself the moment you want Kourion, the Commandaria villages, Omodos or the mountain churches, all of which are within an hour.

Cruise ships and the port

Limassol is the island’s main cruise and ferry port, and the terminal is west of the centre rather than in it, so arriving passengers need a shuttle or taxi for the old town — it is not a walkable distance. When several ships are in, the old town and Kourion are noticeably busier in the middle of the day and quiet again by evening.

When to come, and what it costs

Limassol is hot and dry from June to September and the city holds the heat better than the open coast does, so August in the old town is not where you want to be walking at two in the afternoon. The sea is at its best from late August into October. Spring is lovely and is also when the city is at its liveliest: the Limassol carnival fills the streets in the weeks before Orthodox Lent, and the wine festival takes over the municipal gardens at the end of the summer. Winter is mild, wet in bursts, and the cheapest time to stay in what is otherwise an expensive city; the business trade keeps the hotels open all year, and weekend rates are often better than midweek ones.

Getting in: Cyprus is in the EU but outside Schengen

The Republic of Cyprus belongs to the European Union and uses the euro, but it is not in the Schengen area. Days you spend in Cyprus do not count against the ninety days in any hundred and eighty that Schengen allows, and days spent in Schengen countries do not count against your Cyprus allowance; the two are tracked separately. Cyprus is also outside the EU’s Entry/Exit System, so there is no biometric registration on arrival. Citizens of the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, Australia and the European Union need no visa for a holiday of up to ninety days. British advice is a passport issued within the last ten years and valid three months beyond departure; United States advice recommends six months of validity. Confirm your own nationality’s terms with your own foreign ministry.

One rule matters more than any other here. Enter the Republic only through its own points of entry — the airports at Larnaca and Paphos and the southern seaports. Arriving through the north and crossing south is treated as illegal entry and can mean a fine or a refusal. Crossing north for a day from the south is the opposite: legal, routine and well worth doing, with your passport in your pocket. Time spent in the north still counts towards your ninety days, there are limits on goods brought back, and many hire companies restrict taking their cars across — check your rental agreement first.

Limassol — questions

Old town or the hotel strip?

Old town if you want to walk to dinner, like a city in the evening and are not planning to spend the week on a beach. The strip at Potamos Germasogeias if you want a sunbed in front of your hotel and the city as a bus ride away. The marina sits between the two in both geography and price. A fair number of visitors split the difference by staying at the western end of the strip, which is close enough to walk into the old town along the front in about half an hour.

What are the beaches actually like?

Honest answer: ordinary by Cypriot standards. The sand along Limassol is dark grey to brown, often mixed with shingle, and the water deepens quickly in places. It is perfectly good for swimming and the municipal stretches are well kept and have facilities, but it is not the white sand of Nissi or Coral Bay. If beaches are the main reason for the trip, the south-east coast around Ayia Napa and Protaras is a different league; if you want a city with a sea attached, Limassol is the best one on the island.

Is Limassol a good base for seeing Cyprus?

It is the best single base on the island, because it sits in the middle of the south coast where the two motorways meet. Kourion is twenty minutes, the wine villages and Omodos under an hour, the Troodos peaks about an hour and a quarter, Paphos an hour, Larnaca forty minutes and Nicosia under an hour. Ayia Napa is the only major destination that is a real haul, at about an hour and a half.

Is the carnival worth planning around?

If you like that sort of thing, very much. Limassol's carnival is the biggest on the island and runs for about a fortnight before the start of Orthodox Lent, ending with a large parade through the city. It moves with the Orthodox Easter calendar, so the dates change every year and are usually in February or March; check them before booking, both to catch it and to avoid it, because rooms in the centre are scarce that weekend.

How far is the airport?

Larnaca airport is about forty-five minutes by motorway and is the usual arrival point; Paphos is around an hour. There are regular airport shuttle coaches to both from Limassol, which run to the flight timetable and are much cheaper than a taxi. Limassol itself has no airport.
