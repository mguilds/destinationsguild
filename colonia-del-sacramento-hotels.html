Colonia del Sacramento — cobbles, fig trees and the river

Where to Stay in Colonia del Sacramento — the Best Areas

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Colonia del Sacramento is a Portuguese smuggling town founded in 1680 on a blunt peninsula in the Río de la Plata, directly across the water from Buenos Aires. Its Barrio Histórico — cobbled lanes, single-storey colonial houses, a lighthouse built into convent ruins — is a UNESCO World Heritage site and small enough to cross in ten minutes. That size is the reason the choice of bed matters here: the quarter fills with day-trippers off the ferries until late afternoon and then empties, and the evening belongs to whoever stayed. Below are the three places to sleep, and the one firm recommendation we have about this town.

Inside the Barrio Histórico — the only choice we would argue for

The old quarter is a small blunt peninsula of cobbled streets, fig trees, a lighthouse built into convent ruins and single-storey houses the Portuguese put up in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. It is a UNESCO World Heritage site, listed as the Historic Quarter of the City of Colonia del Sacramento, and it is about ten minutes’ walk end to end. A dozen or so small hotels and guest houses occupy restored houses inside it. This is where you should sleep, for one reason: the quarter is full of day-trippers off the Buenos Aires ferries until late afternoon and then it empties, and the hours after that are the reason to come. The streets are cobbled and genuinely uneven, so if you walk with a stick or a wheel, ask for a ground-floor room and expect to pick your way.

Just outside the walls — the modern town

Immediately inland is an ordinary, pleasant Uruguayan town of about twenty-six thousand people, with the bus terminal, the supermarkets, the banks and most of the larger and newer hotel stock. You are five or ten minutes’ walk from the old gate, you pay less, and you get a bathroom that was not fitted into a three-hundred-year-old room. The trade-off is that you are staying near the thing rather than in it. For a family needing space, or for anyone arriving late by bus, it is the sensible call.

Real de San Carlos and the river road — out of town

Three miles north-west along the shore, Real de San Carlos is the strange leftover of an Argentine entrepreneur’s turn-of-the-century resort: a ruined bullring, an old pelota court, a racecourse still in use, and a long quiet river beach. There are a few hotels and rental houses out this way, plus more along the river road. It is a car base rather than a walking one, and it suits people who want the river and the space more than the cobbles. The bathing is warm, brown and flat.

What to know before you book

Stay the night; do not day-trip. This is the one firm piece of advice we have about Colonia. The ferries from Buenos Aires land through the morning and leave in the late afternoon, and the quarter changes completely once they have gone. An evening and an early morning in those streets is the whole point of the place.

It is an hour or so from Buenos Aires by fast ferry. The crossing of the Río de la Plata is a genuine alternative to flying between the two countries, and the terminal is a short walk from the old quarter. Services and journey times vary by operator and season, so check the timetable rather than a figure printed here, and remember you are crossing an international border.

One full day is enough for the quarter itself. Colonia is small. Two nights lets you see it twice in different light and take the river road or Real de San Carlos on the second day; three is a holiday rather than a visit.

The cobbles are the real accessibility issue in Uruguay. The calle de los Suspiros and its neighbours are original rough stone with a drainage channel down the middle. Lovely, and hard work on uneven feet.

Out of season it is better, not worse. Some restaurants close, the town is quiet and the light in spring and autumn is the best of the year. Unlike the Atlantic coast, Colonia does not shut down.

Colonia del Sacramento — questions

Is one night in Colonia del Sacramento enough?

One night is enough and two is better. The historic quarter is small &mdash; about ten minutes' walk from end to end &mdash; so a single afternoon covers the sights. The reason to stay over is not that there is more to see but that the character changes: day-trippers from the Buenos Aires ferries fill the streets until the late afternoon and then the quarter empties, and the evening and early morning in those cobbled streets are the best of it. A second night lets you add the river road or Real de San Carlos.

Should I stay inside the old quarter or outside it?

Inside, if you can. A dozen or so small hotels and guest houses occupy restored houses within the Barrio Historico, and sleeping there is what makes an overnight stay worth more than a day trip. Staying just outside, in the modern town, costs less, offers newer and larger rooms and is only five or ten minutes' walk from the old gate, which makes it the practical choice for families and for late bus arrivals. Either way the quarter is within easy reach; the question is only whether you want to be in it after dark.

How do I get to Colonia from Buenos Aires?

By ferry across the Rio de la Plata, which takes about an hour on the fast services, and the terminal is a short walk from the historic quarter. Several operators run the route and both journey times and schedules vary by boat and by season, so check the current timetable rather than relying on a figure in a guide. It is an international crossing, so you clear immigration and need your passport. Many people combine a few days in Buenos Aires with a night or two in Colonia, which is the easiest introduction to Uruguay there is.

How far is Colonia from Montevideo?

About two and a half hours by road, with frequent long-distance buses from the Tres Cruces terminal in Montevideo, and it is an easy drive on good roads. Colonia makes a natural first or last stop on a Uruguayan trip, particularly if you are arriving from or leaving for Buenos Aires by ferry. A common and sensible shape for a week is Colonia, then Montevideo, then Punta del Este, moving west to east.

What is actually in the historic quarter?

A small grid of cobbled streets laid out by the Portuguese from 1680, single-storey colonial houses, the remains of the city gate and drawbridge, a handful of small museums in old houses, the ruins of a convent with a nineteenth-century lighthouse built into them, and a riverfront promenade. The much-photographed calle de los Suspiros is a short rough-stone lane with a drainage channel down the centre. It is listed by UNESCO as the Historic Quarter of the City of Colonia del Sacramento. The pleasure of it is the walking and the light rather than any single monument.

Can you swim at Colonia?

Yes, in summer. This is the Rio de la Plata, so the water is brown with river sediment rather than dirty, and it is shallow, flat and warm &mdash; around 73 Fahrenheit, or 23 Celsius, in January and February. The beaches north-west of town along the river road and out at Real de San Carlos are the better ones and are used by local families. Nobody comes to Colonia for the bathing, but on a hot afternoon it is there.
