Serbia

The Serbia Travel Guide

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Serbia is a landlocked country in the middle of the Balkans, and the thing to understand about it first is that it is two different landscapes joined at Belgrade. North of the capital the land is flat — the Pannonian plain, the same one that covers most of Hungary — laid out in enormous fields and planned Habsburg towns, with Hungarian, Slovak and Romanian spoken in the villages. South of Belgrade it crumples into hills and then mountains, with monasteries in the folds of them, spa towns in the valleys and the Dinaric ranges in the southwest. The Danube runs across the top of the country and then turns south and east through the Iron Gates, one of the great river gorges of Europe. There is no coast and no famous ruin that everyone has heard of, which is exactly why it is still inexpensive and still uncrowded. What there is instead: fortresses on every river confluence that mattered, seventeen Roman emperors born on this soil, medieval monastery churches with frescoes that belong in any serious account of European painting, a capital with a nightlife reputation it has earned, and some of the best and cheapest eating in Europe.

Why visit Serbia

Rivers, and the fortresses that watched them

Serbia’s history is written at confluences. Kalemegdan sits where the Sava meets the Danube and has been fortified by Celts, Romans, Byzantines, Hungarians, Serbs and Ottomans in turn. Petrovaradin glowers across the water at Novi Sad with twelve miles of tunnels under it. Golubac guards the entrance to the Iron Gates with nine towers. Smederevo was the last capital of medieval Serbia. None of them charges much and none of them is crowded.

Rome, before anyone else noticed

Seventeen Roman emperors were born in what is now Serbia, Constantine the Great among them, at Naissus — today’s Nis. Felix Romuliana near Zajecar, the palace Galerius built for his mother and named after her, is a UNESCO World Heritage site. Viminacium on the Danube was a legionary fortress and city of forty thousand, and is still being dug. You will usually have these places largely to yourself.

The monastery frescoes

The Serbian medieval churches are the country’s strongest claim on anyone interested in art. Studenica, founded by Stefan Nemanja about 1190 and a World Heritage site, holds marble facades and frescoes of the first order; Sopocani’s Dormition, also listed, is the one art historians single out. They are working monasteries, quiet, and reached on country roads rather than by coach party.

A capital that genuinely does not sleep

Belgrade’s reputation for nightlife is not marketing. It runs from the kafanas of Skadarlija through the bars of Dorcol and Savamala to the splavovi — floating clubs moored on rafts along the Sava and the Danube, which move location and change names and are the most Belgrade thing there is. The city also has a serious concert and festival calendar, and it is all cheap.

The eating

Grilled meat is the national art: cevapi, pljeskavica, and the whole repertoire of the Kazandzijsko Sokace lanes in Nis. But Serbia is also kajmak and ajvar and fresh cheese, Vojvodina’s fish paprikash and strudel, a genuinely good wine country around Fruska Gora and in the south, and rakija — fruit brandy, usually plum, offered to you whether or not you asked. Portions are large and prices are low.

Value, still

Serbia is outside the euro and outside the European Union, and it shows in what things cost. A good dinner with wine, a room in the middle of Belgrade, a bus across the country, a ski pass at Zlatibor — all of it is a fraction of the Adriatic coast two hours away. Of the countries in this part of Europe it is the one where a modest budget still buys an unhurried trip.

Serbia at a glance

Capital

Belgrade — Beograd, the white city — standing on the bluff where the Sava runs into the Danube, with something like a fifth of the country’s people in the city and its suburbs. Belgrade Nikola Tesla Airport is about eighteen kilometres west of the centre, with a city bus, an airport minibus and taxis on a fixed-zone fare from a desk in the arrivals hall. Use the desk rather than a tout.

Money

The Serbian dinar, written RSD or din. Serbia is not in the euro area and the dinar is the only legal tender, although apartments and some hotels quote in euro. Cards are taken widely in Belgrade and Novi Sad. Carry cash anyway: the UK government advises keeping a reasonable amount on you because electronic payment networks have been disrupted during demonstrations, and cash is what works at markets, village places and small museums.

Language and alphabet

Serbian, a South Slavic language, spoken as a first language by about 84 per cent of the population at the 2022 census, with Hungarian and Bosnian next. It is written in both Cyrillic and Latin, and Cyrillic is the official script — so signs, platform boards and museum labels are often Cyrillic only. Learning the alphabet is an hour well spent. Hvala is thank you.

Borders and alliances

An official candidate for European Union membership since 2012 and negotiating since 2014, but not in the European Union and not in the Schengen area. Unlike most of its neighbours Serbia is also not in NATO; it declares military neutrality and takes part only in Partnership for Peace. It borders Hungary, Romania, Bulgaria, North Macedonia, Croatia, Bosnia and Herzegovina and Montenegro, and Kosovo, whose crossings Serbia treats differently — see Getting in, below.

Getting about

Driving is on the right. Unlike much of the Balkans, Serbia now has rail worth using: the Soko fast trains run Belgrade – Novi Sad – Subotica on the rebuilt Hungarian main line and cut Belgrade to Novi Sad to well under an hour. Everything else moves by intercity coach from the big bus stations, which is cheap and reliable if slow. A hire car is the answer for the monasteries and the mountains.

Shape of it

Landlocked, with the Danube, the Sava, the Tisa and the Morava doing the work a coast would do elsewhere. The population was 6,647,003 at the 2022 census, down sharply from 7.2 million in 2011 through emigration and a low birth rate. The highest point is Midzor at 2,169 metres on the Bulgarian border in the Balkan range. Eastern Orthodoxy is the religion of about 81 per cent of the population.

The regions

Belgrade in the middle, the plain to the north, and everything interesting and slow to the south and east of it.

Belgrade and the confluence

The old city on its bluff, with Kalemegdan park and the fortress at the point, Knez Mihailova running down to it, Skadarlija’s cobbles, Dorcol sloping to the Danube and Savamala to the Sava. Across the Sava is New Belgrade, built as a modernist city of blocks after 1948 and now where a great deal of the city actually lives. Zemun, further out, was a separate Habsburg town and still feels like one, with a fishermen’s quarter and the Gardos tower above it.

Vojvodina and the plain

Flat, fertile, planned and multilingual: six official languages in the province. Novi Sad is its capital and the most relaxed city in the country, Subotica on the Hungarian border is a museum of Art Nouveau, and Fruska Gora — a long low ridge above the Danube — holds sixteen working monasteries, the national park and the best of the wine. Sombor and Zrenjanin are the quiet Habsburg towns nobody writes about.

The Danube east, and the Iron Gates

Downstream of Belgrade the river widens past Smederevo and Viminacium, then squeezes into the Djerdap gorges — the Iron Gates — where the water narrows to a couple of hundred metres between cliffs with Romania on the far bank. Golubac fortress stands at the top of it. Lepenski Vir, a Mesolithic settlement with carved stone heads, was lifted above the rising water when the dam was built and is now under a shelter you can walk through.

Sumadija and the monasteries

The wooded heart of the country south of Belgrade, where the first uprisings against the Ottomans began. Kragujevac was the capital of the young principality and has the Sumarice memorial park. South and west of it, in the hills of Raska, are Studenica, Sopocani, Djurdjevi Stupovi and the Ibar valley — the medieval Serbian core, and the reason to hire a car.

The southeast: Nis and Stara Planina

Nis is the third city and the Roman one, at the crossroads where the road from Belgrade splits for Sofia and for Thessaloniki. Around it: Constantine’s birthplace at Mediana, the grim Cele Kula or Skull Tower, the thermal baths at Niska Banja, and the Devil’s Town rock pillars to the south. Further east the Balkan range — Stara Planina — rises to Midzor on the Bulgarian frontier, with waterfalls and emptied villages.

The southwest mountains

The best walking country in Serbia and the part most visitors miss. Zlatibor is a plateau resort at about a thousand metres with Tornik for skiing above it; Tara is a national park of gorges and spruce; the Uvac canyon meanders between cliffs where griffon vultures were brought back from almost nothing. At Mokra Gora the Sargan Eight narrow-gauge railway loops back over itself to climb, and Drvengrad is the wooden village Emir Kusturica built on the hill above.

Things to do in Serbia

Walk out to the point at Kalemegdan

Go in at the Knez Mihailova end and keep walking until the park runs out above the water. You pass the Ottoman gates, the clock tower, the Roman well, the military museum’s rows of guns and the Victor on his column, and then the two rivers are below you with New Belgrade and Zemun spread out beyond. It is free, it takes an hour, it is the best introduction to the city, and late afternoon is the time to do it.

Take the Soko train to Novi Sad for the day

Belgrade to Novi Sad on the fast train takes well under an hour on the rebuilt main line, and it is the easiest good day out in Serbia: the pedestrian centre, the Danube quay, lunch, and the climb or taxi up to Petrovaradin Fortress for the view back over the river at the city. Buy at the station or online; the trains are modern double-deckers and inexpensive.

Eat in the Kazandzijsko Sokace at Nis

The Coppersmiths’ Alley beside the fortress bridge is a short lane of grill houses with tables down the middle of it, and it is the best cheap eating in the country: cevapi, pljeskavica stuffed with kajmak, grilled peppers, a carafe of rakija you did not order. Go early evening, sit outside, and do not try to be sophisticated about it.

Ride the Sargan Eight

A narrow-gauge line at Mokra Gora, rebuilt from the old Belgrade–Sarajevo railway, which climbs three hundred metres by looping back over itself in a figure of eight through tunnels and over viaducts. It takes about two and a half hours there and back with photograph stops. Drvengrad, the wooden village above it, and the Tara national park are the same day out from Zlatibor.

Get to Studenica and Sopocani

Two World Heritage monasteries about an hour and a half apart in the hills of Raska, both still working, both with frescoes that are the reason people who know Byzantine painting come to Serbia at all. Dress modestly, go on a weekday, and give each of them longer than you planned. This is a car day, or a driver for the day from Kraljevo or Novi Pazar.

Go down the Danube to the Iron Gates

From Belgrade the road east runs to Golubac fortress in about two and a half hours, and then along the gorge with the cliffs closing in and Romania opposite. Boat trips run through the narrows in season from Donji Milanovac and Golubac. Lepenski Vir and the Trajan’s Tablet rock inscription are on the same stretch. It is the single most dramatic landscape in the country and it is barely visited.

When to go, and what it costs

Serbia has a moderately continental climate — cold winters, hot summers, and very little maritime softening. May, June and September are the pick of the year: warm without the August heat, long days, everything open. June is the wettest month in Belgrade at about 75 mm, almost all of it afternoon thunderstorms that clear. July and August are hot and still, with highs around 29 degrees and spells when air off Africa pushes it past 35 or even 40; the cities empty towards the rivers and the mountains are the comfortable places to be. October is the quiet triumph of the Serbian calendar — dry, golden, mild, and the best time for the monasteries and Fruska Gora. December to February is genuinely cold, often grey or foggy, with January averaging about 1 degree in Belgrade and cold snaps that can reach minus 20; it is the ski season at Zlatibor and Kopaonik, it is very cheap in the cities, and the sunshine is poor. The dates to know are Serbian Orthodox Christmas on 7 January, Statehood Day on 15 February, Orthodox Easter on its own reckoning, the EXIT festival at Petrovaradin Fortress in July and the Guca trumpet festival in August — both of which move, so check the year’s dates before booking a bed anywhere near them.

Fishing here

Fishing in Serbia is river and lake fishing, and there is a great deal of it: the Danube and the Sava for catfish, carp, pike and zander, the Tisa in Vojvodina, the Drina along the Bosnian border for grayling and trout, and the reservoir lakes of the southwest — Uvac, Zlatar and Perucac — which are the quiet, spectacular end of it. The Djerdap stretch below Golubac has a reputation for very large catfish. Licences are sold by the angling associations that manage each water, seasons and size limits vary between them, and the national parks add their own rules, so this is a thing to arrange with a local association or guide on the ground. Travelling in your later years

Novi Sad is the easiest base in the country

It is flat, the centre is compact and pedestrianised, the Danube quay is level, and taxis are cheap and plentiful. If walking is the constraint, base yourself here and take the fast train in to Belgrade for the day rather than the other way round. The one climb is Petrovaradin Fortress across the bridge, and a taxi goes to the top of it.

Belgrade is hillier than it looks

The old city stands on a bluff, so Knez Mihailova is level but almost everything leading off it falls away steeply, and Kalemegdan’s paths rise and then drop to the point. Skadarlija is cobbled and on a slope. Stay in Stari Grad near Knez Mihailova or on Terazije to keep the walking flat, and use taxis freely — they cost very little and the buses and trams get crowded.

The spa towns exist for exactly this

Serbia has a long tradition of the banja — thermal spa towns with level parks, promenades, treatment houses and hotels built around taking the waters. Vrnjacka Banja is the grandest, Niska Banja sits ten kilometres from Nis, and Palic has its own park beside the lake near Subotica. They are inexpensive, geared to older guests by long habit, and a comfortable way to stay still for a week.

Where the ground is difficult

Petrovaradin and Golubac are both fortress climbs on uneven stone; Studenica and Sopocani involve walking on rough ground and standing a long time in dim churches; Lepenski Vir is down a slope from the road; the Uvac viewpoint is a steep walk; and Devil’s Town is a stepped path down and back up. The Sargan Eight railway, the Danube boat trips and the monastery at Krusedol on Fruska Gora are the gentle alternatives, and the spa parks are level throughout.

Getting in: what applies, and what does not

Serbia is not in the European Union and not in the Schengen area, so the European entry rules you may have read about are not the rules for arriving here. Serbia runs its own border and its own visa policy, and for most Western visitors it is a straightforward one.

British passport holders may visit without a visa for up to 90 days in any 6-month period, for tourism or business, with a passport whose expiry date is at least 90 days after the day they plan to leave. That is what the UK government’s Serbia travel advice said when we read it on 26 November 2026. A great many other nationalities, the United States among them, are admitted visa-free for comparable periods — but the American source refused our request on the day we wrote this, and we would rather say so than print a number we could not read, so check your own nationality’s allowance with the Serbian embassy or consulate for your country before you travel.

Make sure your passport is stamped on the way in. This is the one piece of Serbian red tape that can genuinely spoil a trip. If you try to leave the country without an entry stamp in your passport you can face charges of illegal immigration, a heavy fine and a prison sentence. Check the stamp before you leave the desk. Enter by land only at recognised crossings.

You must also register with the local police within 24 hours of arriving. If you are in a hotel or a registered apartment the check-in staff do it for you automatically and you will never think about it again; if you are staying privately with friends or family, it is on you, and failing to do it can mean a fine, arrest and a court appearance.

Kosovo is the other thing to get right. Serbia does not regard its crossing points with Kosovo as international border crossings. You can travel from Serbia into Kosovo and back again. But to enter Serbia from Kosovo you must already hold a Serbian entry stamp — obtained at Belgrade, Nis or Kraljevo airport, or at a land border with Bosnia and Herzegovina, Bulgaria, Croatia, Hungary, Montenegro, North Macedonia or Romania. Without one you are likely to be refused entry. The UK government also records isolated cases of Serbian officials cancelling Republic of Kosovo stamps in foreign passports, or refusing entry to people carrying them, and notes that tensions can cause difficulties at those crossings. The simple answer, if both are in your plans, is to fly into Belgrade first and do Serbia before Kosovo.

Two customs points worth knowing: cash or travellers cheques worth more than 10,000 euros must be declared on a form that customs signs or stamps, and you keep the certified copy to show on the way out; and if you are bringing Serbian dinars into the country you need the receipt from the bank where you bought them.

On safety, there is no advice against travel to any part of Serbia. The thing to plan around is protest: mass gatherings and demonstrations have been taking place in Belgrade and other cities, called at short notice, mostly peaceful but sometimes escalating, and able to disrupt public transport and mobile phone networks. Keep some cash on you in case card payments fail, keep your phone charged, stay away from gatherings and leave if a crowd turns. Elsewhere it is ordinary urban caution — pickpockets in tourist areas, airports, buses and motorway service stations — with extra care around Red Star and Partizan derby matches. Do not photograph military or police installations anywhere in the country. In the mountains north and east of Kosovo there is still some danger from unexploded ordnance left from 1999: keep to marked roads and paths there.

If your trip also takes in Hungary, Croatia, Bulgaria, Greece or anywhere else inside the Schengen area — and most flights from North America reach Belgrade through a Schengen hub — then you will cross a Schengen border on the way, and these rules do apply to that crossing:

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Getting here: Belgrade Nikola Tesla is the country’s hub, with Air Serbia’s network and most European carriers; Nis has a smaller airport with low-cost flights, and Kraljevo a smaller one again. There is one non-stop from North America — Air Serbia’s New York JFK service, JU501 out and JU500 back, on an Airbus A330 — which runs several days a week in high summer and thins towards roughly weekly in winter, so confirm the days for your own dates with the airline. The Soko fast trains run north to Novi Sad and Subotica on the rebuilt Budapest main line; the through service to Budapest itself has been delayed more than once, so check it with the operator rather than trusting any schedule you read, including ours — which is why we do not print one. Coaches connect Belgrade with Sarajevo, Zagreb, Podgorica, Skopje, Sofia, Thessaloniki and Vienna.

Serbia — questions

How many days do I need in Serbia?

Four days is enough for Belgrade and a day out to Novi Sad, which is the trip most first-time visitors take and a good one. A week lets you add either the south — Nis, the spa at Niska Banja and the Roman sites — or the southwest, where Zlatibor, the Sargan Eight railway and the Uvac canyon sit within an hour or two of each other. Ten days gets you the Danube east as well: Golubac fortress, the Iron Gates gorge and Lepenski Vir. Distances are modest but the roads away from the motorways are slow, so allow more time than the map suggests.

Is Serbia expensive?

It is one of the best-value countries in Europe for a visitor, and clearly cheaper than Croatia, Hungary or Greece. Belgrade is the dearest part of it and still modest by Western European standards; Nis, Subotica and the spa towns are inexpensive. We do not print figures because they move, but the pattern holds: eating and drinking well costs little, intercity buses and the Soko trains are cheap, and the premium times are the EXIT festival week in Novi Sad and the ski season at Zlatibor and Kopaonik.

Do I need a visa for Serbia?

British passport holders can visit Serbia without a visa for up to 90 days in a 6-month period, for tourism or business, and the passport must have an expiry date at least 90 days after the day you plan to leave. That is the rule we could read ourselves, on the UK government's Serbia travel advice, on 26 November 2026. Many other nationalities including the United States are admitted visa-free for similar periods, but we could not open the American source on the day we wrote this, so check your own nationality rather than assuming it matches.

Can I travel between Serbia and Kosovo?

You can go from Serbia into Kosovo and back. Going the other way is where people get caught. Serbia does not treat its crossing points with Kosovo as international borders, so to enter Serbia from Kosovo you need a Serbian entry stamp already in your passport, from Belgrade, Nis or Kraljevo airport or from a land border with Bosnia and Herzegovina, Bulgaria, Croatia, Hungary, Montenegro, North Macedonia or Romania. Without one you are likely to be refused entry. The UK government also records isolated cases of Serbian officials cancelling Kosovo stamps or refusing entry to people carrying them. If Kosovo is in your plans, fly into Belgrade first.

Is it safe to visit Belgrade at the moment?

There is no advice against travel to any part of Serbia. What a visitor should plan around is demonstrations: the UK government records mass gatherings in Belgrade and other cities, called at short notice, mostly peaceful but some turning violent, and capable of disrupting public transport and mobile phone networks. Its own practical advice is worth repeating — carry some cash, because card networks have gone down during disruption, keep your phone charged, and walk away from a crowd rather than through it. Ordinary street crime is the usual city kind: pickpockets in tourist areas, on buses and at service stations.

Do I have to read Cyrillic?

It helps more than you would expect. Serbian is written in both alphabets and Cyrillic is the official one, so street signs, bus destination boards and museum labels often appear in Cyrillic only while menus and shop fronts are usually in Latin. An hour spent learning the thirty letters pays for itself the first time you have to find a platform. English is widely spoken by younger people and in anything to do with tourism. Hvala is thank you, and dobar dan is good day.

Is there a non-stop flight from the United States?

Yes, one: Air Serbia flies New York JFK to Belgrade non-stop on an Airbus A330, as flights JU501 out and JU500 back. It is not an everyday service — published schedules show it running several days a week in high summer and thinning to roughly weekly in winter — so confirm the days for your own dates with the airline rather than assuming. From everywhere else in North America you connect, most often through a European hub, and that connection is where the Schengen rules below apply. Checked 26 November 2026.
