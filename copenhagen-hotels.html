Copenhagen

Where to Stay in Copenhagen

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Copenhagen is an easy city to stay in and a surprisingly simple one to choose in, because it is small, flat and built around one harbour. The short version: the old centre is the convenient choice, Vesterbro behind the station is the enjoyable one and the better value, the lakes district is the calm one, and Christianshavn across the water is the pretty one. Below is what each is actually like to walk out of in the morning.

Indre By — the old centre, Nyhavn and Kongens Nytorv

Most convenientWalk everywhereBest for a first visit

The obvious choice and usually the right one. This is the knot of streets between the town hall, the pedestrian run of Strøget and the painted houses at Nyhavn, with the royal palaces, the round tower and the canal boats all inside a twenty-minute walk. The metro and the airport train both stop in it, the harbour buses leave from it, and almost nothing you came to see is more than a short stroll away. The honest drawbacks: it is the dearest part of town, the main shopping street is a crush in summer, and the blocks nearest the station are the scruffiest in the centre.

Vesterbro and the Meatpacking District — behind the station

Restaurants and barsBest value in the centreLively at night

West of the central station, Vesterbro went from the city’s red-light quarter to its eating and drinking district in about twenty years, and the old meat market at Kodbyen is now full of restaurants and wine bars. It has more hotel rooms per street than anywhere else in Copenhagen and the best value in walking distance of the centre, with the lake-side streets around Værnedamsvej genuinely charming. Istedgade nearest the station is still rough at the edges after dark, which is the trade-off and the reason the rates are what they are.

Nørrebro and Østerbro — the lakes and the parks

ResidentialGood value for spaceShort bus or metro ride

North of the lakes that ring the old city, these are the two neighbourhoods Copenhageners actually live in. Nørrebro is the young, mixed, crowded one, with the best cheap food in the city along Jaegersborggade and Nørrebrogade and a famous cemetery that doubles as a park. Østerbro, east of it, is the quiet, handsome, family one, with the big park at Fælledparken and the harbour baths at Svanemøllen. Both are two or three metro stops from the centre; both have more apartments than hotels, which is worth knowing if you want a kitchen.

Christianshavn, Holmen and the new harbour

Canals and waterQuieterMetro to the centre

Across the harbour on its own set of islands, Christianshavn is a seventeenth-century canal quarter that feels Dutch, with houseboats, the spiral spire at Vor Frelsers and the Christiania free town at its edge. Beyond it, Holmen and Refshaleøen hold the old naval yards, the opera house and a string of street-food halls and galleries in the sheds. It is one metro stop from the centre and a nicer place to come home to than Indre By, but there are far fewer rooms out here and the far end at Refshaleøen needs a bus or a harbour ferry.

Getting around

One ticket covers the metro, the buses, the local trains and the harbour ferries, sold by zone and by time rather than by journey, so a single ticket lets you change between all of them within the hour. Buy it in the transport app or at a machine. The metro is driverless and runs around the clock, and every station is step-free from the street.

The better answer is a bicycle. Copenhagen has separated lanes on almost every main street, their own traffic lights and bridges built for bikes alone, and a day’s hire costs about what a couple of transport tickets do. The city is flat and the distances are short. If you would rather not cycle, the harbour ferries count as ordinary buses on the same ticket and give you the best view in the city for the price of a bus ride.

When to come, and what it costs

Copenhagen is a four-season city with one short, glorious summer. From late May to early September the harbour baths are open, the outdoor tables are out and the light runs past ten at night; midsummer gives better than seventeen hours of daylight. July is the warmest month and the most expensive on the coast, but the city itself empties as Danes take their own holidays, so hotel rates in town are often softer than you would expect.

Late May and the first half of September are our own pick: long days, open gardens, lower rates and no crowding. Winter is dark rather than cold — about seven hours of daylight at midwinter and temperatures either side of freezing, with rain more likely than snow. It is also when Copenhagen is at its most characteristic indoors, and when rooms are cheapest apart from the Christmas weeks. Our month-by-month guide to Denmark sets out the whole year.

Denmark uses the Danish krone (DKK, written kr), not the euro. Denmark is a member of the European Union but voted to keep its own currency and has set no date for changing it, so euro notes are not legal tender — though the krone is held in a fixed band against the euro, which is why the rate barely moves from one year to the next. Pay in kroner. Denmark is one of the most nearly cashless countries in the world: cards and phones are taken everywhere, including on buses, at museum desks and at market stalls, and a good many places no longer handle notes at all, so you need very little cash and may need none. Prices in shops include the twenty-five per cent sales tax, which is among the highest in Europe and is already in the figure on the shelf. Tipping is not expected and never chased — service is in the bill, and rounding up is plenty. Decline any card machine that offers to charge you in dollars; pay in kroner and let your own bank convert.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS — the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years — is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today — nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Denmark has been in the European Union since 1973 and in the Schengen area since 2001, so everything above applies here in full and an arrival from anywhere else in Schengen involves no passport control at all. Denmark did not join the euro, so the currency is the krone. If you plan to cross to Malmö in Sweden, carry your passport even though the bridge is inside Schengen: Sweden has run identity checks on that crossing on and off for years.

Copenhagen hotels FAQ

Which area of Copenhagen is best to stay in?

Indre By, the old centre, for a first visit — almost everything you came for is within a walk, and the airport train and the metro both stop in it. Vesterbro, just behind the central station, for better value and the best eating and drinking. Nørrebro and Østerbro for more space and a residential street at night. Christianshavn if you would rather look at canals than shops.

Is Copenhagen walkable?

Unusually so. The old city is flat and compact and you can cross the whole centre on foot in half an hour, with a continuous harbour promenade from the opera house round to the library. What you must watch is the cycle lane: it sits between the pavement and the road, the bikes move fast and they are silent. Look left before you step off a kerb.

How do I get from Copenhagen Airport to the city?

The airport is about eight kilometres from the centre and both the train and the metro run from the terminal to the middle of town in roughly a quarter of an hour. The metro runs around the clock. Buy the ticket at the machine or in the transport app before you board; the same ticket covers the metro, the buses, the local trains and the harbour ferries within its zones and time.

Do I need a car in Copenhagen?

No, and it is a liability. Parking is expensive and awkward, the street layout assumes you are on a bicycle, and the metro, buses and harbour ferries between them reach everything in the guide. Hire a car only for the day you drive out into the country, and hire it that morning.

Is Copenhagen expensive?

Yes, though less so than Oslo or Zurich. Hotel rooms and restaurant dinners are where the money goes; transport, museums and everyday food are closer to the rest of northern Europe. The cheap pleasures here are genuinely good — bakeries, lunch counters, the harbour baths, the parks and a hired bicycle — so most travellers eat well and spend the money on the room.

When is the best time to come to Copenhagen?

Late May to early September for the long light and the open harbour baths, with late May and the first half of September the best balance of weather, price and calm. July is warm and the city is oddly quiet because the Danes are away, though the coast is busy. December has the Christmas markets and about seven hours of daylight.
