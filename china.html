Why visit China

Few countries pack so many worlds into one trip — 5,000 years of history, headlong modernity, staggering landscapes and one of the world's great cuisines.

The Great Wall & the dynasties

The Great Wall over the northern hills, the Forbidden City and the Terracotta Army of Xi'an — the monuments of the world's longest-lived empire, still overwhelming today.

Cities of the future

Shanghai's neon towers and the Bund, Beijing's scale and energy, and a bullet-train network that glides between them faster and smoother than anywhere on earth.

Landscapes & pandas

The karst peaks and rivers of Guilin and Yangshuo, the terraced rice fields of the south, the sacred mountains — and the giant pandas of Chengdu.

The food

Peking duck in Beijing, fiery Sichuan hotpot in Chengdu, delicate dumplings and soup buns in Shanghai — regional cooking that is a reason to travel in itself.

How the country divides up

Beijing & the Imperial North

Almost every trip begins in Beijing, and it should. The Great Wall climbs the hills within a couple of hours of the city, the Forbidden City and Tiananmen Square sit at its centre, and the Temple of Heaven and Summer Palace ring it with imperial calm. North lie the emperors' summer palaces at Chengde and the Buddhist grottoes near Datong.

Xi'an & the Ancient Heartland

Xi'an was the capital of eleven dynasties and the start of the Silk Road, and it guards the Terracotta Army — thousands of life-size warriors buried with China's first emperor. Its Ming-era City Wall is still walkable, and nearby Luoyang and walled Pingyao deepen the ancient story.

Shanghai & the Yangtze Delta

Shanghai is China at full tilt — the colonial-era Bund facing the space-age towers of Pudong across the Huangpu, plane-tree lanes in the French Concession, and world-class food and nightlife. Ringing it are the classical gardens of Suzhou and the lake and tea hills of Hangzhou, both a short bullet-train hop away.

The South & Southwest

Chengdu, capital of Sichuan, is famous for its giant pandas, fiery hotpot and unhurried teahouse life. South, Guilin and Yangshuo deliver China's postcard landscape — the Li River drifting between sugarloaf karst peaks, with the Longji rice terraces rising behind.

Top things to do in China

The experiences worth building a trip around.

Walk the Great Wall

Hike a restored-but-wild stretch such as Mutianyu or Jinshanling, watching the wall ride the ridgelines to the horizon — the single greatest sight in China.

The Forbidden City

The vast palace of the Ming and Qing emperors at the heart of Beijing, with the Temple of Heaven and Summer Palace to round out the imperial capital.

The Terracotta Army

Row upon row of life-size warriors and horses guarding the tomb of China's first emperor near Xi'an — no two faces alike, and unforgettable in person.

Shanghai's skyline

Stand on the Bund at dusk as Pudong's towers light up across the river, then ride to the top of the Shanghai Tower for the view from above.

Cruise the Li River

Drift by boat or bamboo raft from Guilin toward Yangshuo, past mist-wrapped karst peaks, water buffalo and fishermen — the scene on the ¥20 note.

Meet the pandas of Chengdu

See giant pandas up close at the Chengdu research base at feeding time, then dive into hotpot and teahouse life in Sichuan's easygoing capital.

Best time to visit & getting there

When to go

Spring (April–May) and autumn (September–early November) are the sweet spots: mild, dry and comfortable for the Wall, the cities and the Li River. These are the loveliest weeks to travel.

Summer (June–August) is hot, humid and busy; winter is cold in the north but quiet and cheap. Steer clear of the national holidays in early May and the first week of October, and Chinese New Year, when everything is packed.

See our month-by-month guide to the best time to visit China for weather, crowds and prices through the year.

Getting there

Beijing Capital (PEK), Beijing Daxing (PKX) and Shanghai Pudong (PVG) are the main long-haul gateways, with direct flights from North America, Europe and across Asia.

Inside the country, the high-speed rail network is the way to travel — Beijing to Xi'an in under five hours, Beijing to Shanghai in about four and a half — with domestic flights linking the farther cities like Chengdu and Guilin.

The currency is the Chinese yuan (renminbi), and daily life is largely cashless via Alipay and WeChat Pay, which now accept foreign cards. Most visitors need a visa, though many can now use the 240-hour visa-free transit scheme — confirm the current rules with the official Chinese authority before booking.

China travel FAQ

When is the best time to visit China?

Spring (April to May) and autumn (September to early November) are the finest all-round seasons — mild, dry and comfortable for the Great Wall, the Forbidden City, Xi'an and the Li River. Summer (June to August) is hot, humid and the domestic peak, though it suits the far north and the high mountains; winter is cold in the north but quiet and cheap, and Harbin's ice festival is a highlight. Avoid the national holiday weeks — early May and the first week of October — and Chinese New Year, when sites and trains are jammed.

What currency is used in China?

The Chinese yuan, also called renminbi (CNY, symbol ¥). China is largely cashless — locals pay by phone with Alipay and WeChat Pay, and both apps now let foreign visitors link an overseas card. Cash is still accepted and worth carrying in smaller towns, and international cards work in many hotels and department stores. ATMs are widespread in the cities; tell your bank you are travelling before you go.

Do I need a visa to visit China?

Most visitors need a visa, but China's expanding visa-free transit scheme now lets travellers from many countries, including the US, UK, Canada and most of the EU, stay up to 10 days (240 hours) without one when transiting through major airports. Rules change often, so confirm the current visa and transit requirements with the official Chinese embassy or consulate before you book.

How many days do you need in China?

For a first trip, ten to fourteen days is ideal: three or four days in Beijing for the Great Wall and the Forbidden City, a day or two in Xi'an for the Terracotta Army, two or three days in Shanghai, and a few days for the Li River scenery around Guilin and Yangshuo. With only a week, pair Beijing with Xi'an and Shanghai and save the south for next time. China's high-speed trains link the cities quickly and comfortably.

Is China easy to get around for first-time visitors?

Yes. The world's largest high-speed rail network connects the major cities at up to 350 km/h, with Beijing to Xi'an in under five hours and Beijing to Shanghai in about four and a half. Book train and flight tickets in advance, carry your passport for station and hotel check-in, and download a translation app and offline maps before you arrive, as some Western sites and apps are blocked inside the country.
