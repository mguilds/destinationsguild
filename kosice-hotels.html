Kosice

Where to Stay in Kosice

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Kosice is the second city of Slovakia and the capital of its quiet half. It has about 230,000 people, a steelworks on the edge of town that for decades was the reason the city grew, and in the middle of it one of the best main streets in central Europe: Hlavna, nearly a kilometre long, wide enough to hold a park down the middle, with a cathedral standing in the centre of it and cafe tables along both sides. The cathedral is the largest church in Slovakia. You will read everywhere that it is the easternmost Gothic cathedral in Europe, and that is a guidebook habit rather than a fact — the Gothic churches of Transylvania are further east, as scholarship points out and the tourist brochures do not. What is true is that it is a very fine building in a city that most visitors to Slovakia never reach, and that Kosice is the right base for the whole east: Bardejov, the Slovak Paradise, the wooden churches and the Tokaj vineyards are all within a morning’s drive.

Where to stay in Kosice, area by area

The parts of Kosice a visitor actually chooses between, and who each one suits.

Hlavna and the cathedral

Most centralFlatWalk everywhere

The long pedestrian spine of the city, and the obvious place to stay. Within a few hundred metres you have the cathedral of St Elisabeth, the chapel of St Michael and the Urban Tower beside it, the State Theatre with the singing fountain in front of it, the Immaculata column put up after the plague of 1710 and 1711, and the best of the cafes, restaurants and bars. It is flat, wide, paved and almost entirely traffic-free, which makes it markedly easier walking than Bratislava’s cobbled old town, and a very good choice for anyone with a wheeled case or a slow pace. Rooms directly on the street can hear the evening trade at a weekend; a courtyard room solves it.

The quiet streets inside the old walls

QuieterStill centralGood value

Behind Hlavna on both sides, the grid of smaller streets — Mlynska, Kovacska, Zvonarska, Hrnciarska with its restored craftsmen’s lane — sits two or three minutes from the cathedral and is noticeably cheaper and quieter than the main street itself. This is where the small hotels and guesthouses in converted townhouses are. You lose nothing in walking distance and gain a night’s sleep. It is also where the surviving fragments of the city walls and the Executioner’s Bastion are, and where the Jewish synagogue and the memorial to the deportations stand.

The station and the Aupark end

TrainsShoppingCheaper

The southern end of Hlavna runs down to the main railway and bus stations, with the Aupark shopping centre and a run of larger modern hotels around them. It is a ten to fifteen minute flat walk up the street to the cathedral, so it is perfectly central in practice, and it is the right choice if you are arriving late by train, leaving early, or using Kosice as a base for day trips by rail. It is the least atmospheric part of the centre and the cheapest.

Kavecany and the hills above the city

GreenQuietCar useful

North of the city the ground rises into wooded hills, and at Kavecany there is a small ski slope, a zoo, a bobsleigh track and a cable car down into the valley. A handful of pensions and small hotels sit up here among the trees. It is ten to fifteen minutes by car or a bus ride from the centre, it is cool in high summer when the city is warm, and it suits a family or anyone who would rather sleep among trees than on a main street. You will want a car or to be content with the bus timetable.

Using Kosice as a base for the east

Day tripsCar usefulThe quiet half

This is the real argument for several nights here rather than one. From Kosice you can reach the Slovak Paradise gorges, Spis Castle and Levoca, the Dobsinska Ice Cave and the Slovak Karst caves, the Tokaj vineyards in the far south-east, and the wooden churches scattered along the Polish border. Bardejov, a walled UNESCO town with an intact square of burgher houses, is about an hour and a half north and is worth a night of its own if you have the time — it is one of the least visited good towns in Europe.

Getting around Kosice

Trams and buses

Kosice has an old and useful tram network plus trolleybuses and buses, run as one ticketed system. The trams loop around the old town rather than through it, because Hlavna is pedestrian, and they are the way to reach the station, the outer districts and the steelworks side of the city. Tickets come from machines, kiosks and the app and are time-based. For a visitor staying in the centre, the honest answer is that you will walk: the whole old town is about twenty minutes end to end on the flat.

Kosice Airport

Kosice Airport (KSC) is about six kilometres south of the city, a short bus or taxi ride from the centre. It is a small terminal with a modest timetable — connections through Vienna, Prague, Warsaw and a handful of seasonal routes — but it is the reason to think about flying into the east rather than driving the length of the country. If your trip is the Tatras, the Spis and the east, check Kosice before you default to Bratislava or Vienna; it can save you a whole day of driving at each end.

Trains and coaches

The main station is at the southern end of Hlavna, and the InterCity service to Bratislava takes between five and six hours through Zilina and the Vah valley, which is a scenic ride and a long one. Poprad, for the High Tatras, is about an hour and a quarter and is the single most useful connection from here. Trains also run to Budapest in around three hours, to Krakow with a change, and east to the Ukrainian border. Coaches are often quicker on the diagonal routes that the railway does not serve directly.

Driving, and what the car is for

The D1 motorway from the west is still not complete all the way through, so the drive from Bratislava takes longer than the distance suggests — allow most of a day with stops. Once here, the car is what unlocks the east: the wooden churches, the caves, the Tokaj villages and Bardejov are all road trips. A motorway vignette is required and is usually already on a hire car. Parking in the centre is paid and the old town itself is closed to traffic.

When to come

Kosice is a working city rather than a resort, so it keeps a fairly even calendar, and the shoulder months are the pick. May, June, September and early October give warm days, Hlavna full of tables, the singing fountain running and the east at its best for day trips. July and August are warm and the city is quieter than usual as the locals leave, which makes it a good time to be here even though it is the wrong time to be on a Tatra ridge. Late November and December bring a Christmas market the whole length of Hlavna, which is one of the best-looking market streets anywhere in the region. January and February are cold, grey and cheap.

One scheduling note: the Kosice Peace Marathon, the oldest marathon in Europe, is run on the first Sunday in October and fills the city’s hotels. If your dates fall on that weekend, book early or move them.

Slovakia has used the euro since 1 January 2009 — it was the first of the central European countries to adopt it and the second post-communist state after Slovenia, so there is no Slovak koruna to change and anything quoting one is describing the country before 2009. Cards are taken almost everywhere a visitor goes and contactless is normal on city transport; keep a little cash for a mountain hut, a village pub, a chairlift ticket office in the smaller valleys and a church collection box.

Getting in: what applies, and what does not

Slovakia is in the European Union, in the Schengen area since 21 December 2007 and in the euro since 1 January 2009, so arriving from Austria, Czechia, Poland or Hungary is an internal journey with no routine passport check and nothing to change. The rules below apply when you first enter the Schengen area from outside it.

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen external border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave. United States, Canadian, British and Australian citizens may visit as tourists without a visa for up to ninety days in any one hundred and eighty, counted across the whole Schengen area rather than against Slovakia alone.

We could not read the United States travel advisory for Slovakia on the day this page was written — the State Department’s site would not answer us — so we are not printing a level here rather than quoting one from memory. Read the current advisory yourself at travel.state.gov before you go; the level and the wording change, and it is a two-minute check.

Kosice — questions

Is Kosice worth the journey from Bratislava?

If you have a week or more in Slovakia, yes. It is five to six hours by train from Bratislava and most of a day by car, which is why so few visitors get here — and it is why the east feels different. Kosice itself is worth two nights for the cathedral, the main street and the eating, and it is the base for Bardejov, the Slovak Paradise, the caves, the wooden churches and Tokaj. If you only have four or five days in the country, spend them on Bratislava and the Tatras instead and save the east for a second trip. Alternatively, fly into Kosice and out of Bratislava, or the reverse, and see both without the double drive.

Is St Elisabeth Cathedral really the easternmost Gothic cathedral in Europe?

Probably not, and we would rather say so. The claim appears in almost every guidebook and on most tourist material, but it does not hold up: there are Gothic churches further east in Transylvania, a point the scholarly literature makes and the brochures skip. What is not in doubt is that it is the largest church in Slovakia — about sixty metres long, thirty-six wide, with room for more than five thousand people — that it was begun around 1378 and finished in the early sixteenth century, and that the Rakoczi crypt beneath it, built in 1906, holds the remains of Francis II Rakoczi, his mother Ilona Zrinyi and his son. Go and look at it; just do not repeat the line about it.

Which part of Kosice should I stay in?

Hlavna, if you want to step out of the door into the city and do not mind a little weekend noise — and unlike Bratislava, it is flat and paved rather than cobbled, which matters if you walk slowly. The small streets a block behind it are cheaper and quieter for no loss of walking distance. The station end suits a train-based trip or an early departure. Kavecany in the hills suits a car and a preference for trees.

Is Kosice a good base for the High Tatras?

It is a decent one and an unusual choice. Poprad is about an hour and a quarter by train, so a day in the mountains from Kosice is entirely possible, and Kosice has far more restaurants, hotels and things to do in the evening than the Tatra villages. But if the mountains are the main purpose of your trip you want to be in them: the early starts matter, the weather windows are short, and the Tatra Electric Railway from Poprad is what gets you to the trailheads. Base in Poprad or the resort villages for walking, and use Kosice for the Spis, the caves and the east.

How do I get to Bardejov and the wooden churches?

By car, realistically. Bardejov is about an hour and a half north of Kosice on good road and can be done by bus, and it is worth it — a complete walled square of burgher houses on the UNESCO list, with the Jewish suburb below the town and a spa in the woods above. The wooden churches are the harder trip: eight of them on the UNESCO list, scattered through small villages along the north-eastern border, several with short or seasonal opening and some kept locked with a key-holder in the village. Ring ahead or go with a guide, and give the whole thing a full day.

Is the east of Slovakia safe and easy for visitors?

Yes, on both counts, and it is quieter than the west rather than harder. The practical differences are that English is less widely spoken than in Bratislava, that the bus and train timetables thin out once you leave the main lines, and that some villages are genuinely small with nowhere open for lunch. Those are planning issues, not risks. The one thing to know is that the brown bear country of the central ranges is to the west of here, not around Kosice, so the walking advice in our Slovakia guide applies to the Low Tatras and the Fatras rather than to the Slovak Paradise and the Karst.

Do I need cash in Kosice?

Not much. Slovakia has used the euro since 2009, cards work almost everywhere in the city and contactless is normal on the trams. Carry some cash for the smaller villages in the east, for a wooden-church donation box, for a market stall and for the sort of pub that has been there for forty years and is not about to start taking cards.

What should I eat here?

The east has its own slant on the Slovak table. Look for pirohy, the filled dumplings, which are more an eastern thing than a western one; halusky in the sheep-cheese version as everywhere; and the soups — kapustnica, and the garlicky cesnakova that arrives in a hollowed loaf. The city has a good run of modern restaurants along and behind Hlavna at prices that would be a bargain in Bratislava, let alone Vienna. Drink Saris, which is brewed not far away, and ask about Slovak Tokaj, which comes from the vineyards an hour south-east of here and which almost nobody outside the country has heard of.
