Ecuador — the Andes, the Amazon and the Galápagos

Ecuador Travel Guide — Where to Stay, Region by Region

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Ecuador is the smallest of the Andean countries and the one that asks the least of your itinerary. Quito and Cuenca hold the two finest colonial centres in South America and both are World Heritage sites. The Avenue of the Volcanoes runs south from the capital between two files of snow peaks. Baños sits in hot springs where the mountains fall away towards the jungle. The Amazon starts a short flight east, and the Galápagos are two hours west over open Pacific. The money is the US dollar. Below is each region, where to sleep in it, and the two things that decide whether a trip here works — the altitude, and reading the current safety advice properly rather than from a headline.

Where to stay — the Guild’s Ecuador guides

Where to Stay in Quito ›

The capital, and where almost every trip starts. A colonial old town that is a World Heritage site, a modern north, and 2,850 metres of altitude to respect on your first day.

Where to Stay in Cuenca ›

The handsomest city in the country and the easiest to walk. Cobbles, river walks, a domed cathedral, and a long-stay foreign community that tells you something about the quality of life.

Where to Stay in Baños ›

Hot springs under a live volcano, and the jumping-off town for the waterfall road into the Amazon basin. The adventure capital, and also the place to sit in hot water.

Where to Stay in the Galápagos ›

Puerto Ayora on Santa Cruz is the one real town in the islands and the base for land-based island hopping — the alternative to a cruise, and a far cheaper one.

And the honest word on Guayaquil, which we have deliberately not written a book for. Guayaquil is the biggest city in Ecuador and one of the two airports that fly to the islands, so a great many visitors pass through it. It also sits in Guayas province, and as we write the UK Foreign Office advises against all but essential travel to the whole Coastal Region, Guayas included, while specifically excluding airside transit at Guayaquil airport. The same page singles out the city for a very high murder rate and advises extra caution in the city centre and the port areas. We are not going to write a cheerful where-to-stay guide to a place our own sourced advice says to avoid for non-essential travel. If you are connecting, stay airside or book an airport hotel through your airline or a transfer; if you are going anyway, on business or to see family, the live Trip.com list below is the honest tool and the decision is yours. The Andes, the Amazon and the Galapagos are all outside the advisory area, and a Quito arrival avoids the question altogether.

Why visit Ecuador

Because no other country packs this much difference into this little space. Ecuador is about the size of Colorado, and inside it you can stand on the equator in the morning, be at 4,000 metres by lunchtime and in Amazon rainforest the next day — and then fly 900 kilometres west and swim with sea lions that have no reason to be afraid of you. Because the Galápagos are not an exaggeration: the animals genuinely do not flee, and nothing else in travel is quite like it. Because the money is the US dollar, which removes a whole layer of friction for North American travellers. And because the old cities — Quito and Cuenca, both World Heritage sites — are lived-in rather than preserved, with churches, markets and lunch counters doing what they have always done.

Ecuador at a glance

Language

Spanish, and Kichwa in parts of the highlands. English is spoken in hotels, Galápagos tourism and the better restaurants, and much less so on buses, in markets and in small towns. A little Spanish goes a long way here, further than in most of Latin America.

Money

The US dollar. Ecuador adopted it in 2000, so there is nothing to convert and no exchange rate to watch. Ecuador’s own coins circulate alongside American ones at the same value. Carry small notes: fifties and hundreds are often refused outright.

Time and plugs

Two zones. The mainland is UTC−5 and the Galápagos are UTC−6, an hour behind — which catches people out on island flight days. No seasonal clock change. Sockets are the North American two- and three-pin type at 120 volts, so American and Canadian chargers need no adapter at all.

Getting in by air

Mariscal Sucre (UIO) at Quito takes most long-haul traffic and is the arrival we would choose. José Joaquín de Olmedo (GYE) at Guayaquil is the other international gateway and the second departure point for the islands — note the advisory above, which excludes airside transit. Island flights land at Baltra (GPS) for Santa Cruz and San Cristóbal (SCY).

Getting around

Long-distance buses are cheap, frequent and the way the country actually travels; a hired driver is the comfortable alternative on the Andean routes. The Foreign Office warns that Ecuador has one of the highest rates of road accidents in Latin America and advises against road travel after dark, which is worth taking literally in the mountains. There is no useful passenger rail network. Domestic flights handle Quito–Galápagos and Quito–Coca for the Amazon.

Altitude

Quito 2,850 metres, Cuenca 2,560, Baños a gentler 1,800, and the passes higher still. Nothing to be frightened of and everything to be sensible about: a quiet first day, plenty of water, and no volcano on day one. Guayaquil and the islands are at sea level.

The four Ecuadors

1. The Andes — Quito, Cuenca and the Avenue of the Volcanoes

The spine of the country, and where most of a mainland trip happens. Quito runs north to south along a high valley with the best-preserved colonial centre in the Americas at its southern end. South of the city the Pan-American Highway runs between two files of volcanoes — Cotopaxi, nearly perfectly conical, and Chimborazo, the highest point in the country and, because of the bulge of the equator, the point on the earth’s surface furthest from its centre. Baños sits where the mountains drop towards the jungle, with thermal baths fed by the volcano above it. Cuenca, further south, is the quiet, handsome one. North of Quito, Otavalo holds one of the great indigenous markets of the continent on a Saturday.

2. The Galápagos — 900 kilometres out in the Pacific

A province of Ecuador, a national park, and the reason a lot of people come. There are two ways to do it. A live-aboard cruise reaches the remote islands and visitor sites that day boats cannot, and costs accordingly. Land-based island hopping — sleeping in Puerto Ayora on Santa Cruz, or on San Cristóbal or Isabela, and taking day boats out — costs a fraction of that, puts you in a town with restaurants and a tortoise reserve on the doorstep, and is what our island book covers. The Foreign Office notes that there have been several serious accidents involving tour company boats and smaller vessels in the islands, and advises choosing reputable operators and checking for life vests before you board. It also records that flooding on Santa Cruz prompted an emergency declaration on 31 October 2026, so ask your hotel about current conditions before you travel.

3. The Amazon — the Oriente

Half of Ecuador’s land area and a tiny share of its visitors. The lodges are reached by flying to Coca and then going by motor canoe for anything from two to five hours, which is why three nights is the sensible minimum — one night is mostly boat. Yasuní and Cuyabeno are the two great reserves, with parrot clay licks, caiman on the night paddle and canopy towers. It is hot, humid and buggy, and it is the part of the trip people talk about afterwards. There is no Guild lodge book yet and we are not going to pretend otherwise.

4. The coast

Beaches, surf towns, fishing ports and the country’s biggest city. We are not writing it up as a holiday region while the Foreign Office advises against all but essential travel to the entire Coastal Region, and we will revisit that the moment the advice changes. The honest position is in the section above.

Things to do in Ecuador

Walk Quito’s old town on a Sunday morning when the streets are closed to cars. Stand with one foot either side of the line at the equator north of the city and accept that the monument is in the wrong place by a couple of hundred metres — the museum next door will tell you so itself. Take the Saturday market at Otavalo seriously; it is a working market, not a performance. Sit in the hot springs at Baños after dark with the mountain above you. Ride the waterfall road east out of Baños. Give Cuenca two unhurried days. Snorkel off Santa Cruz and understand within ten seconds why the islands are famous. Fly to Coca and go up a river.

Best time to visit & getting there

Ecuador sits on the equator, so there is no summer and winter — there is wet and dry, and it differs by region. In the Andes, June to September is the driest and clearest stretch, with December and January a shorter second window; February to May brings afternoon rain. In the Galápagos, December to May is warmer, wetter and calmer at sea with the best water for snorkelling, while June to November is cooler and drier with rougher crossings and livelier wildlife. The Amazon is wet all year and wetter from March to July. One thing to plan around: the Foreign Office records that the Ecuadorian authorities have issued a red alert ahead of the forecast 2026–2027 El Niño event, which may bring heavy rain, flooding and landslides. The month-by-month detail is in our best time to visit Ecuador guide.

Fishing here

Ecuador does not have a Guild fishing book yet. What is here is worth naming honestly rather than overselling: high-altitude trout in the Andean lakes and streams, stocked long ago and fished locally rather than commercially guided, and offshore billfish and tuna out of the Pacific ports — which sit inside the coastal area the Foreign Office currently advises against for non-essential travel, so we are not going to sell it. Fishing in the Galápagos is tightly restricted inside the marine reserve and is a matter for licensed local operators, not for a booking link. The whole shelf is in The Guild Fishing Library.

Travelling in your later years

Altitude is the whole conversation here, and it is a manageable one. Quito at 2,850 metres and Cuenca at 2,560 are high enough to be felt — shortness of breath on a stairway, a headache, broken sleep on the first night — and a trip that lands in Quito and goes straight up a volcano is the one that goes wrong. Build in a quiet first day. Drink water, skip the first evening’s wine, and leave the cable car at Quito and anything above 4,000 metres until day three. If you have a heart or lung condition, or you use oxygen, that is a conversation with your own doctor before you book. A useful trick: arrange the trip low to high — islands or coast first, Quito and Cuenca after — rather than the other way round.

Cuenca is the gentlest city in the country for anyone who would rather not climb: the historic core is largely flat, the river walk is level and the distances are short. Quito’s old town is steep and cobbled, beautiful and hard underfoot, and taxis are cheap enough to use freely. Baños is mostly flat in town, and the thermal baths themselves are the kind of thing an adventure town does not advertise to older visitors but should. In the Galápagos, the limiting factor is getting in and out of boats — a wet landing means stepping off a dinghy into shallow water, and a dry landing means a step up onto a dock, often with a swell. Say plainly what you can and cannot manage when you book a day trip, because the good operators will tell you honestly which sites to skip, and ask specifically about the panga step. The Foreign Office’s warning about island boats and life vests applies to everyone and doubly to anyone unsteady on their feet.

Getting in: what to expect

No visa for a holiday, and a passport with six months left on it. The UK Foreign Office states that British passport holders can visit Ecuador without a visa for up to 90 days in any 12-month period, and that the passport must have an expiry date at least six months after the date of arrival. Most other Western nationalities are treated the same way, but that is a page written for British passports and you should read the one written for yours.

The Galápagos have their own front door. The same Foreign Office page states that tourists can stay in the islands for up to 60 days, and that you must buy a transit control card at your departure airport before you check in for the island flight — so you do that at Quito or Guayaquil, on the way, and you keep a copy. A national park entrance fee is payable on arrival in the islands. Both amounts are set by the Ecuadorian authorities and were last revised in 2024; we are not printing a figure we could not confirm on an official source, so budget for both, pay only at the official desks, and check the current amounts before you fly. Your luggage is inspected for plant and animal material on the way in — that is a biosecurity check, not a formality, and it is the reason the islands still look the way they do.

And read the advisory properly rather than the headline. As we write, the Foreign Office advises against all but essential travel to the Coastal Region provinces and to within 20 kilometres of the Colombian border, with named exceptions in Carchi including the Rumichaca crossing, Tulcán, the El Ángel Ecological Reserve and the Pan-American Highway. It also records a 60-day state of emergency declared on 15 September 2026 across eight provinces, which brought more police and military onto the streets and, for a fortnight at the end of September, overnight curfews in four of them. None of that covers Quito, Cuenca, Baños, the Avenue of the Volcanoes, the Amazon lodges or the Galápagos. Advice of this kind changes in both directions, so check the current page for your own passport before you book, and buy insurance that covers disruption.

Ecuador — questions

How many days do I need in Ecuador?

Ten to fourteen nights if the Galapagos are part of the plan, and about a week if they are not. The islands are a trip inside a trip: four or five nights there is the honest minimum once flights, the arrival formalities and the boat day are taken out. On the mainland, two nights in Quito, two in Cuenca and two in Banos covers the Andes comfortably, and the Amazon lodges want three nights because the first and last are mostly travel.

Do I need a visa for Ecuador, and what is different about the Galapagos?

The UK Foreign Office states that British passport holders can visit Ecuador without a visa for up to 90 days in any 12-month period, and that your passport must have an expiry date at least six months after the date you arrive. For the Galapagos the same page states that tourists can stay up to 60 days, and that you must buy a transit control card at your departure airport before you check in for the island flight, and keep a copy. There is also a national park entrance fee payable on arrival in the islands. Both amounts are set by the Ecuadorian authorities and we do not print a figure we cannot source officially: pay only through the official channels at the airport or the park, and confirm the current amount before you travel. Rules change, so check your own government's current advice before you book.

Is Ecuador safe to visit?

That is not a question we will answer with a flat yes or no, because the honest answer depends on where you are going. As we write, the UK Foreign Office advises against all but essential travel to the whole Coastal Region - Esmeraldas, Manabi, Santa Elena, Guayas, El Oro, Los Rios and Santo Domingo de los Tsachilas - and to areas within 20 kilometres of the Colombian border, with named exceptions in Carchi province. Airside transit through Guayaquil airport is specifically excluded from that advice, which matters because that is how many people reach the Galapagos. Quito, Cuenca, Banos, the Avenue of the Volcanoes, the Amazon lodges and the Galapagos are all outside those areas. The same page says mugging and pickpocketing are common and to take care in Quito's old town and at transport hubs, that kidnapping rates have risen, and that Ecuador has one of the highest rates of road accidents in Latin America. Read your own government's current advice, which is written for your passport and updated far more often than any guidebook.

Will the altitude in Quito be a problem?

It is the thing most people underestimate. Quito sits at about 2,850 metres and Cuenca at about 2,560, which is high enough that a brisk walk uphill on your first afternoon will stop you. Give yourself a quiet first day, drink more water than you think you need, go easy on alcohol for the first evening and do not plan a volcano or the Quito cable car for day one - the Foreign Office warns specifically about altitude sickness at Pichincha, reached by that cable car. If you have a heart or lung condition, ask your own doctor before you book rather than after you land. Guayaquil and the Galapagos are at sea level, so a trip that starts low and climbs is kinder than one that does the reverse.

What is the money, and will my cards work?

The money is the US dollar. Ecuador adopted it in 2000, so for American and Canadian visitors there is no conversion to do and no exchange desk to find, and prices read exactly as they look. Ecuador mints its own coins, which circulate alongside US ones and are worth the same. Cards are accepted in city hotels, restaurants and larger shops; carry small notes for markets, taxis, buses and village cafes, and be aware that a fifty or a hundred is often refused. The Foreign Office notes that using unregistered taxis significantly increases your risk, so book through your hotel or a known dispatch app rather than hailing one in the street.
