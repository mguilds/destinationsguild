Kaohsiung

Where to Stay in Kaohsiung

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Kaohsiung is the flattest, warmest and most relaxed of Taiwan’s cities, a working port that spent twenty years turning its old harbour over to art, cycling and water, and it is where the winter sun is when the north of the island is grey. It is also easy to choose in: two metro lines and a circular light railway mean most of the obvious districts are a few minutes apart, so the question is whether you want the harbour, the fast train or the city centre.

Yancheng and the harbour at Pier-2 — where we would stay

Best for a first visitWaterfrontFerry to Cijin

The old warehouse district at the mouth of the harbour has become the best part of Kaohsiung: the Pier-2 Art Center occupies a long run of restored godowns with galleries, studios, bookshops and bicycle paths between them, the light railway runs along the water, the Cijin ferry leaves from the end of the street and the Music Center and the great swooping library are a few minutes further on. Behind it, Yancheng is the oldest commercial quarter in the city, with a covered market, a famous night market and some genuinely old streets. There are boutique hotels in converted buildings and a few larger ones on the water. It is flat, it is walkable, it is on the metro’s orange line, and the sunsets are free.

Zuoying and the high speed rail station — arrive and go

Fast trainLotus PondBig modern hotels

Unlike Taichung and Tainan, Kaohsiung’s high speed rail station is inside the city and on the metro’s red line, which is a considerable convenience. Zuoying is the district around it: large newish hotels, a big shopping centre and, ten minutes away, the Lotus Pond with its dragon-and-tiger pagodas, the Confucius Temple and the Spring and Autumn pavilions. It is about fifteen minutes on the metro to the city centre and twenty-five to the harbour. Stay here for an early train, for a quieter and more spacious room for the money, or if you are using Kaohsiung as a base for day trips. It is a suburb rather than a destination in the evening.

Central Park, Sanduo and the Love River — the city centre

ShoppingNight marketsMost hotels

The commercial middle of Kaohsiung runs along the red line from Formosa Boulevard — whose station holds the Dome of Light, the stained-glass ceiling that is worth going out of your way for — down through Central Park to Sanduo. This is where the department stores, the cinemas, the big chain hotels and the Liuhe and Ruifeng night markets are, and the Love River, with its promenades and small boats, cuts through the top of it. It is the most convenient district for the metro and the widest choice of rooms at every price. It is also the least distinctive; you could be in the middle of several Asian cities.

Cijin — the sandbar, across the water

QuietestBeach and seafoodFive-minute ferry

Cijin is the long thin island that shelters the harbour, five minutes away on a passenger ferry that takes bicycles and scooters and runs late. It has a beach, a lighthouse, an old fort, a temple-lined main street and a row of seafood restaurants where you choose the fish. Most people come for an afternoon and leave; staying is a different and rather nice idea, because the island empties in the evening. Be clear about the trade-offs: there are few hotels, the choice of dinner is seafood or seafood, and everything else in Kaohsiung is a ferry ride away. Best for a second visit or a quiet night at the end of a trip.

Getting around

Two metro lines cross at Formosa Boulevard, the red one running north to south through the fast-train station and the city centre and the orange one running east to west to the harbour, and a circular light railway loops round the waterfront and the old port railway alignment. Between them they reach nearly everything a visitor wants. An iPASS covers the metro, the light rail, the buses, the Cijin ferry and the hire bicycles.

Kaohsiung International Airport is on the red line, about fifteen minutes from the city centre, which makes arriving here markedly less work than arriving at Taoyuan — worth knowing if you are planning a winter trip, since it puts you straight into the good weather.

The city is flat, and that changes what is possible: the harbour cycle paths, the Love River promenades and the Cijin seafront are level and continuous, hire bicycles are everywhere, and a walk along the water at dusk is the best thing to do here for nothing. In high summer, do the outdoor part of the day early. Taxis are cheap, metered and plentiful for the rest.

When to come

Taiwan’s weather is split in two by the mountain range down its middle, and Kaohsiung sits on the southern, sheltered side of it. October and November are the best weeks of the year here, as they are everywhere on the island: warm, dry, clear and comfortable, with the typhoon season finished. Late March and April are the next best, before the humidity arrives and before the plum rains of mid-May to mid-June.

Summer, June to September, is hot and humid with heavy convective rain in the afternoons, and it is the typhoon window; it is also the cheapest time for a room. Winter is Kaohsiung’s season. From December to February, while Taipei sits under drizzle, the mountains keep the northeast monsoon off the south and the city is dry and sunny at twenty to twenty-eight degrees, which is close to perfect for the harbour, the cycle paths and Cijin.

The one week to book around, wherever you are going, is Lunar New Year, in late January or February: the whole island travels at once, trains sell out, room rates roughly double and many small restaurants close for several days. Getting in

Citizens of the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, the European Union and Schengen countries, Australia, New Zealand, Japan and South Korea, among others, are admitted to Taiwan visa-free for ninety days, with no fee and no application. Some other nationalities get thirty or fourteen days and some need a visa in advance, and the list is revised from time to time, so check your own passport at boca.gov.tw. There is also a free Taiwan Online Arrival Card to complete shortly before you land; use the official government site and not an agent charging for the same form.

Buy an EasyCard or an iPASS in the arrivals hall and keep it in your pocket for the rest of the trip: it pays for the metros, the buses, the local trains, the ferries and the hire bicycles the length of the island, and for your coffee in the convenience store. Kaohsiung hotels FAQ

Which part of Kaohsiung should I stay in?

Yancheng and the harbour, around Pier-2, for a first visit. It is the most interesting part of the city, it is flat and walkable, the light railway and the orange metro line both serve it, and the Cijin ferry leaves from the end of the street. Stay at Zuoying instead if you have an early fast train or want more room for the money, and in the Central Park area if you want the widest choice of hotels and the night markets on the doorstep.

Is Kaohsiung better than Taipei in winter?

For weather, yes, and the difference is larger than people expect. From December to February the northeast monsoon leaves Taipei damp and overcast at twelve to eighteen degrees, while Kaohsiung, on the other side of the mountains, is usually dry and sunny at twenty to twenty-eight. If you are planning a winter trip to Taiwan, flying into Kaohsiung and working northwards is a better plan than the reverse.

How do I get to Cijin?

By the passenger ferry from Gushan, near Pier-2, which takes about five minutes, costs very little, is covered by your iPASS and runs late into the evening. It takes bicycles and scooters as well as foot passengers. There is also a road tunnel, but the ferry is quicker, cheaper and much more enjoyable.

Is the airport convenient?

Unusually so. Kaohsiung International Airport sits on the metro's red line about fifteen minutes from the city centre, so there is no airport express to work out and no long taxi ride. It handles regional international flights and domestic services; most long-haul arrivals still land at Taoyuan in the north, from where the high speed rail reaches Kaohsiung in well under two hours.

How long should I stay?

Two or three nights. That is enough for the harbour and Pier-2, a Cijin afternoon, the Lotus Pond and the Dome of Light, an evening at a night market and a day trip to Fo Guang Shan or further south. Add two more if you want Kenting, the beach end of the island, which is about two hours away by bus and really wants its own nights rather than a day trip.
