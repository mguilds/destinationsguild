Riga

Where to Stay in Riga

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Riga is by some distance the biggest city in the Baltic states and the grandest thing in Latvia. It stands on both banks of the Daugava a little inland from the Gulf of Riga, and it comes in two layers: a compact medieval core of guild halls, brick gables and cobbled lanes, and then, ringing it, boulevard after boulevard of Art Nouveau apartment buildings thrown up in the boom before the First World War — one of the densest collections of that architecture anywhere in Europe, and the reason the historic centre has been a UNESCO World Heritage site since 1997. It is flat, it is walkable end to end, and it takes a fraction of the visitors that cities half as interesting manage.

Where to stay in Riga, area by area

The parts of Riga a visitor actually chooses between, and who each one suits.

Vecriga: the Old Town

Most centralWalk to everythingNoisy at weekends

The medieval core inside the line of the old walls, between the Daugava and the canal: the Dome Square, St Peter’s, the House of the Blackheads, the Three Brothers and a tangle of cobbled lanes with the best of the city’s restaurants in them. Almost everything a short-stay visitor wants is within ten minutes on foot. Two honest warnings. The cobbles are uneven and slippery wet, which matters if your knees do. And a handful of streets around Kalku iela and the Livu Square bars are loud on Friday and Saturday nights, because Riga has spent twenty years as a stag-party destination and has not entirely shaken it off; ask for a courtyard room, or stay one street out.

The Quiet Centre: the Art Nouveau streets

Best for architectureResidential calmShort walk in

North of the canal, around Alberta iela, Elizabetes iela and Vilandes iela, is the Klusais centrs — the Quiet Centre — and it is the single nicest place to stay in Riga if you want the city rather than the tourist version of it. These are the Art Nouveau boulevards the UNESCO listing is partly about: five and six storeys of masks, peacocks and plasterwork, with embassies, galleries and small boutique hotels and apartments behind the facades. It is ten to fifteen minutes on foot into the Old Town through the canal park, it is quiet at night, and the rooms are usually better value than the equivalent inside the walls.

The boulevard ring and the Opera

Easiest first visitBig hotelsLevel walking

The ring of nineteenth-century boulevards and parks laid out where the fortifications used to be, with the canal winding through them, the Freedom Monument at the middle and the Opera House beside it. Most of the city’s larger international hotels are on or just off this ring. It is the easy choice for a first visit: level pavement rather than cobbles, the Old Town on one side and the Art Nouveau streets on the other, both a few minutes’ walk, and trams and trolleybuses from the door.

The station, the market and the Academy tower

Best valueTransport hubMixed streets

South-east of the Old Town, around the central station and the Central Market, is where the value is: mid-range and chain hotels, the bus station, the commuter trains for Jurmala and Sigulda, and the market pavilions, one of them a converted Zeppelin hangar. Behind it rises the Stalin-era Academy of Sciences tower and the old wooden Moscow suburb beyond. It is a working part of town rather than a pretty one, and the streets immediately around the station and the market are scruffier after dark than anywhere else a visitor is likely to be. Walk two blocks out and it is fine; pick your hotel by its street, not just its price.

Pardaugava: across the river

CheapestBest skyline viewsTram in

The left bank, facing the Old Town across the Daugava: Agenskalns with its streets of wooden houses, Kipsala, and the glass wedge of the National Library on the waterfront. Rooms and apartments here are the cheapest in central Riga, the view back at the spires and the castle is the one on all the postcards, and trams cross the bridges into town in a few minutes. There is less to walk to in the evening, so it suits a longer stay, a family or anybody happy to ride a tram, better than a two-night visit.

Getting around Riga

On foot

The Old Town, the boulevard ring and the Quiet Centre form one continuous walkable city about two kilometres across, and it is flat. Walking is how you should see Riga. The only real obstacles are the Old Town cobbles and, in winter, the ice on them.

Trams, trolleybuses and buses

A dense network run as one system covers the whole city, including across the river and out to the suburbs. Buy a ticket before you board where you can — kiosks and machines sell them — and check what the driver or validator accepts on the day, because the payment arrangements change from time to time. Services are frequent and run late.

The commuter train

The single most useful thing for a visitor. Trains from Riga Central run out to Jurmala in about half an hour, and up the Gauja valley to Sigulda and Cesis in roughly one to one and a half hours, several times a day. They are cheap, level with the platform at the main stations and far less effort than driving.

To and from the airport

Riga’s airport is about ten kilometres west of the centre and is the largest in the Baltic states. A city bus connects it with the middle of town, there is an express coach, and a rail and tram link has been built out that way too, so ask at the arrivals desk which is quickest when you land rather than relying on an old guidebook. Taxis and ride-hailing apps both work; agree the fare or use the app rather than taking a street price.

Taxis and apps

Ride-hailing apps are widely used and are the simplest way to get a fair price. For street taxis, use a marked rank and look for the fare card in the window — the ranks immediately outside the Old Town gates and the big hotels have a long-standing reputation for charging what they can get.

When to come

Riga is a year-round city and the season changes what sort of trip you get rather than whether to come. June brings light until nearly midnight, the lindens in flower and the parks full. July and August are the warmest and busiest, and the only time a lot of Riga’s own residents are out at the beach instead. Late September and early October are our pick: long gold light, no crowds, the Gauja valley turning an hour away, and rooms cheaper than in summer. December brings the Christmas market to the Dome Square and dark by four in the afternoon. January to mid-March is cold, often snowy, and the cheapest and emptiest time of the year. The dates that fill the city are midsummer on 23 and 24 June, Independence Day on 18 November, the Christmas market weekends, and — about every five years, next in 2028 — the week of the Song and Dance Celebration, when there is effectively no room to be had.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Latvia is in the European Union, the Schengen area and the euro, so arriving from anywhere else in Schengen there is no check at all. Month-by-month weather and the dates worth planning around are in Best Time to Visit Latvia, and the country guide is The Latvia Travel Guide.

Riga — questions

Which part of Riga should I stay in?

For two or three nights and no strong feelings, stay on the boulevard ring: it is level, central and five minutes from both the Old Town and the Art Nouveau streets. For the nicest version of Riga, stay in the Quiet Centre among the Art Nouveau buildings and walk in through the canal park. Stay in the Old Town itself if being inside the walls matters to you, and ask for a courtyard room away from the bar streets. Stay near the station or across the river in Pardaugava if the budget is the deciding factor.

Is Riga Old Town noisy?

Parts of it, on Friday and Saturday nights. Riga spent two decades as a cheap-flight stag-party destination and the legacy is a cluster of bars around the Livu Square and Kalku iela that is loud until the small hours. The rest of the Old Town is quiet, and the whole of it is quiet midweek. If you are booking inside the walls for a weekend, ask the hotel directly for a room facing a courtyard rather than a street.

Do I need to worry about safety?

Riga is a safe European capital and ordinary care is enough. The two things worth knowing are practical rather than alarming: pickpocketing in the Old Town and the Central Market in high season, and the streets immediately around the central station and the market, which are scruffier after dark than anywhere else a visitor is likely to walk. Taxis touted outside the Old Town gates have a long-standing reputation for overcharging; use an app or a marked rank with a fare card.

Is Riga walkable, and is it hilly?

Very walkable, and almost entirely flat. The Old Town, the boulevard ring and the Quiet Centre run into one another over about two kilometres, so a visitor on foot can cover the whole of central Riga without ever taking a tram. The cobbles in the Old Town are the only difficulty, and in winter they ice over, so boots with grip matter more here than a map.

How long do I need in Riga?

Two full days covers the Old Town, the market, St Peter’s tower and a proper walk through the Art Nouveau streets. Three lets you add a museum or two, the Corner House and an evening at the Opera without rushing. Beyond that, use Riga as a base: Jurmala is half an hour away by train and Sigulda and Cesis are an hour or so, and all three are better as day trips from the city than as separate stays for a short visit.

Can I use Riga as a base for the whole country?

For the northern half of it, easily. The commuter railway reaches Jurmala, Sigulda and Cesis, the coach station reaches almost everywhere else, and nothing in Vidzeme or on the Gulf coast is more than a couple of hours away. For the Kurzeme coast at Liepaja or Ventspils, or the Latgale lakes in the south-east, the journeys get long enough that a night or two out there is better than a day return.

When is the Song and Dance Celebration, and does it matter to me?

The nationwide celebration happens about every five years; the last was in 2023 and the next is set for the summer of 2028. It brings tens of thousands of singers and dancers into Riga for a week, and the city fills completely. If your travel year is one of them, it is either the best possible week to be here or the worst possible week to find a room, depending on whether you book early. Check the dates before you fix anything.
