Where to Stay in Hallstatt

Where to Stay in Hallstatt — Best Hotels & Neighbourhoods

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated August 2026

Hallstatt is the village on every poster of Austria: a few hundred wooden and pastel houses stacked up a mountainside above a green lake, with a church spire at the water’s edge and salt mines above that have been worked for three thousand years. It is also very small — a little over seven hundred people live here — and on a summer day it receives many times that number between ten in the morning and four in the afternoon. That single fact should shape your whole visit. Stay the night and you get the village at breakfast and at dusk, when the coaches have gone and the lake goes still, which is when Hallstatt is genuinely one of the loveliest places in Europe. The second thing to know is that this is a shore so steep the houses climb it in stairs: lanes are narrow, steps are everywhere, and which house you choose matters far more here than its star rating. Stay the night — that is the whole point

Between about ten in the morning and four in the afternoon Hallstatt belongs to the day trippers, and the main lane can be shoulder to shoulder. Before and after those hours it belongs to the people staying, and it is quiet, cool and astonishingly beautiful. If you are only passing through, come early or come late. If you are staying, plan your day the other way round from everyone else: see the village at breakfast, go up to the salt mine or across to the Dachstein caves in the middle of the day, and come back down for the evening. One night is enough to change the visit entirely.

Stairs, and how to avoid the worst of them

The village is built on a strip of shore a few metres wide, and everything else climbs. Lanes are cobbled and narrow, many houses are reached by steps, and a good number of guesthouses are old buildings with no lift at all. This is the single most important thing to ask about before you book: how many steps from the road to the front door, and how many from the front door to the room. The flattest part of the village is the Lahn, the southern end where the bus stops, the boats leave and the funicular to the salt mine starts — if walking is difficult, look there first.

Getting here: the ferry is the lovely way

Trains on the Salzkammergut line do not stop in the village — the station, Hallstatt Bahnhof, is across the water, and a small ferry meets the trains and crosses to the village in about ten minutes. It is one of the best arrivals in Austria, and it lands you in the middle of the old part. From Salzburg the public route is a bus to Bad Ischl and a train onward, about two and a half hours in total; by car it is around an hour and a half. Note the ferry is a boat with a step, and it stops running in the evening — check the last crossing if your train is late.

Cars, coaches and drones — the rules have tightened

Hallstatt has spent several years trying to manage the crowds, and the rules now have teeth. Private cars are kept out of the village core; you park in the numbered lots on the approach and walk in, and in high season those lots fill by late morning. Coach numbers and arrival times are restricted. Drones are banned over the village. None of this affects an overnight guest much — tell your hotel you are arriving by car and they will tell you exactly where to leave it, and many have an arrangement — but it matters a great deal if you were planning to drive in at noon in July.

Hallstatt Hotels — common questions

Is it worth staying overnight in Hallstatt?

Yes, and it is close to the only way to see the place properly. Hallstatt receives an enormous number of day visitors between mid-morning and late afternoon, and in those hours the main lane can be uncomfortably crowded. Guests who stay the night get the two best parts of the day to themselves: the early morning, when the light is on the far shore and the village is silent, and the evening, when the coaches have gone. One night is enough to turn a disappointing stop into the memory people come home talking about.

Is Hallstatt suitable for someone who cannot manage stairs?

It can be, but only with care in choosing. The village is built up a steep shore, and much of the old part is reached by steps and narrow lanes, with old houses that have no lift. The flat ground is at the southern end, the Lahn, where the buses stop, the boats leave and the funicular to the salt mine begins; guesthouses there can be reached without climbing, and the shore path into the centre is level. Wherever you book, write and ask two questions: how many steps from the road to the door, and how many from the door to the room.

How do you get to Hallstatt from Salzburg or Vienna?

From Salzburg the usual public route is a bus to Bad Ischl and a train onward to Hallstatt station, about two and a half hours in all; by car it is roughly an hour and a half. From Vienna, take the train to Attnang-Puchheim and change onto the Salzkammergut line, about four hours in total. The station is on the far side of the lake, and a small ferry meets the trains and crosses to the village in about ten minutes — a lovely arrival, but note that it involves a step down into the boat and that the last crossing is in the evening.

Can I drive into Hallstatt and park?

You cannot drive into the village centre. Private cars are kept out of the core and directed to numbered car parks on the approach road, from which you walk in; in summer those fill by the middle of the morning. Coach numbers and times are restricted, and drones are banned over the village. If you are staying the night, tell your hotel you are coming by car when you book — they will tell you which lot to use and whether they have an arrangement, and that one e-mail saves an hour of circling.

When is the best time to visit Hallstatt?

May, and late September into October. The weather is kind, the lake is green, the mountain paths and the salt mine are open, and the crowds are a fraction of the July and August peak. Midsummer is the busiest and hottest; winter is beautiful and quiet, though snow and ice on those lanes are a real consideration for anyone unsteady, and the days are short. Whenever you come, the time of day matters as much as the month: before ten and after four are the hours worth having.

What is there to do besides look at the village?

More than a day’s worth. The salt mine above the village is the oldest still working in the world, reached by a funicular railway, with a viewing platform — the Skywalk — that hangs out over the lake. The village museum tells the Iron Age story that gave the whole Hallstatt period its name. Boats cross the lake and can be hired by the hour. And across the water at Obertraun a cable car climbs to the Dachstein ice caves and the mountain terraces, which makes an excellent middle-of-the-day excursion while the village below is at its busiest.
