Kotor

Where to Stay in Kotor

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Kotor is a walled town of a few thousand people at the blind end of a long flooded river valley, with a mountain standing more or less vertically behind it. That shape decides everything about where you sleep. Inside the walls you are in the middle of the picture and also in the middle of the noise, and you will carry your bag the last few hundred metres because no car comes in. Along the water to the north, in Dobrota, you get a room with a view of the bay and a flat ten- or twenty-minute walk into town. Across the water in Muo and Prčanj you get the view back at Kotor, which is the better of the two views, and you need wheels or a short taxi. Further up the bay, Perast is a different and quieter holiday altogether.

Where to stay in Kotor, area by area

The parts of Kotor a visitor actually chooses between, and who each one suits.

Inside the walls: Stari Grad

Most centralCar-free lanesLively at night

The old town is a small triangle of stone lanes between the sea gate and the mountain, and it is genuinely car-free, so taxis and cars stop at the gates and you walk the rest. Staying inside is the romantic choice and the practical one if you want to be out late: every restaurant, every bar and the start of the fortress climb are within three minutes. Two things to know before you book. The lanes amplify sound, and the bars on the squares run late in summer, so ask for a room off the square or at the back if you are a light sleeper. And on cruise days the town fills between the middle of the morning and the late afternoon, which is the main reason to be staying here rather than arriving for the day — the early morning and the evening belong to the people who slept in it.

Just outside the gates: Škaljari, Tabacina and the marina

Easy parkingTwo minutes from the gateQuieter nights

The streets immediately outside the walls get you the old town at a walking pace of two or three minutes while putting a road and a bit of distance between you and the late bars. This is the sensible choice if you are driving, because parking inside the walls does not exist and the car parks here are the ones everyone ends up using. Škaljari climbs the slope behind, so check how many steps stand between the road and your door before booking — some of these buildings are up a flight or two, and the hill is steeper than it looks on a map.

Dobrota: the waterfront walk north

Bay viewsFlat walk inSwimming off the shore

Dobrota is a long ribbon of houses, churches and small hotels strung along the water north of Kotor, and it is where most visitors who want a view and a quiet night actually stay. The waterfront path runs the whole way, flat and lit, so walking into town takes somewhere between ten minutes from the near end and half an hour from the far end — worth pinning your exact address before you book, because “Dobrota” covers several kilometres. You can swim off the rocks and concrete platforms here, which you cannot really do in town.

Muo and Prčanj: the view back at the town

Best viewResidentialNeed wheels or a taxi

Directly across the water from Kotor, Muo and then Prčanj look straight back at the walls and the fortress zigzagging up the cliff, which is the view people photograph and is much better from here than from inside it. These are working villages rather than resorts, so expect a shop, a couple of places to eat and not much else; you drive or take a short taxi round the head of the bay to get into town, five to ten minutes depending on the traffic. Perast: the quiet alternative up the bay

CalmestBoats to the isletsSmall and expensive

Perast is a single row of baroque stone houses facing two small islands, about twenty minutes up the bay by road, and it is the most peaceful place to sleep in the whole Boka. There are no real shops, a handful of restaurants and no beach to speak of — people swim off ladders and platforms — and it has its own cruise-hour rush when the tour buses come for the boat trip out to Our Lady of the Rocks. If you want the bay without the town, this is it; if you want anything to do after dinner, stay in Kotor instead.

Getting around Kotor

On foot

Inside the walls there is no alternative, and none is needed: nowhere in the old town is more than a few minutes from anywhere else. The surfaces are polished stone and are slippery in the wet, and there are steps almost everywhere, which is worth weighing if you are unsteady on your feet or travelling with a case.

The fortress climb

The path up to the fortress of St John behind the town is a stone staircase of well over a thousand steps, exposed most of the way, with the view improving the whole time. It is a real climb rather than a stroll, there is a fee at the gate in season, and the sensible times to do it are early morning or the last hours of daylight rather than the middle of a summer day. Take water; there is little shade.

Driving and parking

A car is the practical way to see the rest of the country, and a liability in Kotor itself. Nothing parks inside the walls, the waterfront car parks fill in season, and the coastal road through the bay is single-carriageway and slow when the cruise coaches are moving. If you are driving between Herceg Novi and Kotor or Budva, the Kamenari–Lepetane ferry across the narrows saves a long loop around the inner bay; it runs frequently and takes only a few minutes.

Buses and boats

Buses connect Kotor with Budva, Herceg Novi, Podgorica and the airports, and the station is a short walk from the gates. In summer small boats run across and up the bay, including to Perast and the islets, which is a pleasanter way to move than the road. Timetables thin out sharply out of season, so check on the day rather than trusting a summer schedule.

When to come, and what it costs

Kotor has a mild, very wet winter and a hot summer, and the mountain behind it makes both more extreme than the numbers suggest: the bay is one of the wettest places in Europe, with most of that rain falling between late autumn and early spring, and in July and August the walls hold the heat well into the night. May, June, September and early October are the months the Guild would choose — warm enough to swim, light enough to climb, and without the full weight of the cruise season. Prices follow the same curve, with July and August dearest by a distance and many smaller places closing altogether from November to March.

Getting in: Montenegro is outside the EU and outside Schengen

Montenegro uses the euro but is not in the European Union and not in the Schengen area. That has one very useful consequence for a traveller: days spent here do not count against the ninety days in any hundred and eighty that Schengen allows, and the reverse is also true. Citizens of the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, Australia and the European Union do not need a visa for a short holiday, with ninety days in any hundred and eighty the usual allowance — confirm your own nationality’s terms with your own foreign ministry rather than with any website. Your passport should have been issued within the last ten years and be valid for at least three months beyond your departure, and you should make sure it is stamped on the way in and out, because those stamps are the record of your stay.

Every stay must be reported to the police. Montenegro’s own government says that duty falls on the accommodation provider, and hotels do it automatically at check-in; British government advice tells visitors to make sure they are registered within twenty-four hours and warns of a fine if they are not. In a hotel it is handled for you. In a private flat, ask your host to confirm it is done and keep the slip. Crossing to or from Croatia is a Schengen border with full checks and, in high summer, queues — and if you are driving a hire car across it, confirm first that the rental company permits it.

Kotor — questions

Should I stay inside the walls or outside?

Inside if the evenings matter most to you and you are travelling light; outside if you are driving, if you want a view of the town rather than a view from inside it, or if you sleep badly. The old town is at its best very early and after about nine in the evening, and those hours belong to the people staying in it. Against that, no car reaches your door, the lanes are loud in summer, and almost everything is up or down steps. Dobrota is the usual compromise: a flat walk in, a view out, and a quiet night.

How bad are the cruise days really?

Busy rather than unbearable, and predictable, which is the useful part. When ships are in, the old town and the walk out to Perast fill from the middle of the morning until the late afternoon, and the coast road through the bay slows down with coaches. The town empties again in the evening. If you are staying the night you can simply invert the day — climb the fortress or drive out early, spend the middle of the day away from the water or up the mountain, and come back to a town that has gone quiet. Cruise schedules for the port are published, so it is worth a look when you plan.

Is the fortress climb manageable?

It is a long stone staircase rather than a mountain path, so there is no scrambling and no route finding, but there are well over a thousand steps, little shade and an uneven surface in places. Most reasonably fit people do it in under an hour at a steady pace with stops for the view. If the whole climb is too much, the first stretch to the church of Our Lady of Remedy is roughly half way and already gives you most of the photograph. Avoid the middle of the day in summer, and do not start it in the wet — the stone is slick.

Is Kotor a good base for the rest of Montenegro?

For the bay and the coast, yes. Perast, Herceg Novi, Tivat and Budva are all inside an hour, and Lovćen and Cetinje are a spectacular hour up the hairpins behind the town. For the mountains in the north it is a long day each way, and the Durmitor side deserves a night or two of its own — we would not try to see Žabljak from Kotor and back in a day. Podgorica, the capital and the main transport hub, is about an hour and a half by road.

Do I need a car?

Not to enjoy Kotor, and not if you are staying in the old town and taking boats and buses along the bay. Yes, if you want Lovćen, the villages behind the bay, Lake Skadar or the north, where buses are infrequent and the good roads are the point. If you do drive, book somewhere with parking in writing rather than hoping, and remember the ferry across the narrows: it saves a long, slow loop.
