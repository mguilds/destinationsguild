Where to Stay in Ksamil

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Ksamil is a village at the very bottom of Albania with the best sand and the clearest water on the country’s coast, a handful of small islands just offshore that you can swim or wade out to, and Butrint ten minutes down the road. It was almost nothing thirty years ago and it is now the most intensively visited place on the Riviera, which is both why you would come and the main thing to plan around: in late July and August it is full, and the building has been fast and not always tidy. Come in June or September and it is close to idyllic.

The village beaches and the islands

Best swimmingWalk to the sandBusiest

The centre of Ksamil is its string of small sandy bays facing the islands, and this is where most of the hotels, apartments and beach clubs are. Staying here means the water is a few minutes’ walk away, the restaurants and bars are at hand, and you can be in the sea before the day-trippers arrive — which in high season is the single best reason to sleep in Ksamil rather than visit it. The cost is crowds and noise in July and August, and sun-bed concessions covering a good deal of the sand.

The lanes back from the water

Best valueQuieterFive minutes in

A few streets inland from the bays, Ksamil is still a village of guesthouses, apartments and small family hotels, at prices well below anything with a sea view and often with more space. It is a five- to ten-minute walk down to the beaches, the evenings are quieter, and you are among people who live here rather than only visitors. The trade-off is no view and a short walk back uphill with wet towels.

The Butrint end and the Vivari channel

CalmestFor ButrintNeeds a car or taxi

South of the village the road runs on to the Vivari channel and the Butrint peninsula, past the lagoon and a thinner scatter of places to stay. This end is markedly quieter, the setting is lagoon and woodland rather than beach bars, and Butrint itself is on the doorstep — which means you can be at the ruins when they open and before any coach arrives. You will want a car or will use taxis for the beaches and for dinner.

North along the Saranda road

Larger hotelsPoolsShort drive to the sand

The road between Ksamil and Saranda carries most of the bigger hotels and apartment complexes in the area, with pools, parking and more facilities than the village can offer, generally at better value per room. You are a few minutes by car from the Ksamil beaches and ten or fifteen from Saranda. It suits families and car-based trips; it is a poor choice without a vehicle, and some properties are further from a walkable beach than their photographs suggest.

The southern coves and the lagoon side

Quietest swimmingThin on restaurantsCar essential

Beyond the main village the coast runs south in a series of smaller coves and rocky inlets with far fewer people on them even in August, and the Butrint lagoon lies behind. A small number of guesthouses and rooms sit out this way. This is the choice for anyone who wants the water without the crowd and does not mind driving to dinner — and it is worth checking what is actually open in your month before booking, because much of it is seasonal.

Getting around Ksamil

On foot

The village itself is small and flat enough to walk end to end in fifteen or twenty minutes, and most people staying in the middle never need transport. The lanes are short and the gradients gentle, though surfaces away from the main street can be unmade and unlit, so a torch for the walk back is not a silly idea.

Buses, furgons and taxis

Minibuses run frequently between Ksamil and Saranda through the season, which is the cheapest way in and out, and continue to Butrint. Taxis are plentiful and the fares to Saranda and Butrint are short and inexpensive. Services thin right out in winter, when a good deal of Ksamil closes.

Getting to Butrint

Butrint is about ten minutes south by road, and it is the best thing in the region: a Greek theatre, a Roman forum, a baptistery with mosaics, and a Venetian fort with the view, on a wooded peninsula. Staying in Ksamil lets you get there early, before the coaches come down from Saranda. Opening hours are seasonal, so check on the day.

The islands

The small islands off the village beaches are the postcard, and in calm weather the nearest can be reached by wading and swimming from the sand — many people do. Small boats and pedal craft are hired on the beaches in season. Mind the depth and the boat traffic in the channel between, and do not attempt it in a swell.

Driving and parking

A car makes the whole Riviera accessible and is close to essential if you are staying outside the village. In high season parking near the beaches is genuinely scarce and the village roads are congested, so ask your hotel directly whether it has its own parking rather than assuming.

When to come

Ksamil is entirely a summer place, and the difference between its good weeks and its bad ones is bigger than anywhere else in Albania. June and September are the months to come: warm clear water, everything open, space on the sand and prices well below the peak. Late July and August are at capacity — this is the busiest spot on the Albanian coast, the diaspora is home, rooms are at their dearest and must be booked months ahead, and the beaches are full from mid-morning. Late May and early October are quiet and swimmable for most people. November to April much of the village closes: the weather is mild and wet, there is little open, and Butrint rather than the beach is the reason to be here.

Getting in: what applies, and what does not

Albania is not in the European Union and not in the Schengen area, so it runs its own border. United States citizens may enter as tourists without a visa and may stay up to one year without a residence permit; your passport must be valid for at least three months from arrival and carry a blank page for the stamp. Most other Western nationalities get ninety days visa-free — check your own. The United States rates Albania Level 2, exercise increased caution, in an advisory issued on 26 August 2026 on account of crime; read the current text at travel.state.gov, because the level and wording change.

If your trip also crosses into Greece, Italy or anywhere else in the Schengen area, the following applies to that crossing and not to Albania:

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Ksamil — questions

Which part of Ksamil should I stay in?

Stay in the village by the beaches if swimming early and walking to dinner matter most — in August that is worth real money. Stay in the lanes a few streets back for the same village at a lower price and a quieter night. Stay at the Butrint end or the southern coves for calm and space, with a car. Stay along the Saranda road for a larger hotel with a pool and parking, again with a car.

Is Ksamil too crowded to be worth it?

In late July and August it is genuinely crowded, and visitors who arrive then expecting the empty turquoise bays of the photographs are disappointed. In June and September the same beaches are lovely and comparatively quiet. So the honest answer is that Ksamil is worth it, but the month decides: shift your dates rather than your destination if you can.

Ksamil or Saranda?

Ksamil has far better beaches and water; Saranda has the hotels, the shops, the ferries, the bus connections and the evening life. A common and sensible answer is to stay in Ksamil if the point of the trip is the sea, and in Saranda if you want a base for Butrint, Gjirokaster, the Blue Eye and the coast road. They are fifteen to twenty minutes apart, so the choice is not a big one.

Can I really swim to the islands?

In calm weather, the nearest of them, yes — the channel is short and shallow enough that people wade and swim across, and plenty do. Small boats and pedal craft are also hired on the beaches in season. Two cautions worth taking seriously: depths change and there is boat traffic in the gaps between the islands, and it should not be attempted when there is any swell.

Do I need a car in Ksamil?

Not if you stay in the village: minibuses and taxis to Saranda and Butrint are cheap and frequent in season, and the beaches are a walk away. You do need one, or will be dependent on taxis, if you stay along the Saranda road, at the Butrint end or in the southern coves — and a car is what makes the rest of the Riviera, up through Himare to Vlore, reachable.

What is there to do besides the beach?

Butrint, which is ten minutes away and one of the finest classical sites in this part of Europe; the Vivari channel and the Butrint lagoon, with birds and a Venetian triangular fortress; the Blue Eye spring inland on the Gjirokaster road; and Saranda for an evening. Beyond that, Ksamil is a place to swim and eat, and it does not pretend otherwise.

Is anything open in winter?

Not much. Ksamil is a seasonal village and from about November to April a large part of its accommodation and most of its beach restaurants are shut. The weather is mild and wet rather than cold. If you are in the far south out of season, base yourself in Saranda instead and come down to see Butrint, which is open year-round.
