Bucharest

Where to Stay in Bucharest

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Bucharest is a city that does not try to charm you on the way in, and then does it anyway. It was rebuilt grandly in the late nineteenth century by architects who had trained in Paris, flattened in places by the 1977 earthquake, and then torn into by Ceausescu, who levelled a whole quarter of the old city to build the boulevard and the vast parliament at the end of it. What is left is a city of contradictions on the same street: a Belle Epoque bank next to a concrete block, a seventeenth-century monastery church in the shadow of an office tower, and some of the best eating and drinking in south-eastern Europe in the lanes between. It is flat, green, surprisingly cheap, and the one Romanian city with real international flight connections, so most trips start here.

Where to stay in Bucharest, area by area

The parts of Bucharest a visitor actually chooses between, and who each one suits.

The Old Town: Lipscani and the historic centre

Most centralNightlifeCobbles

The surviving pocket of the pre-war city, a few blocks of cobbled lanes between Calea Victoriei and the river, with the Stavropoleos monastery church, the old National Bank, the Manuc’s Inn courtyard and the remains of the princely court among the bars. It is the densest concentration of restaurants and terraces in the country, and from Thursday to Sunday it is loud until very late. Stay here if you want to walk out of the door into the middle of it, and ask for a room at the back or on a high floor if you intend to sleep.

Calea Victoriei and Revolution Square

Grand boulevardBig hotelsMuseums

The handsome spine of the city, running north from the old town past the Romanian Athenaeum, the former royal palace that now holds the National Art Museum, the Revolution Square balcony where Ceausescu gave his last speech in December 1989, and a run of restored nineteenth-century facades. Most of the larger international hotels are on or just off it. This is the easiest choice for a first visit: level pavement, taxis outside, the main museums at the door and the old town a ten-minute walk south.

Universitate and Piata Romana

MetroCentral valueWalk everywhere

The student and bookshop end of the centre, around the university, the Intercontinental corner and Piata Romana, sitting on the main north-south metro line with everything a visitor wants inside twenty minutes on foot. Rooms here are mostly mid-range hotels and apartments at better prices than Calea Victoriei, and it is quieter at night than the old town while still being central. A sensible compromise if you want to be in the middle without being in the noise.

Herastrau: King Michael I Park, Primaverii and Floreasca

QuietestGreenBest for a longer stay

North of the centre the city opens into its largest park, laid out round a lake and renamed for King Michael I, with the open-air Village Museum on its western shore and streets of embassies and interwar villas around it. This is the pleasant residential Bucharest, good for running and for sitting outside, with a fifteen-minute metro ride or a short taxi into the middle. Expect apartments and smaller design hotels rather than big houses. The best choice for anything longer than three or four nights.

Dorobanti and Piata Victoriei

RestaurantsBusinessLeafy streets

Between the centre and the park, around Piata Victoriei and the streets of Dorobanti, is where a good deal of the city’s better eating and its office life sit together. Quiet tree-lined side streets, a cluster of restaurants that Bucharest itself books tables at, the Antipa natural history museum and the Victoriei metro interchange. It suits a traveller who wants good food and a calm street and does not mind being two stops from the old town.

Getting around Bucharest

The metro

Five lines, clean, cheap and the fastest way across the city, running from early morning until about eleven at night. One line runs the length of the centre from the north down through Universitate, which covers most of what a visitor needs, and another reaches the airport. Buy a card at a station machine or pay contactless at the gate. It is the one form of Bucharest transport that is immune to the traffic.

Buses, trams and trolleybuses

A dense network run by the city transport company, useful for the gaps the metro leaves, with contactless payment on board and a card you can also buy at kiosks. Trams on their own tracks move well; buses sit in the same traffic as everyone else. Check the route on a phone map rather than at the stop, because diversions are common and the printed timetables are optimistic.

Taxis and ride-hailing

Ride-hailing apps are widely used, cheap by Western European standards and the simplest way to get a fair price without a conversation. For a street taxi, use a rank, look for the per-kilometre rate printed on the door, and insist on the meter. Avoid the drivers who approach you inside the airport terminal; use the official rank or an app pickup point.

To and from the airport

Henri Coanda International is at Otopeni, about sixteen kilometres north of the centre, and it handles nearly all of Romania’s long-haul and international traffic. There is a rail link to the city, an express bus, and taxis and apps at the terminal. The road in crosses the city’s worst bottleneck, so allow well over the distance at rush hour and take the train if your timing is tight.

Getting out of town

Trains north to Brasov through the Prahova valley take roughly two and a half to three hours and are the pleasant way into Transylvania; Sinaia and Peles Castle are on the same line. Buses and minibuses leave from several stations depending on direction rather than from one terminus, so confirm which one on the day. The Black Sea at Constanta is about two hours by train, and Cluj and Timisoara are far enough that most Romanians fly.

When to come

Bucharest is a working capital rather than a resort, so it keeps a fairly even calendar, but the shoulder months are plainly the best. May, June and early September to October give warm days, terraces open and the parks at their best. July and August are genuinely hot on the plain, often well into the thirties, and the city thins out as Bucharest itself goes to the mountains or the sea — which makes hotels cheaper if you can take the heat. November to March is cold, grey and sometimes snowy, and the city carries on entirely as normal; December brings Christmas markets in the central squares and is the one time rooms are tight. The dates that fill hotels are 1 December, the national day, and the Orthodox Easter weekend, which moves each year.

Getting in: what applies, and what does not

Romania is in the European Union and, since 1 January 2025, a full member of the Schengen area — air and sea borders opened in March 2024 and the land borders followed on New Year’s Day 2025. Arriving from another Schengen country is now an internal journey with no routine passport check, which is a real change from older guidebooks. Romania is not in the euro area: the currency is the Romanian leu.

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen external border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave. Ninety days in any one hundred and eighty is the visa-free tourist allowance for American, Canadian, British and Australian visitors, and it is counted across the whole Schengen area rather than against Romania alone.

Bucharest — questions

Which part of Bucharest should I stay in?

For a first visit of two or three nights, stay on or just off Calea Victoriei: it is central, level, next to the main museums and a short walk from the old town. If you want to be in the middle of the eating and drinking, stay in the Old Town itself and expect noise until late. For better prices in the centre, Universitate or Piata Romana. For a quiet, green, longer stay, the streets around King Michael I Park. For good restaurants on calm streets, Dorobanti.

How long do I need in Bucharest?

Two nights covers the city properly: the Palace of the Parliament, Revolution Square and Calea Victoriei, the old town churches, and an evening eating in Lipscani. A third night lets you add the Village Museum and the park, or the art museum without rushing. Many travellers use it as the start and end of a Transylvania trip and give it one night at each end, which works perfectly well.

Is Bucharest walkable?

The centre is flat and walkable, and the old town is pedestrianised. The city is larger and more spread out than it looks on a map, though, and the main boulevards are wide and hostile to cross on foot. Pavements are often parked across. Use the metro for anything over about twenty-five minutes and walk the rest; between them they cover everything.

Is Bucharest safe?

For an ordinary visitor it is a comfortable European capital, and the centre is busy and well lit in the evenings. The practical cautions are the usual city ones: pickpocketing on crowded transport and in the old town at night, taxi drivers who will not use the meter, and the stray dogs that are far fewer than they once were but still about on the outskirts. What should I not miss?

The Palace of the Parliament, which has to be seen to be believed and is visited on a booked guided tour with identification; Revolution Square and the balcony; the Romanian Athenaeum, ideally for a concert; the little Stavropoleos monastery church in the old town; and the open-air Village Museum by the lake, which assembles real houses from all over the country and is the best short course in Romania there is.

Do I need a car in Bucharest?

No, and it is the last Romanian city you would want one in. Traffic and parking are both difficult, and the metro and apps between them do everything. If you are hiring a car to drive into Transylvania, collect it on the morning you leave rather than on arrival, and consider taking the train to Brasov and hiring there instead.

Is Bucharest a good base for day trips?

For the south, yes. Sinaia and Peles Castle are about an hour and a half by train, Brasov and Bran two and a half to three hours each way, which is a long but workable day, and Constanta on the Black Sea about two hours. Transylvania proper deserves its own nights rather than a dash out and back, and Maramures and the painted monasteries of Bucovina are much too far for a day.
