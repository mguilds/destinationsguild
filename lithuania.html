Lithuania

The Lithuania Travel Guide

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Lithuania is the largest and southernmost of the three Baltic states, and the one with the longest memory. For three centuries from the thirteen-hundreds the Grand Duchy of Lithuania was one of the biggest states in Europe, reaching from the Baltic towards the Black Sea, and the last corner of the continent to accept Christianity. What a visitor sees of that now is Vilnius: the largest and least-tidied baroque old town in this part of Europe, inland on the Neris, a UNESCO World Heritage site since 1994. Beyond it the country divides simply enough — Kaunas and the Nemunas valley in the middle, a short coast screened by the great sandbar of the Curonian Spit, the pine forests and spa towns of Dzukija in the south, and the lakes and low hills of Aukstaitija and Samogitia in the north. It is flat, more than a third forest, and you can cross the whole of it in an afternoon.

Why visit Lithuania

Vilnius has the old town nobody expects

The historic centre of Vilnius has been a UNESCO World Heritage site since 1994, and it is bigger than the better-known old towns of Riga and Tallinn put together. It is also less tidy than either, in the best way: some forty churches from Gothic through a particularly florid baroque, university courtyards opening off one another, lanes that lead nowhere, peeling stucco next to restored facades. The city marked seven hundred years since its first written mention in 2023 and still takes a fraction of the visitors of either neighbour.

Kaunas built a whole country in twenty years

From 1920 to 1939 Vilnius was held by Poland and Kaunas was Lithuania’s provisional capital. In two decades it put up the ministries, banks, university, schools and apartment houses of a new state, in a calm modernism of its own invention. In 2023 that quarter went onto the UNESCO World Heritage List as Modernist Kaunas: Architecture of Optimism, 1919–1939 — and because it is a working part of the city rather than a museum, you see it by walking down an ordinary street.

The Curonian Spit is unlike anywhere else in Europe

A sandbar ninety-eight kilometres long, in places only a few hundred metres wide, between the open Baltic and a shallow lagoon: pine forest planted to hold the sand down, bare dunes that have buried villages within recorded memory, and four hamlets of wooden fishermen’s cottages painted blue and oxblood. It has been a UNESCO World Heritage site since 2000, shared with Russia, and the Lithuanian half is a national park. Nothing else on the Baltic looks remotely like it.

The twentieth century is close to the surface

Lithuania was independent between the wars, occupied by the Soviet Union, then Germany, then the Soviet Union again, and independent once more from 1990 — the first republic to break away. You see it in the former KGB building in Vilnius, now a museum you walk through cell by cell; in the Ninth Fort outside Kaunas; in the Soviet statues collected in the forest at Grutas; and in the Hill of Crosses near Siauliai, which the authorities bulldozed repeatedly and which local people rebuilt every time.

Singing, and a chain of two million people

Lithuania holds a national Song Celebration that brings tens of thousands of singers and dancers to Vilnius, a tradition whose centenary was marked in the summer of 2024 and which UNESCO lists, with its Latvian and Estonian counterparts, as intangible cultural heritage. It is not folklore laid on for visitors. On 23 August 1989 around two million people joined hands in a line from Tallinn through Riga to Vilnius against Soviet rule, and the independence movement that followed is still called the Singing Revolution.

Forest, lakes and almost nobody in them

More than a third of the country is woodland, there are a couple of thousand lakes in the north-east alone, and the Nemunas delta on the coast is one of the better bird places on this side of Europe. None of it is crowded. The national habit of going into the forest for berries and mushrooms in late summer is universal and unselfconscious, the paths are good, the ground is flat, and you can walk for an afternoon in Dzukija or Zemaitija without meeting anybody at all.

Lithuania at a glance

Capital

Vilnius, inland in the south-east where the small Vilnia joins the Neris, with roughly a fifth of the country’s people in it. The historic centre is a UNESCO World Heritage site and is the largest in the Baltic states. The airport sits a few kilometres south of the middle of town, with a short rail shuttle into the central station.

Money

The euro, which replaced the litas on 1 January 2015 — Lithuania was the last of the three Baltic states to adopt it. Cards and contactless are taken almost everywhere a visitor goes, including on trains and in the market halls. Prices sit at the lower end of the euro area; the Curonian Spit in high summer is the one real exception.

Language

Lithuanian only, as the official language — one of just two living Baltic languages, and notably archaic in its grammar. Russian is understood by many older people and Polish in the south-east, and neither is a neutral choice for a visitor. English is common in Vilnius, Kaunas and tourism generally, and thinner in small towns.

Borders

In the European Union since May 2004, in NATO since the same year and in the Schengen area since December 2007, so arrival from elsewhere in Schengen involves no check at all. The borders with Belarus and with the Russian region of Kaliningrad are external borders of the European Union and are not places to turn up casually; the Latvian and Polish borders you will cross without noticing.

Getting about

Driving is on the right. Trains link Vilnius, Kaunas and Klaipeda and run out to Trakai and the airport; coaches are thorough, cheap and slower, and reach the towns the trains do not. Vilnius has trolleybuses and buses, Kaunas has those and two nineteen-thirties funiculars. A car earns its keep in the forests, the lakes and the delta.

Shape of it

About sixty-five thousand square kilometres, the largest of the three Baltic states, almost all of it low and flat with a highest point under three hundred metres. Only some ninety kilometres of coast, and most of that sheltered behind the Curonian Spit. About two point nine million people, a number that has been falling for a generation. A parliamentary republic with a directly elected president: Gitanas Nauseda has held the office since July 2019 and was re-elected in 2024.

The regions

The capital, the middle, the coast and the north — and you can drive from any one of them to any other inside a day.

Vilnius and the south-east

The capital, the lake-and-castle country immediately west of it, and the pine forests of Dzukija running south to the Polish and Belarusian borders. Most visitors spend most of their trip inside this corner, and with trains to Trakai and coaches to Druskininkai they are right to.

Kaunas and the Nemunas valley

The middle of the country, where the Neris runs into the Nemunas and the Nemunas turns west for the sea. Kaunas is the base and the reason to come; the river valley below it carries castles, a bend-in-the-river spa town and the national open-air museum.

The coast and the Curonian Spit

Ninety kilometres of coast, most of it hidden behind the spit, and the strangest landscape in the Baltic states. Klaipeda is the city and the ferry port; Nida is the reason to go further. Four hours from Vilnius either way, so give the coast its own nights rather than a day return.

Samogitia and the north

Zemaitija — Samogitia — is the western upland, the last part of Europe to be Christianised and still conscious of it, with lakes, forest and a former Soviet missile base in a national park. North of it, outside Siauliai, stands the one place in Lithuania that every visitor has heard of.

Things to do in Lithuania

Walk up Gediminas’ hill and then get lost below it

The hill above Vilnius Cathedral carries the brick tower of the upper castle and the best view of the old town; there is a funicular if the climb does not appeal. Come down and give the afternoon to the streets: Pilies and Didzioji, the university courtyards, St Anne’s, the Gate of Dawn, the lanes of the old Jewish quarter, and Uzupis over the bridge. No ticket, no plan, and the single best thing in the country.

Stand on the Parnidis dune at Nida

A bare sand hill above the strait at the southern end of the Lithuanian spit, with a granite sundial on top, pine forest on one side, the lagoon on the other and the Russian border visible down the sandbar. Boardwalks keep you off the dune itself, which is actively moving. Go early or at the end of the day, when the light is long and the sand is empty.

Take the modernist walk in Kaunas

An hour or two on foot around Laisves aleja and up into Zaliakalnis, looking at what a small country built for itself between 1920 and 1939 when it suddenly had to be a capital: banks, ministries, the post office, clinics, apartment houses, two funiculars, and the white basilica on the hill with a terrace on its roof. The quarter has been a UNESCO World Heritage site since 2023.

Go to the Hill of Crosses

Twelve kilometres north of Siauliai, a low mound carries hundreds of thousands of crosses, from carved oak monuments down to rosaries hung on other crosses. People began leaving them after the rising of 1831; the Soviet authorities bulldozed the hill more than once and it was rebuilt by hand each time. It is quiet, it rattles in the wind, and it takes about an hour including the walk.

Trakai: an island castle and a pastry

Half an hour out of Vilnius by train, a red-brick castle sits on an island in a chain of lakes, reached by a wooden footbridge. The town along the shore is the home of the Karaim, a small Turkic community brought here in the fourteenth century, and their kibinai — a folded pastry of mutton and onion — is the thing to eat before you get the train back.

Walk out to Kernave

Five grass mounds in a meadow above the Neris, with a buried medieval town under them: the capital of pagan Lithuania, abandoned after the Teutonic Order burned it, excavated and left as it is. It has been a UNESCO World Heritage site since 2004. There are no ruins to look at, which is exactly why it is one of the quietest and most affecting places in the country.

When to go, and what it costs

Lithuania has four real seasons and no unusable month, but they are not equal. Late May to early July is the prize: long light, lilac and then linden, the courtyards open and midsummer nights that barely go dark. July and August are the warmest and the only time the Baltic is swimmable, and the only time Nida and Palanga are genuinely full. September and early October are the country at its best looking — the forests turn, the light goes long and gold, and the crowds have gone. November to March is cold, often snowy and dark by mid-afternoon, which sounds like a reason to stay away and is not: Vilnius under snow with the Christmas market open by the Cathedral is a lovely thing, and it is the cheapest time to be here. The dates to know are Jonines, midsummer on 23 and 24 June, when the country leaves town for the countryside, and Statehood Day on 6 July.

Fishing here

Fishing in Lithuania means the Curonian Lagoon and the Nemunas delta for pike, bream and zander, the Neris and the Zeimena for trout and grayling, the lakes of Aukstaitija in the north-east, and the lagoon again in winter, when ice fishing is a serious institution rather than a novelty. An angler’s card is required, the salmon and sea-trout waters carry their own seasons and restrictions, and parts of the delta sit inside a regional park with rules of their own, so it is best arranged locally rather than puzzled out from abroad. Travelling in your later years

Vilnius is the hilliest of the Baltic capitals

Not severely, but more than Riga or Tallinn: the Old Town rises and falls, the two famous viewpoints are real climbs and the cobbles are uneven. There is a funicular up to Gediminas’ tower, which takes the worst of it away. Staying on or just off Gedimino prospektas in the New Town gives you level pavement, the big hotels with lifts, and the Old Town five flat minutes away.

The trains and coaches do the work

Trakai, Kaunas and Klaipeda are all reachable without a car, the trains are level with the platform at the main stations, and the coach network reaches the small towns thoroughly. For anybody who would rather not take a hire car onto unfamiliar roads, this is a country where you genuinely do not have to — the one exception being the forests and the lakes, which do need one.

Druskininkai was built to look after people

The spa town in the southern forest has been in the business of treatment since the early nineteenth century, and its sanatoria still run proper programmes of baths, mud and physiotherapy rather than simply having a pool. The town is flat, the park and river paths are level and shaded, and a week there is a restful rather than a strenuous holiday. Say when you book that you want a programme.

Where the ground is difficult

The Parnidis dune at Nida is a long boardwalk and a flight of wooden steps; Gediminas’ hill is steep unless you take the funicular; the Hill of Crosses is loose gravel and uneven paths between the crosses; the Trakai castle footbridge and courtyards are cobbled and narrow; and the Kaunas funiculars, charming as they are, involve a step up into the car. None of it rules the country out, but it is worth choosing deliberately.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Lithuania itself is straightforward: in the European Union since May 2004, in the Schengen area since December 2007 and in the euro since 2015. Arriving from anywhere else in Schengen there is no check at all, and coaches between Vilnius, Riga and Tallinn cross the internal borders without stopping. Vilnius airport carries most of the long-haul connections, with Kaunas and Palanga serving low-cost routes.

One practical point. Lithuania’s borders with Belarus and with the Russian region of Kaliningrad are external borders of the European Union, and crossing them is not a casual matter for a visitor. Druskininkai and the south-east run close to the Belarusian line and the Curonian Spit ends at the Russian one; in both places, keep to the marked roads and paths and ignore any short cut your map offers.

Lithuania — questions

How many days do I need in Lithuania?

Three days is enough for Vilnius, and Vilnius is what most people come for. Five gets you the capital plus Trakai and Kaunas, both inside an easy half-day of it. A week lets you cross to the coast for Klaipeda and the Curonian Spit without hurrying, which is the part of the country least like anywhere else. Ten days or a fortnight joins Lithuania to its neighbours: Riga is about four hours by coach and Tallinn a long day, and all three Baltic capitals together make one of the better value trips in Europe.

Is Lithuania expensive?

It is among the cheaper countries in the euro area, and noticeably cheaper than the Nordic capitals a short flight away. Vilnius is the dearest part of it in general, but the real exception is Nida and the Curonian Spit in July and August, where the number of beds inside a national park cannot grow and the prices show it. We do not print figures because they move, but the pattern holds: the spit in high summer is a premium, Vilnius Old Town a small one, and everywhere else is modest.

What language should I speak?

Lithuanian is the only official language, and it is one of only two living Baltic languages — the other is Latvian — and unusually old in its grammar, which is why linguists keep writing about it. Russian is understood by many older people and Polish in the south-east, but neither is a neutral convenience and a visitor should not reach for either first. English is widely spoken in Vilnius, in Kaunas and in anything to do with tourism, and thinner in small towns. Labas for hello and dekui for thank you go a long way.

Do I need a car?

Not for Vilnius, Kaunas or Klaipeda, which are compact, and not for Trakai, which is half an hour out of Vilnius by train. Trains link the three big cities and coaches reach almost every town, cheaply and thoroughly if slowly. A car earns its keep for the Dzukija and Zemaitija forests, the Nemunas delta, the Aukstaitija lakes and the Hill of Crosses, and on the Curonian Spit it brings a national-park fee and a vehicle ferry queue with it, so think about whether you want one there. Driving is on the right.

Where exactly is Lithuania, and what is it next to?

Lithuania is the southernmost and largest of the three Baltic states, with Latvia to the north, Belarus to the east and south-east, Poland to the south and the Russian region of Kaliningrad to the south-west. It has only about ninety kilometres of coast, most of it screened from the open sea by the Curonian Spit. It is a little over sixty-five thousand square kilometres, low and wooded, with a highest point under three hundred metres, and about two point nine million people live here.

Is it a good trip for a first visit to Europe?

It is a good trip for a second or third one. Vilnius is beautiful, safe, cheap to be in and far less crowded than the obvious European capitals. What it does not have is familiarity: the language is unlike anything most visitors have met, the history takes some reading, and outside the cities and the coast English thins out. Paired with Latvia and Estonia it becomes an unusually rewarding fortnight, and the coaches between the three capitals make that easy.

What is the weather actually like?

Four real seasons, with a maritime edge on the coast and a continental one inland. Summers are mild and long-lit rather than hot, and midsummer nights barely go properly dark. Autumn is the country at its best looking and its wettest. Winter is cold, grey and genuinely snowy in a normal year, with the light gone by mid-afternoon. Spring arrives late and then all at once.
