The Austria Guide

Austria Travel Guide

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated August 2026

Austria is a little smaller than Maine and holds two quite different holidays inside it. One is imperial and urban — Vienna and Salzburg, palaces and picture galleries, coffee houses and concert halls, all of it flat enough and compact enough to see on foot. The other is alpine — Innsbruck, the Tyrol and the lake country of the Salzkammergut, where the pleasure is a cable car, a lake steamer and an afternoon that asks nothing of you. The happy fact is that you do not have to choose, because Austrian trains are fast, frequent, punctual and comfortable, and they run from the middle of one city to the middle of the next. This guide covers where to go first, why a rental car is usually the wrong instinct, what a room actually costs now that Vienna has raised its bed tax, and where to stay.

It is not Switzerland, and that is good news

Americans routinely file Austria alongside Switzerland and expect the same prices. They are not the same. Austria is roughly level with Germany and well below Switzerland — hotel rooms, restaurant meals, cable cars and rail fares are all noticeably cheaper than across the western border, and the mountains on this side of it are just as high. The currency is the euro, not the franc. If you have been putting Austria off on the assumption that it would cost what Zermatt costs, put it back on the list.

Two countries in one, joined by rail

The imperial half is Vienna, Salzburg and Graz: palaces, galleries, coffee houses and concert halls in flat, compact, walkable centres. The alpine half is Innsbruck, the Tyrol and the Salzkammergut lakes: cable cars, lake steamers and long views. The reason you can have both in one trip without exhausting yourself is the railway, which is fast, frequent and punctual, and lands you in the middle of each town rather than on its edge.

English is fine, German is appreciated

The language is German, and Austrian German has its own warm vocabulary — you will hear Servus and Gruess Gott far more often than Guten Tag. English is widely and comfortably spoken in hotels, restaurants, museums, ticket offices and on the trains, and station and museum signage is generally bilingual. You will not be stuck. But Austria is a more formal country than the Netherlands or Scandinavia, and a greeting on entering a shop and a Danke schoen on leaving are noticed and returned.

Sunday is genuinely closed

This surprises more American visitors than anything else on the list. Austrian shops, including supermarkets, close on Sundays and public holidays, and many close early on Saturday afternoon as well. Restaurants, cafes, museums and transport run as usual, and there are exceptions at the main railway stations and the airport, but if you need a chemist, a corner shop or a bottle of wine, Saturday morning is your last chance. Plan your Sunday as a museum-and-lunch day and you will find it one of the pleasantest days of the trip.

The cobbles matter more than the hills

Vienna, Salzburg and Graz are all flat or nearly flat in the parts you will walk, so the terrain is not the problem — the surface is. Historic centres are laid in cobble and setts that are hard underfoot, uneven at the edges and slippery in rain, and wheeled suitcases hate them. Flat, cushioned, grippy shoes are worth more here than fitness is. The other trap is indoors: many old-town hotels sit in protected buildings with narrow staircases and no lift at all. Never assume a lift; confirm it in writing before you book.

Coffee houses are a place to sit, not a queue

The Viennese coffee house is on the UNESCO list of intangible cultural heritage, and the custom is worth understanding because it is so unlike an American cafe. You are buying the table, not the cup. Nobody will hurry you, a glass of water arrives beside the coffee and is refilled, the newspapers on wooden rods are there to be read, and sitting for two hours over one Melange is the intended use rather than an imposition. Order at the table, wait to be seated, and do not tip on a card — leave the coins.

The trains, and why you probably should not rent a car

Between the cities the train wins on time, money and comfort. In the lake valleys the argument changes.

Austrian Federal Railways — OeBB — run the kind of railway that makes American visitors slightly wistful. Railjet services link Vienna, Linz, Salzburg and Innsbruck several times an hour at the busy end of the day, with proper seats, a dining car, luggage racks that take a real suitcase and a punctuality record that means you can plan a concert around an arrival. Vienna to Salzburg takes about two and a half hours; Salzburg to Innsbruck about one hour and fifty minutes; Vienna to Graz about two and a half.

The map changed on 14 December 2025, when the Koralm railway opened to passengers after twenty-seven years of building. Its thirty-three-kilometre tunnel through the Koralpe cut Graz to Klagenfurt from about three hours to about forty minutes, and took three quarters of an hour off Vienna to Klagenfurt, now around three hours and ten minutes. For a visitor this quietly opens up the south: Carinthia and its warm lakes are now an easy addition to a Graz or Vienna trip rather than a separate expedition.

Book long-distance tickets on the OeBB website or app a few weeks ahead for the cheap Sparschiene fares, and note that seat reservations are a small extra and worth it in summer and December. Within the cities, trams and undergrounds are excellent, cheap and step-free at most stops. Where a car does earn its keep is the Salzkammergut lakes and the smaller alpine valleys, where public transport exists but is slow and thins out in the evening. The sensible compromise for most first trips is trains between the cities and, if you want it, a rental for two or three days in the lake country alone.

What it actually costs in 2026

Mid-priced Europe, with one change this year that affects Vienna specifically.

Austria sits in the comfortable middle of western Europe: dearer than Czechia, Hungary or Poland, clearly cheaper than Switzerland, and about level with Germany. The spread within the country is wide, though, and it is worth planning around. Vienna is the expensive city and Graz is the cheap one; Salzburg is expensive in summer and during the Festival and much softer in November; the alpine resorts are dearest in February and in August.

The 2026 change to know about is Vienna’s accommodation tax, the Ortstaxe. It rose from 3.2 per cent of the room rate to 5 per cent on 1 July 2026, and the legislation passed in December 2025 raises it again to 8 per cent from July 2027. It is charged on the room rate net of VAT and breakfast, and it is usually added at the desk rather than shown in the price you booked, so read your final bill rather than your confirmation. On a week in Vienna it is real money, and it is one more argument for the pattern we suggest throughout this book: fewer nights in Vienna, more nights somewhere calmer.

Two smaller notes that save Americans money. First, restaurant service is included by law, so there is no obligatory tip — rounding up, or five to ten per cent for something genuinely good, is generous and sufficient. Second, tap water is safe and excellent everywhere, and in Vienna it comes off the mountains by aqueduct; ordering it is normal, though in a restaurant you may be charged a small amount for the glass.

Arriving, and the first two hours

Vienna is the main gate; Salzburg and Innsbruck are the useful side doors.

Vienna International Airport (VIE) at Schwechat is the main arrival point and an easy one. The railway station sits under the terminal, and there are two ways into town: the ordinary S-Bahn line S7, which is cheap and takes about twenty-five minutes to Wien Mitte, and the City Airport Train, which is faster and dearer. Railjet services also run from the airport directly to Vienna Hauptbahnhof and onward to Salzburg, Linz and Graz, which means you can land in Vienna and be in another city the same afternoon without ever entering Vienna proper.

Salzburg (SZG) and Innsbruck (INN) are small, pleasant airports with a handful of European connections, and both are minutes from their city centres by bus. Munich is also worth pricing: it is often the cheapest transatlantic fare into this corner of Europe, and there are direct trains from Munich to Salzburg in about an hour and a half and to Innsbruck in about two.

One practical caution for 2026. The European Union’s new biometric entry system is being phased in at external borders, and arrival halls at the bigger airports have been slower than they were. Allow generous time on the way in, and be careful with tight same-day connections onward. Once you are inside, land borders within the Schengen area — to Germany, Italy, Czechia, Slovakia, Hungary and Slovenia — are normally uncontrolled, and the train simply carries on.

When to go, briefly

The long version, month by month, is in its own chapter.

The two windows we would choose are late May into June and September into early October. Both give long daylight, warm days, cool comfortable nights, alpine paths open and clear of snow, lake steamers running and the cities pleasant rather than pressed. July and August are the hottest, dearest and most crowded weeks — the Salzburg Festival fills that city entirely, and Hallstatt is at its most difficult. December is the great exception: the Christmas markets in Vienna, Salzburg and Innsbruck are genuinely among the best in Europe, the cities look their finest, and the trade-off is short dark days and thick weekend crowds. January to March is ski season, superb if that is why you came and quiet to the point of shuttered if it is not.

Music, and how to hear it without being fleeced

Austria’s greatest export is worth planning one evening around — but not the evening the touts are selling.

Buy from the hall, not from the wig

The costumed sellers around Stephansplatz and the Getreidegasse offer tourist concerts at high prices and variable quality. The real institutions sell their own tickets online, months ahead, at prices that are often lower: the Vienna State Opera, the Musikverein, the Konzerthaus, the Volksoper. Book direct from the house’s own website before you fly, and you will pay less for something far better.

The standing places are a bargain

The Vienna State Opera sells a few hundred standing tickets for a handful of euros on the day, which is the cheapest world-class opera on earth. It does mean queuing and then standing, so it suits some travellers and not others. If standing is not for you, the upper circle seats booked well ahead are still modestly priced by American standards.

Salzburg is Mozart’s, and it knows it

His birthplace and residence are both museums, the fortress hosts chamber concerts in a hall that has been used for the purpose for centuries, and the Salzburg Festival in late July and August is one of the world’s great music events — and books out the entire city, so decide early whether you are coming for it or avoiding it.

Austria — common questions

When is the best time to visit Austria?

Late May into June, and September into early October. Both give you long daylight, warm days and cool nights, alpine trails open and clear of snow, and none of the July and August crush in Salzburg and Hallstatt. July and August are the busiest and dearest weeks of the year and the ones when the small lakeside villages are least pleasant. December is a genuine exception worth making: the Christmas markets in Vienna, Salzburg and Innsbruck are among the loveliest in Europe, though the days are short and the queues are long. January to March belongs to the ski resorts, and outside them a good deal of the countryside is shut.

Do I need to rent a car in Austria?

For the cities, no, and a car will cost you more than it saves. Vienna, Salzburg, Innsbruck and Graz are all joined by fast, frequent, comfortable OeBB trains running centre to centre, and all four have historic cores where driving is restricted, parking is expensive and walking is simply better. Where a car does start to earn its keep is the lake country and the smaller alpine valleys, where buses run but run slowly. A sound plan for most first trips is trains between the cities and a rental picked up for two or three days only if you want the Salzkammergut on your own schedule.

Will my American credit card work in Austria?

Yes, more reliably than in Germany, but Austria is still more cash-minded than the United States. Hotels, restaurants, department stores and the railways all take Visa and Mastercard without fuss, and contactless is normal. What still catches Americans out is the small end of the day: village bakeries, market stalls, mountain huts, some taxis, church donation boxes and the smaller cable-car kiosks may want euros in hand. American Express is accepted far less widely than at home. Carry a contactless Visa or Mastercard, load it into your phone as well, keep fifty to a hundred euros in small notes, and when a card machine offers to charge you in dollars, decline and pay in euros.

How many days do I need?

Ten days is the comfortable answer for a first trip, and you can do a good deal in seven. Three or four nights in Vienna covers the Hofburg, Schoenbrunn, the Kunsthistorisches Museum and an evening of music without rushing. Two or three in Salzburg gives you the old town, the fortress and a day in the lake country. If you want the Alps properly, add two or three in Innsbruck. Because the trains are quick and go city centre to city centre, you can do all of that from two bases and unpack twice rather than five times, which matters more as the years go on.

Is Austria expensive?

It is mid-priced by western European standards: dearer than Czechia or Poland, noticeably cheaper than Switzerland, and roughly level with Germany. Vienna is the costly city and Graz the cheap one, and the gap between them is large. A useful piece of 2026 planning: Vienna's accommodation tax rose from 3.2 per cent of the room rate to 5 per cent on 1 July 2026, and is legislated to rise again to 8 per cent in July 2027, so a Vienna room now carries meaningfully more tax than a Salzburg or Graz one.

Is Austria safe for older travellers?

Austria is among the safer countries in Europe and violent crime against visitors is rare. The honest risks are physical rather than criminal. Historic centres are laid in cobble and setts, which are hard underfoot and slippery in rain. Many old-town hotels occupy protected buildings with narrow stairs and no lift, so confirm a lift in writing before you book rather than assuming one. In the mountains, weather changes fast and cable cars close in wind. And pickpocketing does happen where crowds concentrate: Stephansplatz, the Naschmarkt, Salzburg's Getreidegasse in high season and the Christmas markets in December.
