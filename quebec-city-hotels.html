Quebec City

Where to Stay in Quebec City

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Quebec City is the oldest walled town in North America and the only one left, a small French-speaking capital built on a cliff above the St Lawrence, and it is the most European-feeling place on the continent. It is also genuinely small: you can walk across the old town in twenty minutes. The one thing to get right before you book is the vertical dimension — the town is split into an Upper and a Lower Town with a cliff between them, and that matters more here than any question of district.

Upper Town, inside the walls — the one you came for

Most atmosphericInside the gatesSteep and dear

Vieux-Québec Haute-Ville, on top of the cliff and inside the seventeenth-century fortifications, is the heart of it: the Dufferin Terrace boardwalk along the clifftop with its enormous view of the river, the Plains of Abraham, the citadel, the seminary, and a lattice of narrow stone streets. Staying inside the gates means stepping out into the old town at seven in the morning before the coaches arrive, which is the best hour of the day here. The rooms are mostly small inns and converted houses and they are the dearest in the city, the streets are cobbled and steep, and from June to October it is busy in the middle of the day.

Petit Champlain and the Lower Town — the prettiest streets

Oldest streetsFunicularFlat along the quay

Below the cliff, reached by the funicular, a stairway or the long way round, the Lower Town holds the oldest commercial streets in North America: Rue du Petit-Champlain, Place Royale and the Old Port, with the Musée de la Civilisation and the big market hall along the water. It is the most photographed part of the city and, along the quay at least, the flattest. The hotels are small and characterful. The catch is that you are below the main sights and will use the funicular or the stairs several times a day, and the cruise terminal is here, which means crowds in the middle of a summer day.

Saint-Roch and Saint-Jean-Baptiste — where the city lives

Best valueLocal restaurantsUphill walk

Immediately west and below the walls, these two districts are modern working Quebec City: Saint-Jean-Baptiste running along the ridge outside the gates with its shops and bistros, and Saint-Roch below it, the city’s regenerated quarter, with the best concentration of restaurants and bars that are not aimed at visitors. Rooms cost appreciably less than inside the walls. It is a ten to twenty-minute walk to the old town, and the honest caveat is that the walk is uphill in one direction and up a long stairway from Saint-Roch.

Sainte-Foy and the west — for a car and a budget

CheapestParkingBus or taxi in

Out towards the bridges and the university, Sainte-Foy is suburban Quebec City: large modern hotels, shopping centres, easy parking and prices well below the old town. It makes sense in exactly two situations — you are driving and want to leave the car somewhere, or you are travelling on a tight budget in high summer when the old town is full. Otherwise do not stay here. It is fifteen to twenty-five minutes into the old town by bus or taxi, and there is nothing to walk to in the evening.

Getting around

The old town is walkable and hilly, and that is the whole transport story. Nothing inside the walls is more than twenty minutes on foot from anything else, the streets are cobbled, and the gradient between the Upper and Lower Towns is real: the funicular runs from Dufferin Terrace down to Petit Champlain for a couple of dollars and saves a long stairway, and there is a free lift in the Lower Town near the market as well.

For anything beyond the old town the RTC city buses are frequent and inexpensive, and they are how you reach Sainte-Foy, the Montmorency Falls to the east or the bridges. The airport is small and about twenty minutes out by taxi, with limited flights; most visitors arrive instead on the train from Montreal, which takes a little over three hours and comes into the handsome old station in the Lower Town, or by coach, which is slightly quicker.

In winter, the one piece of equipment that matters is boots with grip, or a set of strap-on ice cleats, which are sold in every pharmacy here. The cobbles and the slopes freeze, the city sands and clears but cannot keep up, and a fall on Rue Saint-Jean is the commonest way for a visitor to spoil a trip.

When to come

June to October is the main season: warm, long-dayed, every terrace out, the summer festival in July and the old town busy with coach parties and cruise passengers in the middle of the day. The trick in high summer is simply timing — be out early and late, and spend the middle of the day in a museum or across the river.

Late September and early October are the best weeks of the year here: the colour comes down the north shore and into the Charlevoix, the light is low and gold, and the coaches thin out. February is the other high point, and quite different: the Winter Carnival fills the Plains of Abraham, the old town under snow is as close to a European Christmas card as the continent gets, and it is bitterly cold. November and April are the quiet, cheap, unlovely months.

Two short trips worth the time: Montmorency Falls, higher than Niagara and fifteen minutes east, and the Île d’Orléans just beyond it, a farming island of cider, strawberries and old stone houses. Our month-by-month guide to the best time to visit Canada goes through the year properly.

Getting in

Most visitors from visa-exempt countries arriving in Canada by air need an Electronic Travel Authorization, an eTA, applied for online before they fly and tied to the passport they travel on. Citizens of the United States do not need one but do need proof of citizenship. Nationals of visa-required countries need a visitor visa instead. An eTA is not required if you arrive by land or by sea. We do not print the fees here because they are revised from time to time — read them off the official pages at canada.ca when you book, and apply there rather than through an agent charging a mark-up for the same form.

Two Canada-specific points worth checking well before you travel: past criminal convictions, including an old drink-driving conviction, can make a traveller inadmissible, and there is a formal process for it that is not quick; and taking cannabis across the border in either direction is a federal offence whatever is legal at home. Quebec City hotels FAQ

Do I need French in Quebec City?

Not in the old town, where everyone who deals with visitors works in both languages. The city operates in French, the signs and menus are French and you will hear little English on the street, so a bonjour and a merci are worth having and are warmly received. A few streets out into Saint-Roch or on a bus, English gets thinner, and translation apps earn their keep.

Upper Town or Lower Town?

Upper Town, inside the walls, if you want to be in the middle of the old city and can manage the cobbles and the slopes; it is the more atmospheric half and the dearer one. Lower Town, around Petit Champlain, if you want the prettiest streets and a flatter walk along the quay, and do not mind using the funicular to get up to the main sights. Both are small, and the funicular joins them in a minute.

Is it difficult to get around on foot?

The old town is compact but it is built on and under a cliff, and the cobbles, stairways and gradients are serious if walking is hard for you. The funicular and the Lower Town lift help, the quayside and the Dufferin Terrace are level, and taxis will take you between the two halves by road. In winter, ice on the slopes is the real hazard — boots with grip or strap-on cleats, which the pharmacies sell.

How many days does Quebec City need?

Two nights for the old town itself, three if you want to add the Plains of Abraham, a museum and an unhurried dinner, and four if you want Montmorency Falls and the Île d'Orléans as well. It pairs naturally with Montreal, three hours away by train, and the two together make a comfortable week.

How do I get here from Montreal?

By train, in a little over three hours, into the old station in the Lower Town; by coach, which is slightly quicker and cheaper; or by road in about two and a half hours. The train is the pleasant way, running along the river for much of it. Flights exist but make no sense for that distance once you add the airports.
