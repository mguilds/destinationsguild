Estonia, month by month

Best Time to Visit Estonia

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Estonia has a short, bright summer and a long, dark winter, and the gap between them is wider than almost anywhere else a short flight from western Europe. At midsummer the sky never properly darkens; at the turn of the year there are only six or seven hours of daylight. Almost everything about a trip here — what is open, what it costs, whether the sea is any use to you, whether the island ferry is running — follows from which half of that year you arrive in. This page sets out what each month is actually like, which two the Guild would pick, and the handful of dates worth planning around.

The short answer

If you want light, come in June. Around midsummer the sun is up for roughly eighteen hours and the sky between sunset and sunrise never goes beyond a deep blue twilight, which is a genuinely strange and lovely thing to experience and the single best reason to choose Estonia over somewhere further south.

If you want the best all-round trip, come in late May or September. Mild, green, long evenings, everything open, the cities uncrowded and the prices below the summer peak. These are the two months the Guild would pick.

If you want to swim, you need July or August and you should go to Pärnu, where the bay is shallow enough to warm properly. If you want Christmas markets and snow, come in December. The two stretches we would steer a first-time visitor away from are the March thaw and November, which are grey, muddy and short on daylight without yet being festive.

Estonia month by month

January and February

ColdestDarkestCheapest

The heart of the Estonian winter: daylight runs to roughly six or seven hours, the sea freezes along the shore and in the bays, and temperatures sit below freezing for weeks at a time, sometimes well below. It is a dry, still cold rather than a raw wet one, and the country is thoroughly organised for it — everything indoors is heated to the point of being too warm, and the saunas and spa hotels are at their busiest. Tallinn under snow is beautiful and almost empty of visitors, prices are the lowest of the year, and the Old Town cobbles are an ice rink, which is the one thing to take seriously. Island ferries run less often and can be disrupted; in a hard winter official ice roads are sometimes opened across the straits at short notice.

March and April

ThawMudSoomaa floods

The thaw is not Estonia’s loveliest season and nobody local pretends otherwise. Snow goes patchy and grey, the ground turns to mud, and the light comes back fast — by the end of April the evenings are long again. The compensation is particular and worth planning around: the bogs and floodplain forests at Soomaa flood each spring in what Estonians call the fifth season, and for a few weeks you can take a canoe through woodland that is normally dry ground. The timing varies with the winter and cannot be booked far ahead; watch the forecasts and ask locally.

May

Guild’s pickGreen and lightStill quiet

May is when the country comes back to life and it is one of the two months the Guild would choose. The birches come into leaf, the evenings stretch out past nine and then past ten, the migrating birds come through the western coast and the islands in numbers that bring birdwatchers from all over Europe, and the summer businesses start opening. It is not reliably warm — you will want a coat — but it is dry more often than not, the cities are uncrowded, and prices have not yet moved to summer rates.

June

White nightsMidsummerEverything opens

June is the best month for light in the whole year. Around midsummer the sun is up for something like eighteen hours and the sky never goes properly dark, which changes the feel of the evenings completely. The one thing to plan around is Midsummer itself: Victory Day on 23 June and St John’s Day on 24 June are both public holidays, the whole country goes to the countryside for bonfires, and city restaurants, shops and offices close or keep short hours for a day or two. It is a wonderful thing to be invited to and an awkward couple of days to be a tourist in a city without knowing it was coming.

July and August

WarmestBusiestDearest

The high season, short and intense. This is when the Baltic is warm enough to swim in — properly so at Pärnu, where the bay is shallow, and bracingly so everywhere else — when the islands fill with Estonian and Finnish holidaymakers, and when the festival calendar is at its thickest. It is also when rooms on the coast and on Saaremaa are at their most expensive and need booking well ahead. Tallinn is busy with cruise passengers through the middle of the day. The weather is mild rather than hot by southern European standards, and a week of rain is entirely possible; bring a layer.

September

Guild’s pickMushrooms and berriesEmptying out

The other month we would choose. The crowds go home with the school year, the light is still good into the evening, the forests fill with mushrooms and late berries, and Estonia has a broad public right of access that makes picking them an ordinary weekend activity rather than a trespass. Tartu comes back to life as the students return. The sea holds its summer warmth for the first half of the month and then gives it up. Prices fall away from the August peak almost immediately.

October and November

Autumn colour then greyVery quietCheap

October is the end of the good light: the birches and aspens turn, the bogs go copper, and the walking is excellent in clear weather. By November the colour has gone, the days are short and often grey, and this is the flattest stretch of the Estonian year — the one month we would not particularly recommend to a first-time visitor. Coastal resort businesses shut for the season. The compensation is price and emptiness, and the fact that the spa hotels are open, warm and considerably cheaper than they will be in a month.

December

Christmas marketsDark and festiveBusy in Tallinn

The dark turns into an attraction rather than a problem. From late November the Christmas market fills Tallinn’s Town Hall Square with a tree, wooden stalls and hot spiced drink, and the Old Town in the cold and the lamplight is as good as this city ever looks. Days are at their shortest, which means planning outdoor sightseeing for the middle of them. Tallinn is busier and dearer in these weeks than at any other point in the winter; the rest of the country is not.

Light, dark and the sea

The light swings further than you expect

Tallinn sits at about the same latitude as the southern tip of Greenland and well north of Moscow. At midwinter there are roughly six or seven hours between sunrise and sunset, with the sun low all day; at midsummer there are around eighteen, and a long twilight at both ends that stops the night ever arriving properly. Plan outdoor sightseeing for the middle of a winter day, and expect to lose track of the time entirely in June.

The sea is cold, except where it is not

The Baltic here is shallow, brackish and slow to warm. Pärnu Bay is the exception that built a resort town: it is shallow enough and sheltered enough to reach a comfortable temperature through July and August, which is why Estonian families go there. Everywhere else, including the island beaches, the water stays bracing even in high summer. The sea freezes along the shore in most winters.

Snow is normal but no longer reliable

A usual winter brings lying snow for a good part of the period from around the turn of the year into March, more of it inland and in the east than on the coast. It has become markedly less dependable than it was, and a mild winter can leave Tallinn grey and wet rather than white. If snow is the reason you are coming, treat it as a hope rather than a booking, and check conditions close to the time.

Forests and bogs have their own calendar

Estonia is more than half forest, and the countryside has a timetable of its own: the spring flood at Soomaa, migrating birds along the west coast and the islands in spring and again in autumn, berries from midsummer onwards and mushrooms through late summer and September. Public access to forest and bog is broad here, and foraging is an ordinary part of the year rather than a special permission.

Dates worth planning around

Midsummer: 23 and 24 June

Victory Day and St John’s Day fall on consecutive days and are both public holidays. The towns empty, families go to the countryside, bonfires are lit and the night never gets dark. If you can be somewhere rural, be there. If you are in a city, expect shops, restaurants and transport to keep holiday hours for a day or two.

Independence Day, 24 February, and Restoration of Independence, 20 August

Two national days at opposite ends of the year, marking the declaration of 1918 and the restoration of 1991. Both are public holidays with parades, concerts and ceremonies, and both are worth knowing about if your dates fall near them, because offices and some businesses close.

The Song and Dance Celebration

Held in Tallinn every five years, with a youth celebration in between, and recognised by UNESCO as part of the Baltic song and dance tradition. Tens of thousands of singers perform together on the Song Festival Grounds and the city fills completely. Because the cycle is long, check the year rather than assuming; if your trip coincides, book accommodation far earlier than you otherwise would.

The Christmas market, from late November

Tallinn’s Town Hall Square takes a tree and a ring of wooden stalls for about six weeks, and the Old Town in the dark is at its best. It is the one period when winter Tallinn is busy and prices rise. Dates shift a little from year to year, so confirm them before booking a trip built around it.

What it costs, and when

Estonia is not an expensive country by western European standards, and the swing between its seasons is large. July and August on the coast and on Saaremaa are the peak, and rooms in Pärnu and Kuressaare need booking well ahead; Tallinn’s peak is less pronounced in summer but real in the Christmas weeks. Late autumn and the deep winter outside the festive period are the cheapest times to come, and the spa hotels — which are a serious part of the Estonian hospitality trade rather than an add-on — are open and good value then. Prices move year to year, so we do not print figures here; what holds is the shape of the year.

Fishing here

Estonian fishing runs from pike and perch in the coastal shallows and the great lakes to sea trout in the short rivers of the north, and ice fishing is a normal winter pastime on Lake Peipus and the frozen bays. Licences are required for most of it and are arranged through the Estonian authorities; the rules differ between the sea, the lakes and the rivers, so settle it locally rather than from abroad. Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Estonia itself is straightforward: in the European Union since 2004, in the Schengen area since the end of 2007 and on the euro since the beginning of 2011. An arrival from another Schengen country has no passport control at all, and an arrival from outside it is a single check at the airport you land in. The land borders with Latvia and the sea crossing from Finland are internal Schengen movements. The eastern border with Russia is an external EU frontier and the arrangements at its crossings have changed more than once in recent years, so if any part of your plan depends on one, check with the Estonian Police and Border Guard Board before you book rather than trusting anything written earlier, including this page.

One point of confusion worth clearing up: Estonian e-Residency is a digital identity for running a business online. It is not residency, it is not a visa and it gives you no right to enter or stay. If you are coming as a visitor, the Schengen rules above are the ones that apply to you.

Estonia’s seasons — questions

When is the single best time to visit Estonia?

For most visitors, late May to mid-June, or September. You get long light evenings, mild weather, everything open and noticeably fewer people than in the July and August peak. If swimming matters, you need July or August, because the Baltic simply is not warm before then. If the Christmas markets are the draw, December. The months we would steer a first-time visitor away from are the March thaw and November, which are the two flattest stretches of the Estonian year.

How cold does it actually get?

Cold enough to be a factor rather than an adventure. Midwinter temperatures sit below freezing for long stretches and can drop a good way below it, especially inland and in the east, and daylight at the turn of the year is down to around six or seven hours. It is a dry cold and the country is built for it, so with proper boots, a hat and gloves most visitors are comfortable. The real hazard is ice underfoot on Tallinn’s cobbles rather than the temperature itself.

Is the Baltic warm enough to swim in?

In July and August, yes, with a caveat. Pärnu Bay is shallow and sheltered and warms up far more than the open sea, which is why it is the country’s summer resort; elsewhere the water stays bracing even at the height of summer. Estonians swim regardless and start earlier in the year than most visitors would. Outside those two months, treat the sea as scenery and the saunas as the plan.

What is Midsummer, and should I avoid it?

Victory Day on 23 June and St John’s Day on 24 June are consecutive public holidays and together they are the biggest event in the Estonian year. Towns empty, families go to the countryside, bonfires are lit and the night barely gets dark. If you have Estonian friends or are staying somewhere rural, it is the most memorable thing you could possibly be there for. If you are in a city with a sightseeing list, expect closed shops, altered restaurant hours and reduced transport for a day or two, and plan accordingly rather than being surprised.

When is the Song and Dance Celebration?

The great Song and Dance Celebration is held in Tallinn every five years, with a youth celebration in the years between, and it is one of the defining events of Estonian national life — tens of thousands of singers on a single stage, and a tradition recognised by UNESCO. Because the cycle is long, the dates are worth checking against the year you are travelling rather than assumed; if your trip happens to coincide, book accommodation very early indeed, because the city fills.

Do the islands close in winter?

They do not close, but they thin out. Ferry sailings to Saaremaa and Hiiumaa are less frequent from late autumn, several island restaurants and guesthouses shut for the season, and weather or ice can disrupt a crossing for a day. The spa hotels in Kuressaare stay open and busy throughout. If you are going in winter, leave slack around your ferry times, check what is open before you book, and do not plan a tight connection off the island.
