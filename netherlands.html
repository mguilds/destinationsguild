The Netherlands Guide

Netherlands Travel Guide

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated August 2026

The Netherlands is about the size of Maryland, almost entirely flat, and threaded with one of the densest railway networks on earth — which means that the five cities in this chapter sit within two and a half hours of one another, most of them within one, and you can see a great deal of the country from a single hotel room. It is also a place where two things changed in 2026 that will affect what your trip costs and how your first day goes: hotel tax went up sharply, and the new biometric border checks have made arriving at Schiphol genuinely slow. This guide covers where to go first, why you should not rent a car, what a room actually costs now, the payment trap that catches American visitors, and where to stay in Amsterdam, Rotterdam, The Hague, Utrecht and Maastricht.

It is smaller than you think

This is the single most useful fact for planning. The whole country fits comfortably inside Maryland, and the five cities in this chapter sit within two and a half hours of one another by train — most of them within one. You do not need to change hotels every second night. Pick one or two bases, unpack properly, and make everything else a day trip. Older travellers in particular find this the most restful way to see the Netherlands, and it is not a compromise: you genuinely lose nothing.

English will not be a problem

Dutch is the language, but the Netherlands has among the highest rates of English fluency in the world, and it is not the polite phrasebook kind. Hotel desks, restaurants, museums, ticket machines, railway announcements and taxi drivers all operate comfortably in English, and station and museum signage is routinely bilingual. You will not need a word of Dutch, though dank u wel — thank you — is always received warmly.

The card question, plainly

Dutch shops were built around contactless debit cards, and older advice that your American credit card will simply be refused is now half true rather than wholly true. Albert Heijn, the big supermarket chain, has taken Visa and Mastercard credit cards since March 2024, as do Jumbo and Dirk. But “PIN only” signs persist at bakeries, market stalls, parking machines and small cafes, and American Express is close to useless outside the airport and the chain hotels. Bring a contactless Visa or Mastercard debit card as your main card.

Tipping is light

Service is included in the price by law, so there is no obligatory tip in the Netherlands. Rounding a restaurant bill up, or leaving five to ten per cent for something genuinely good, is generous and entirely sufficient. Nobody will chase you down the street. American visitors habitually over-tip here; you do not need to, and the money is better spent on the museums.

Watch the bicycles, not the traffic

The most common way a visitor gets hurt in the Netherlands is by stepping into a bike lane. They are paved in red, they look like pavement, and they are not. Cyclists travel fast, ride silently, have the legal right of way and will not slow down for you. Before you step off any kerb, look right and left specifically for bikes and scooters — and never stop to take a photograph while standing in a red-surfaced lane.

The cobbles are the real terrain

The Netherlands is famously flat, and that is true — you will barely climb anything outside Maastricht. But the historic centres are laid in brick and cobble, which are hard underfoot, unkind to wheeled suitcases and genuinely slippery in rain. Flat, cushioned, grippy shoes matter more here than fitness does. The other trap is indoors: canal houses have famously steep, narrow staircases, and many small hotels in them have no lift at all. Never assume a lift — confirm it in writing before you book.

Where to stay — the five places to consider

Each has its own chapter, with hotels picked by style, an honest word on which of them actually have a lift, and a plain note on how far you will be walking.

Where to Stay in Amsterdam ›

The canal ring, the Rijksmuseum and the Van Gogh, and the flattest forty-minute walk in Europe. Also the most expensive and most crowded bed in the country, and the place where the steep-staircase problem is worst. Worth two or three nights rather than a week.

Where to Stay in Rotterdam ›

The easiest large Dutch city to walk in, for an unhappy reason — the medieval centre was bombed flat in 1940 and what replaced it is wide, level and modern. Bold architecture, a real harbour, and Delft and Gouda ten and twenty minutes away by train.

Where to Stay in The Hague ›

Vermeer’s Girl with a Pearl Earring at the Mauritshuis, the Peace Palace, and a proper North Sea beach at Scheveningen fifteen minutes away on the tram. Flat, dignified and much calmer than Amsterdam.

Where to Stay in Utrecht ›

Our pick for a first-trip base. A beautiful two-level canal city with the Dom Tower restored and open again, half the crowds of Amsterdam, and a direct train to Amsterdam Centraal in about twenty-seven minutes, running some hundred and fifteen times a day.

Where to Stay in Maastricht ›

The oldest city in the country and the least Dutch — Roman, Burgundian, and pressed up against Belgium and Germany. Two great squares, a bookshop in a Gothic church, and Liège and Aachen as easy day trips. Two and a half hours from Amsterdam on one direct train.

Three more that reward a day

None has its own chapter yet. All three are short train rides from the cities above.

Haarlem — Amsterdam without the crowds

Fifteen minutes west of Amsterdam by train and a different temperature entirely: a handsome market square under a vast Gothic church, the Frans Hals Museum, canals, almshouse courtyards and shops that serve residents rather than visitors. It is also the closest town of any charm to the tulip fields. A great many people who have done Amsterdam once choose to sleep in Haarlem the second time, and are glad they did.

Delft — the prettiest short trip in the country

Direct trains from Rotterdam take ten to twelve minutes and run constantly, which means you need no plan at all. What you get is the old Holland that Rotterdam lost: a canal-laced centre, a great market square between two churches, Vermeer’s town, and the blue-and-white pottery. Do mind the cobbles and the step-up thresholds — this is where the walking gets harder rather than easier.

Leiden and Gouda

Leiden is a canal city with the oldest university in the country and, for many visitors, more genuine atmosphere per square yard than Amsterdam. Gouda, eighteen to twenty-one minutes from Rotterdam, has a spectacular Gothic town hall, the stained glass of the Sint-Janskerk, and a summer cheese market on Thursday mornings. Either makes an easy half day.

The trains — and why you should not rent a car

The country is small, the network is dense, and the stations are in the middle of town rather than twenty miles outside it.

The distances are tiny

Amsterdam to Utrecht is about twenty-seven minutes, with roughly a hundred and fifteen trains a day. Amsterdam to Rotterdam is about forty minutes on the fast Intercity Direct, and Amsterdam to The Hague around fifty. Even Maastricht, right down in the far south against the Belgian border, is a single direct train of about two and a half hours — two hours twenty-three at its fastest — with no changes at all. Schiphol has a railway station directly beneath the terminal. Check ns.nl for the exact times on the day you travel.

You can now simply tap your card

This changed recently and it makes an enormous difference. You no longer need to buy a Dutch travel card: on NS trains, and on trams, buses and the metro, you hold a contactless Visa, Mastercard, Maestro or V Pay card — or your phone — against the reader on the way in and again on the way out, and the fares are totalled and charged as a single amount the following day. One lump sum on your statement is normal, not an error.

Four ways people get this wrong

First, forgetting to tap out, which charges you a maximum fare. Second, mixing card and phone — tap in with the plastic and out with the same card in Apple Pay and the system sees two open journeys and bills two maximum fares, so use the same physical object throughout. Third, each traveller needs a separate card; you cannot tap one card twice for a couple. Fourth, American Express is not accepted on the readers at all.

What tapping does not get you

Tapping a bank card puts you in second class at the full fare, with no railcard discount. Second class on Dutch trains is perfectly comfortable, and on the short hops in this chapter it is what almost everyone uses. If you want first class, or you are making several long journeys and want a discount, buy tickets on the NS site or app instead. In Amsterdam, city trams and buses cap your spending at ten euros a day on the same card.

Why a car is worse, not better

City parking is punishing and low-emission zones restrict where you may drive at all. More to the point, Dutch city driving means yielding constantly to cyclists who have the right of way and exercise it confidently, at junctions laid out on assumptions no American driver shares. A rental car will cost more, take longer and raise your blood pressure. The one honest case for one is a rural base in Friesland or Zeeland.

Bear the stations in mind

Dutch stations are well kept and lifts are standard, but the big ones — Amsterdam Centraal, Utrecht Centraal, Rotterdam Centraal — are genuinely large, and Utrecht Centraal is joined to a shopping centre you walk through to reach the street. Allow a few extra minutes to find the right lift with luggage, and if you are changing trains, know that Dutch connections are usually generous rather than tight.

The two 2026 changes, stated plainly

On 1 January 2026 the reduced 9 per cent VAT rate on Dutch hotel rooms ended and the standard 21 per cent rate took its place. Amsterdam separately charges a tourist tax of 12.5 per cent of the room rate, among the very highest in Europe. Together that is a tax load of roughly a third on top of an Amsterdam room. Museums, concerts and other culture were spared and kept the 9 per cent rate, so ticket prices did not move.

The tourist tax is usually not in the price

This catches people out at the desk. The 12.5 per cent is normally added at check-in or checkout rather than shown in the rate you booked, so a room advertised at two hundred euros a night is not a two-hundred-euro night. Budget for it deliberately. Amsterdam has also said it intends to raise the rate further from 2027, stepping up toward 20 per cent by the end of the decade, so this gap will widen rather than close.

Where the money actually goes

Accommodation is far and away the biggest line, and Amsterdam is the reason. Sleeping in Utrecht, Haarlem, Delft or Rotterdam and taking the train in costs materially less for a better night’s sleep. Museums are the second line, but they are honestly priced at twenty-five euros for the two great Amsterdam collections, and anyone under eighteen goes free. Food is reasonable: an ordinary good restaurant meal is about twenty euros a head.

Four ways to soften it

Sleep outside Amsterdam. Buy museum tickets online in advance, which is compulsory at the big ones anyway and avoids both the queue and the mark-up. Eat your main meal at lunch, when the same kitchens charge less. And use the trains rather than taxis — a tapped bank card on a twenty-seven-minute run to Amsterdam costs a small fraction of the cab fare, and gets you there faster.

Arriving at Schiphol — allow far more time than you think

This is the most time-sensitive thing on the page, and the one we would most want an older traveller to read before booking.

The new border system is causing real queues

The European Entry/Exit System became fully operational on 10 April 2026. It replaces the passport stamp with a digital record, and on your first entry you give four fingerprints and a facial photograph at the border; later trips within three years are a quicker check. It is working, but it is slow. In mid-August 2026 Schiphol itself said non-EU passengers were waiting one to two hours at arrivals border control, and called that unacceptable.

What that means for your first day

Do not book anything tight on your arrival day. No timed museum entry that afternoon, no train you must catch, no tour, no restaurant reservation you would hate to lose. Assume you may be standing in a queue for the better part of two hours after a long flight, and plan a first day that consists of getting to the hotel and having a quiet dinner nearby. Departures are much less affected, so this is an arrival problem rather than a both-ways one.

ETIAS is not in force — do not pay anyone for it

The EU’s ETIAS travel authorisation is not required today. Its target of late 2026 was dropped and no new date has been set, with 2027 the general expectation; the EU has promised to announce the start several months in advance. Nothing is legitimately on sale yet, so treat any website offering to sell you an ETIAS as a scam. When it does begin it will cost twenty euros, last three years, and be free for travellers over seventy.

The good news about the journey in

Once you are through, the rest is easy. Schiphol has a railway station directly under the terminal — you take a lift or an escalator down to the platforms rather than finding a bus — and trains run into Amsterdam Centraal in roughly a quarter of an hour, with direct services to Rotterdam, The Hague, Utrecht and beyond. You can tap a contactless card at the gates. There is no need to arrange a transfer in advance.

When to go

The short answer is late May into mid-June, or September into early October.

The two good windows

Late May to mid-June gives you the longest days of the year — light until nearly eleven at night — gardens and terraces in full use, mild weather and crowds that have not yet peaked. September into early October is the other one: softer light, warm enough, the summer crush gone, the students back so the cities feel alive rather than emptied, and hotel rates noticeably below the summer level. If you are choosing purely on comfort, mid-September is arguably the best fortnight of the Dutch year.

Tulip season is glorious and very crowded

The bulb fields and Keukenhof run roughly from the third week of March to the middle of May, with the peak display usually around mid-April — but the dates shift with the season and with the garden’s own calendar, so confirm them on keukenhof.nl before you book flights. Understand what you are buying: this is simultaneously the most beautiful and the most crowded six weeks in the Netherlands, with the highest hotel prices of the year and coaches arriving in convoy.

King’s Day, 27 April

The national holiday, moved to the 26th when the 27th falls on a Sunday. The entire country dresses in orange, a free nationwide flea market takes over the pavements, and Amsterdam becomes a dense, cheerful, heavily drinking canal-side street party. It is a genuine spectacle and it is also physically hard going, with crowds that do not thin until late. Commit to it deliberately or avoid it entirely; there is no comfortable middle.

Amsterdam is trying to have fewer visitors

This is worth knowing because it shapes what you will find. The city has frozen its hotel bed count, so no new hotels are being built and rates are not softening. From 1 April 2026 homeowners in the centre and De Pijp may let to visitors only fifteen nights a year, which has cut short-term rentals sharply. The tourist tax is explicitly designed as a deterrent. Basing outside Amsterdam is now both cheaper and, more or less, what the city is asking visitors to do.

Windmills, and the picture you came for

There are two serious windmill sites and one very pretty village that has been loved half to death.

Kinderdijk — the real thing

Nineteen authentic eighteenth-century mills standing in a working drainage landscape near Rotterdam, and a UNESCO World Heritage Site. This is the more authentic of the two and by some distance the more moving: flat, open, windy, with long level paths and no shade, which makes for good easy walking. From Rotterdam the pleasure is arriving by water — a shuttle boat from the Willemskade takes about half an hour, though it runs only Friday to Sunday, so check before you build a day around it.

Zaanse Schans — the convenient one

An open-air conservation village of relocated green timber houses and working mills about fifteen kilometres north of Amsterdam, reached in a short train ride to Zaandijk Zaanse Schans. It is the easier day trip from Amsterdam and correspondingly the busier one, and it feels more curated than Kinderdijk does. Admission arrangements here have been changing, so check the current position on the site’s own website before you go rather than relying on an older guidebook.

Giethoorn — go early or not at all

The “Dutch Venice”, a village in Overijssel whose old core has no through-roads at all, only canals, footbridges and thatched cottages. It is as charming as the photographs suggest and it has become severely overcrowded, with day-tour coaches concentrating into the middle of the day in high season. If you go, go early in the morning or stay the night; arriving at noon in July is the one way to guarantee disappointment.

The Netherlands — common questions

When is the best time to visit the Netherlands?

Late May into mid-June, and September into early October. Both give you mild weather, long daylight, gardens and canal-side terraces in full use, and far thinner crowds than the tulip rush or high summer. April brings the tulips but also the heaviest crowding of the year, the highest hotel prices and King's Day on 27 April, when Amsterdam becomes an orange street party. July and August add a second problem in 2026: the new biometric border checks have pushed arrival queues at Schiphol to one and two hours for non-EU passengers. November through February is genuinely grey, wet and dark by mid-afternoon.

Do I need to rent a car?

No, and for most American visitors a car is a liability rather than a help. The country is about the size of Maryland with one of the densest rail networks on earth, running centre to centre with several trains an hour on the main routes, and Schiphol has a station directly beneath the terminal. City parking is punishing, low-emission zones restrict where you may drive, and Dutch city driving means yielding constantly to cyclists who have the right of way and take it. Trains plus the occasional taxi will be cheaper, calmer and usually faster.

Will my American credit card work in the Netherlands?

Mostly, but this is the single most common American stumble, so plan for it. Dutch shops are built around contactless debit cards, and 'PIN only' signs are still common at bakeries, cafes, market stalls, public toilets and parking machines. The good news is that Albert Heijn, the dominant supermarket chain, began taking Visa and Mastercard credit cards in March 2024, and Jumbo and Dirk take them too. American Express is close to useless outside the airport and chain hotels, and cash is not a safe fallback either, because a good many newer cafes are deliberately card-only and refuse notes. Carry a contactless Visa or Mastercard debit card, a Visa or Mastercard credit card behind it, both loaded into your phone as well, and some small notes.

Should I base myself in Amsterdam or somewhere else?

There is a strong case in 2026 for not sleeping in Amsterdam. The city has capped its hotel beds, cut short-term rentals in the centre to fifteen nights a year, charges a 12.5 per cent tourist tax on top of the VAT that rose from 9 to 21 per cent in January, and has said the tax will go higher still. Utrecht, Haarlem, Delft, Leiden and Rotterdam are all cheaper and calmer and sit within fifteen to forty-five minutes of Amsterdam by train. A good compromise for a first trip is two or three nights in Amsterdam for the museums and the canals, then a quieter regional base for the rest.

How many days do I need?

Seven to ten days is the honest answer for a first trip at an unhurried pace. Three or four days covers Amsterdam's essentials, including the Rijksmuseum, the Van Gogh Museum, a canal boat and the Anne Frank House if you book far ahead. Another three or four lets you add two or three of Haarlem, Delft, Leiden, Utrecht, The Hague for the Mauritshuis, Rotterdam and a windmill site. Because the journeys are so short you can do all of that from one or two bases without repacking every other night, which matters more as you get older.

Is Amsterdam safe, and what should I actually watch for?

Violent crime against visitors is rare and Amsterdam is a safe city to walk in, including after dark in the main districts. The two real risks are mundane. Bicycles come first, and they are the genuine hazard: bike lanes look like pavement and are not, cyclists travel fast and do not slow down, and you are expected to have looked. Pickpockets come second, on crowded trams toward the museums, at Centraal Station and in the crush around Dam Square. And more visitors are hurt by wet cobbles and steep canal-house staircases than by anything criminal at all.
