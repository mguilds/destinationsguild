When to Go to Costa Rica

The Best Time to Visit Costa Rica

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated August 2026

Short answer: December to April is the dry season on Costa Rica’s Pacific side and the most reliable time to go — and the busiest and dearest. May, June, November and the first half of December give you bright mornings, an afternoon shower, green countryside and much better prices. September and October are the wettest and cheapest weeks on the Pacific — and, remarkably, two of the best months on the Caribbean coast, which runs on an entirely different calendar. Below is the full picture: season by season, month by month, the cheapest weeks, the honest word on storms, and the right timing for each region.

Costa Rica sits between eight and eleven degrees north of the equator, so it is warm all year and has no winter to speak of. What changes is the rain, and where it falls. The Pacific side — which is where most visitors go — has a clear dry season from December to April and a green season from May to November. The Caribbean side has neither, and is at its brightest in the very weeks the Pacific is at its wettest. Altitude does the rest: San José is spring-like the year round and Monteverde, at 4,600 feet, is cool and damp in every month there is. Get the timing right for your region and you will trade grey skies for blue.

The Seasons at a Glance

Costa Rica has two seasons on the Pacific side and a calendar of its own on the Caribbean — here is the quick read.

Pacific dry season — December to April

$$$ PeakGuanacaste, Arenal, Manuel AntonioSunniestBusiestBook well ahead

The classic window and the reason most people come in winter: reliably sunny in Guanacaste, dry in Manuel Antonio and Arenal, and driest of all in Monteverde in February and March. It is also the most expensive and most heavily booked stretch of the year, and Christmas, New Year and Easter week need reserving months ahead. Guanacaste turns brown and dusty by late March — beautiful light, but not the emerald country of the brochures.

Green season — May to November

$$ ValueBright morningsAfternoon rainGreen countrysideSofter prices

Costa Rica’s wet season is far kinder than the name suggests. The usual pattern is a clear, bright morning and a defined afternoon downpour that clears by evening, which leaves the whole first half of every day for sightseeing. Rates drop, crowds thin, the countryside turns properly green and the waterfalls run hard. May, June and November are the value sweet spots.

The veranillo — mid-July into August

$$ ShoulderA dry spell inside the wet seasonWhale seasonUS school holidaysBook two months out

Costa Ricans call it the veranillo, the little summer: a genuine drier spell in the middle of the green season, usually running from about mid-July into August. It is why July and August work far better than their rainfall averages suggest, and it coincides with humpback whales on the Pacific. North American school holidays create a mini high season, so book cars and popular hotels a couple of months out.

The wettest weeks — September and October

$ CheapestPacific at its wettestCaribbean at its bestLowest pricesSome lodges close

On the Pacific side these are the two wettest months of the year, tourism falls to its annual floor, and prices are the lowest you will ever see. Some lodges on the Osa Peninsula close outright. But — and this matters — the Caribbean coast runs on the opposite calendar, and September and October are among its finest and driest weeks. A trip planned in these months should be a Caribbean trip, or an Arenal and Central Valley trip, not a Pacific beach one.

Month by Month

The concise version — weather, crowds, and what each month does best.

January

$$$ PeakPacific primeDry and sunnyBook ahead

Peak dry season and peak crowds. Guanacaste is reliably sunny, the Central Valley is at its most pleasant, and the Caribbean is at its wettest. Prices stay high through mid-month; book four to six months out for the popular lodges.

February

$$$ PeakBest all-round monthDriest in MonteverdeQuetzal season begins

Arguably the best all-round month of the year: driest conditions nationwide, including the driest stretch in Monteverde, and a dry window on the Caribbean too. Crowds and prices stay high, but the weather reliability justifies it, and resplendent quetzals begin their nesting season in the cloud forest.

March

$$$ PrimeHot and dryGuanacaste brownsSpring break

Still dry and hot, with Guanacaste turning brown and dusty by the end of the month. Spring-break traffic pushes crowds and rates up, and if Easter week falls in March the country effectively stops for it.

April

$$ ShoulderDry season endsSemana SantaPrices fall late

The dry season’s last stretch, with the first afternoon showers appearing late in the month. Holy Week brings the heaviest domestic travel of the year — beaches packed, roads busy, many businesses shut on the Thursday and Friday. Prices drop noticeably in the final ten days.

May

$$ ValueGreen season beginsClear morningsCrowds vanish

The green season begins, usually with mornings still clear and rain arriving in the afternoon. Crowds fall away sharply, rates drop, and the countryside turns green again within a fortnight. One of the best value-for-weather months of the whole year.

June

$$ ValueLow crowdsLow pricesRivers running

A consistent green-season pattern: sunny mornings, afternoon downpours, few visitors and low prices. Rivers run high, which is excellent for whitewater rafting and less good for road conditions in the far south.

July

$$ ShoulderVeranillo dry spellUS holidaysWhales

The veranillo brings a genuine drier spell, and North American summer holidays create a mini high season. Book cars and popular hotels two months ahead; prices sit above June but well below the winter peak.

August

$$ ValueWhale seasonGood valueQuiet after mid-month

Much like July with a little more rain, and humpback whale season under way on the Pacific. Crowds thin after mid-month and the value is good.

September

$ LowestPacific wettestCaribbean bestCheapest rates

The wettest month on the Pacific side and the start of the true low season, with the lowest prices of the year and empty trails. The Osa and parts of the central Pacific are genuinely difficult. On the Caribbean, by contrast, this is one of the two best months.

October

$ LowestSlowest monthSome lodges closeCaribbean shines

The slowest and cheapest tourism month of the year. Pacific rainfall peaks, some Osa lodges close, and even Monteverde can be socked in for days. Caribbean weather, however, is at its finest — plan a September or October trip around Puerto Viejo, Cahuita and Tortuguero.

November

$$ ValueRains taperGreen and emptyQuiet bargain

A transitional month and a quiet bargain: the rains taper on the Pacific through the month, crowds have not yet returned, and by late November much of the country is dry and green at the same time. The Caribbean, though, is entering its wettest stretch.

December

$$$ PeakTwo months in oneGreat value to the 15thPeak from the 18th

Two different months in one. Through about the 15th the weather is drying, crowds are light and prices are still low — one of the best-value windows all year. From roughly the 18th the country flips to peak season, and Christmas and New Year are the dearest and most heavily booked days of the year.

The Cheapest Time to Go

If price is the deciding factor, two windows are worth knowing — one cheap, one merely good value.

September and October — the lowest prices of the year

$ LowestPacific wettestEmpty trailsCaribbean at its best

Tourism falls to its annual floor in these two months and hotel rates, tours and car hire follow it down. The catch is real: the Pacific side is at its wettest, some Osa Peninsula lodges close outright, and mountain roads can wash out. The way to use these months well is to point the trip at the Caribbean — Puerto Viejo, Cahuita, Tortuguero — where September and October are two of the driest and finest weeks of the year, or to stay inland at Arenal and in the Central Valley.

For better weather at only a modest premium, aim instead for May, June, November or the first two weeks of December. These are the value sweet spots: clear mornings, an afternoon shower, green countryside, thin crowds and prices well below the winter peak. Avoid Christmas to New Year and Semana Santa entirely unless you have booked months in advance.

Storms, Roads & Staying Comfortable

The honest version, because this is the part travellers most often get wrong.

Costa Rica and hurricanes — the truth

One landfall in 170 yearsWater, not windSep–Oct caution

Costa Rica lies far enough south that hurricanes almost never reach it: in more than 170 years of records exactly one has made landfall, Otto in November 2016, on the far northern border. Do not, however, let anyone tell you the country is untouched by storms. The danger here is water rather than wind — Tropical Storm Nate in October 2017 never made landfall and still caused the costliest natural disaster in the country’s history through flooding and landslides.

The practical reading for a traveller: September and October are the wettest months on the Pacific side regardless of storms, and a system passing offshore can turn that into flooded rivers and closed mountain roads within hours. If you travel then, buy travel insurance, build a spare day into the itinerary, favour the Caribbean, the Central Valley and Arenal over the Osa Peninsula, and do not plan a tight connection on your last day.

Roads, shuttles and driving after dark

Dark by 5:45 in high seasonNo guard railsShuttles are easier

Costa Rica is close to the equator, so the sun sets between about a quarter past five and six o’clock all year — and it is at its earliest in November and December, exactly the months most visitors arrive. Treat five o’clock, not six, as the hour to be off the mountain roads in high season. Mountain roads are narrow, unlit and unshouldered, often without guard rails, and shared with pedestrians, cyclists and the occasional cow. Do not drive outside towns after dark, and plan every travel day to finish by late afternoon — this matters more in the green season, when a landslide or a flooded ford can add an hour without warning.

For most travellers we would suggest shared or private shuttles between the main hubs, with a short domestic flight for the long hops. If you do hire a car, take a high-clearance vehicle rather than a compact, and budget for the mandatory Costa Rican liability insurance charged at the counter on top of the advertised rate — it is the single most common billing surprise visitors report. Never leave anything visible in a parked car, at any hour, anywhere.

Frequently Asked Questions

The questions travellers ask most when timing a trip to Costa Rica.

What is the best time to visit Costa Rica?

For the Pacific side — Guanacaste, Manuel Antonio, Arenal, Monteverde and the Central Valley — the best weather runs December to April, with February and March the most reliable of all. This is also the busiest and most expensive season. The Caribbean coast runs on a different calendar and is at its best in February–March and again in September–October. If you want good weather without peak prices, aim for May, June, November or the first half of December.

What is the cheapest time to visit Costa Rica?

September and October are the cheapest months by a clear margin — they are the wettest weeks on the Pacific and tourism falls to its annual floor. If you would rather not gamble on the weather, May, June, November and the first two weeks of December offer materially better conditions for a modest premium. The dearest periods are Christmas through January and Easter week.

Does Costa Rica get hurricanes?

Direct hits are almost unheard of. Costa Rica lies far enough south of the Atlantic hurricane belt that in more than 170 years of records only one hurricane has made landfall — Otto, in November 2016, on the far northern border. The genuine risk is water, not wind: September and October are the wettest months on the Pacific side, driven by the seasonal rain belt rather than by storms, and a hurricane passing well offshore can still amplify that into flooded rivers, landslides and closed mountain roads within hours. In those two months, buy travel insurance, build a spare day into the itinerary, and favour the Caribbean, the Central Valley or Arenal over the Osa Peninsula.

When is the best time to see wildlife in Costa Rica?

There is no bad month, but there are good windows. Resplendent quetzals nest in the Monteverde and Talamanca cloud forests from about February to May. Humpback whales are on the Pacific from July to October, with a second northern-hemisphere season from December to March. Leatherback turtles nest at Playa Grande roughly October to March; green turtles arrive at Tortuguero on the Caribbean from July to October. Green-season months bring the most active frogs, insects and birdsong.

Is the green season a bad time to visit Costa Rica?

Not at all, and many regular visitors prefer it. The typical green-season day gives you a clear, bright morning and a defined afternoon downpour that clears by evening — so sightsee early and keep the afternoon for the hot springs or a book. You get greener country, fuller waterfalls, far fewer people and materially lower prices. The two months to treat with real caution are September and October on the Pacific side.

Which month should I avoid in Costa Rica?

If your heart is set on a Pacific beach, avoid September and October — they are genuinely wet, some Osa lodges close, and mountain roads can wash out. If your concern is crowds and cost rather than rain, avoid Christmas to New Year and Semana Santa (Easter week), which are the most expensive and most heavily booked days of the year and require reserving months in advance.
