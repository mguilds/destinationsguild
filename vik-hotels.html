Where to Stay in Vík

Where to Stay in Vík

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated August 2026

Vík í Mýrdal is a village of about seven hundred and fifty people, the southernmost settlement on the Icelandic mainland, sitting between a black beach and a green ridge two and a half hours east of Reykjavík. Most visitors see it for forty minutes, buy a coffee and drive on, and that is a small tragedy: the sea stacks at sunrise, the cliff-top church in the evening light and the emptiness of the beach after five o’clock are the reasons to come, and none of them are available to a day tripper. Stay the night. The village has perhaps a dozen places to sleep, a supermarket, a petrol station and two or three restaurants, and that is the honest sum of it — but it puts you two hours ahead of every coach leaving the capital in the morning.

Choosing Your Spot

In the village, or out along the ring road?

OrientationWalkabilityWind and rainDistances

The village itself is small enough to walk end to end in fifteen minutes, and staying in it means you can leave the car and walk to dinner, to the wool shop and up to the church — a genuine climb, but a short one, with the best view in the district at the top. The ridge and the farms east of town, including the largest hotel in the district at Höfðabrekka, are five to ten minutes’ drive away: quieter, darker for the northern lights, and with a car essential for so much as a pint of milk. West towards Dyrhólaey puts you nearer the arch and the puffin cliffs and further from anywhere to eat. Two things to know wherever you sleep: Vík is the wettest inhabited place in Iceland, with well over twice Reykjavík’s rainfall, and the wind here comes off the open Atlantic with nothing in its way. Pack for both, and do not judge the village by an afternoon of horizontal drizzle.

The best beds in the district

No grand hotels here — but two properly run, comfortable places that anchor the village and the valley.

Hótel Kría

The newest and most comfortable hotel in Vík, purpose-built on the edge of the village with a restaurant, a bar and rooms that are quiet and properly insulated against the weather that is the whole point of this coast. It is the easy answer for anyone who wants a modern room with level access and a lift after a day of gravel car parks. Ask for a room facing the ridge or the sea rather than the road.

Family-friendly

Vík has no dedicated family hotel and we will not pretend otherwise. What works for families here is space and a kitchen: the suites and country hotels listed above take families comfortably, and several have rooms sleeping four. Two honest cautions for anyone travelling with children on this coast. First, Reynisfjara is not a beach to play on — the waves there are genuinely lethal and have killed visitors, and children must be kept well back from the water at all times. Second, the sights here are cliffs, sea and weather rather than playgrounds; the nearest swimming pool is at Vík itself and is the reliable rainy-afternoon answer.

Adults-only & grown-up quiet

There is no adults-only property in Vík that Trip.com sells, and in a village of seven hundred and fifty people we would be surprised if there were. If quiet is what you are after, the answer here is geography rather than policy: take one of the country hotels out along the ridge or towards Dyrhólaey, where the nearest neighbour is a field away and the only sound after ten at night is the sea. Hotel Katla and Hótel Dyrhólaey, both above, are the two we would choose.

Vík Hotel FAQ

Is it worth staying overnight in Vík rather than doing a day trip?

Yes, and it is the single best decision most south-coast itineraries can make. Vík is two and a half hours from Reykjavík, which means every coach and every self-drive day tripper arrives between about eleven and three and leaves again. Sleep here and you get Reynisfjara at seven in the morning, the church hill at sunset and the sea stacks with nobody in the photograph. It also breaks the drive to Jökulsárlón into two civilised halves rather than one exhausting one.

How dangerous is the black beach, really?

Genuinely dangerous, and this is not a formality. Reynisfjara is exposed to the open Atlantic with no shelf offshore, which produces sudden “sneaker” waves that run far higher up the sand than the ones before them, in water cold enough to disable a strong swimmer in minutes. People have been killed there, including in recent years. There is now a warning system with a danger rating displayed at the car park. Read it, stay well up the beach, never turn your back on the sea, and do not let anyone stand on the wet sand for a photograph.

How far is Vík from the main sights?

Skógafoss is about half an hour west, Seljalandsfoss forty-five minutes, and Reynisfjara and Dyrhólaey are ten and fifteen minutes from the village. Going east, Fjaðrárgljúfur canyon is about fifty minutes and the glacier lagoon at Jökulsárlón is around two and a half hours. Reykjavík is 187 kilometres, roughly two and a half hours in good conditions.

Does it really rain that much?

Yes. Vík is the wettest inhabited place in Iceland, receiving well over twice as much rain as Reykjavík, because the moist Atlantic air runs straight into the Mýrdalsjökull ice cap behind the village. Plan for it rather than resent it: proper waterproofs, a spare pair of shoes, and the knowledge that a wet morning here very often turns into a clear evening.

What about Katla, the volcano?

Katla lies under the Mýrdalsjökull ice cap above the village and is closely monitored. The hazard for Vík is not lava but a glacial flood, and the village practises for it: the church on the hill is the assembly point, and evacuation drills are a normal part of local life. This is a well-managed known risk rather than a reason to stay away, and any alert would be public and unmissable long before it mattered to you.

Is there anywhere to eat?

A handful of places — the hotel restaurants, a soup and sandwich place or two, the restaurant at the petrol station, and a supermarket for anything else. In high summer they are busy and in winter some keep short hours. If you arrive after nine at night outside high season, having something in the car is a sensible precaution.

Can I see puffins from Vík?

Yes, in season. Dyrhólaey, fifteen minutes west, is one of the easiest places in Iceland to see them from level ground, roughly from mid-May to early August. Part of the promontory closes during the height of nesting to protect the birds, and the upper road can shut in bad weather. They are gone by the middle of August.

Do I need a car?

To reach Vík, effectively yes, unless you are on a tour or the scheduled bus. Once here, the village itself is walkable but Reynisfjara, Dyrhólaey and the farm hotels are not — they are a few miles apart on the ring road with no footpath. If you would rather not drive, choose accommodation in the village and book the sights as a guided half-day from Reykjavík instead.
