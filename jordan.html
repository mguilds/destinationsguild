Jordan — Petra, Wadi Rum & the Lowest Place on Earth

Jordan Travel Guide — Where to Stay, Region by Region

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Jordan is a small country that holds an unreasonable amount. Petra is the reason most people come, and it earns it. But the Roman street at Jerash is one of the best preserved anywhere, Wadi Rum is a desert you sleep in rather than drive through, the Dead Sea is the lowest dry land on earth, and Aqaba puts a coral reef at the bottom of the country. Distances are short, the roads are good, and almost everyone travels the same north-to-south line. Below is each region, where to sleep in it, and the honest word on heat, steps and season that decides whether a trip works.

Why visit Jordan

Because it is the rare country where the famous thing is not a disappointment. Petra is bigger, stranger and more demanding than the photographs suggest, and it rewards two days rather than one. Because the distances are genuinely short — Amman to Petra is about three hours, Petra to Wadi Rum about an hour and a half — so a week covers the country without living on a coach. Because Jordanian hospitality is not a tourist-board phrase; tea gets offered constantly and it is meant. And because the country stacks things that have nothing to do with each other within a morning’s drive: a Roman colonnade, a Crusader castle, a desert of red sand, a sea you cannot sink in and a coral reef.

The honest counterweight: Jordan is not cheap, the summer in the south is severe, and the region around it has been unsettled. We say what we know about all three below rather than leaving you to find out.

Jordan at a glance

Language

Arabic. English is widely spoken in Amman, at the main sites and anywhere that deals with visitors, and much less so in villages. A few words of Arabic are received very warmly.

Money

The Jordanian dinar (JOD), pegged to the US dollar, so it does not swing the way some regional currencies do. Cards work in Amman and the bigger hotels; carry cash for camps, taxis, village cafes and small sites. Jordan is more expensive than most visitors expect.

Time and plugs

One time zone, UTC+3 all year — Jordan no longer changes its clocks seasonally. Sockets are a mix of European two-pin and British three-pin at 230 volts, so a universal adapter is the safe answer rather than a single one.

Getting in by air

Queen Alia International (AMM), about 35 km south of Amman, takes almost all international flights. King Hussein International (AQJ) at Aqaba is the back door to the south and can save a long drive at the start or end of a trip.

Getting around

Most visitors hire a car or a driver; the Desert Highway and the Dead Sea road are straightforward, and the King’s Highway is the slow scenic alternative. The JETT coach links Amman, Petra and Aqaba. There is no passenger rail network to speak of.

The working week

Friday and Saturday are the weekend, and Friday morning is the quietest half-day of the week. During Ramadan, daytime eating and drinking in public is restricted and opening hours shift; it is a rewarding time to visit if you plan around it rather than ignore it.

The four Jordans

1. Amman and the north

The capital spreads over a series of hills, with a Roman theatre and a hilltop citadel sitting in the middle of ordinary city traffic. Most people give it a night at each end of the trip, which is about right. North of the city, Jerash is the reason to add a day: a Roman provincial city with its colonnaded street, oval forum and two theatres still standing, and an easy half-day from Amman. Umm Qais, further north, looks out over three countries. Note that the Foreign Office advises against all travel to within three kilometres of the Syrian border, which is north of Umm Qais, not at it.

2. The King’s Highway and the Dead Sea

The old road south runs through Madaba, where a sixth-century mosaic map of the Holy Land is set into a church floor, past Mount Nebo and the Crusader castle at Kerak. It is slower than the Desert Highway and much better. West of it the land drops to the Dead Sea, the lowest dry land on earth at around 430 metres below sea level, where a line of resort hotels holds the Jordanian shore. Floating in it is genuinely strange; it is also oily, stings any cut you had forgotten about, and must never get in your eyes.

3. Petra and the south

Petra is entered from the town of Wadi Musa, which is where you sleep. The approach runs down the Siq, a narrow cleft well over a kilometre long, and opens on the Treasury; beyond that the city carries on for miles, with the Monastery up a long rock-cut stair. South of it, Wadi Rum is red sand and sandstone towers, run largely by Bedouin families as desert camps — a night there is one of the things people remember longest. Between the two sits the Dana Biosphere Reserve, for walkers.

4. Aqaba and the Red Sea

Jordan’s only coast, and a short one. Aqaba is a working port and a relaxed resort town with reefs close to shore, warm water in winter and the country’s second airport. It is the natural end of the classic route, and it is where to go in January when Amman is cold and wet.

Things to do in Jordan

Walk into Petra early, before the coaches and before the heat, and give it a second day rather than trying to see it in one. Sleep in a Wadi Rum camp and stay up for the sky. Walk the colonnaded street at Jerash. Float in the Dead Sea for ten minutes and then wash it off properly. Dive or snorkel the reefs at Aqaba. Drive the King’s Highway instead of the Desert Highway at least once. Eat mansaf if it is offered to you, and accept the tea.

We do not sell tickets or tours, and we do not carry booking links for attractions. Entry to more than forty sites, Petra included, is bundled into the official Jordan Pass described below, which is almost always the sensible way to pay for them.

Best time to visit & getting there

Spring and autumn, without much argument. March to May and September to November give warm days and cool nights across the whole country and are the only months when Petra, Wadi Rum and the Dead Sea are all comfortable at once. Summer is severe in the south and at the Dead Sea; Amman, high and dry, copes better. Winter brings rain and real cold to Amman and Petra — snow closes Petra occasionally — while Aqaba stays mild and is the winter-sun exception. The month-by-month detail is in our best time to visit Jordan guide.

Fishing here

Jordan does not have a Guild fishing book, and with one short stretch of Red Sea coast and no trout water to speak of, it is not an obvious candidate for one. What exists is sea fishing out of Aqaba, arranged locally with a skipper from the harbour rather than bought as a package. We would rather say that plainly than pad the page. The whole shelf is in The Guild Fishing Library.

Travelling in your later years

Jordan is very manageable with one large exception, and it is the one everybody comes for. Petra is a serious walk. The Siq alone is well over a kilometre each way on uneven ground before you reach the Treasury, the site continues for miles past it, and the Monastery is up a long flight of rock-cut steps with no handrail. Horses, carts and donkeys are offered at the gate; a cart along the Siq is the usual answer and is a reasonable one. Plan Petra as two shorter days rather than one long one, start early, and choose a hotel near the gate — our Wadi Musa guide says which they are, because the walk down into town and back up is itself a climb.

Wadi Rum means a four-wheel drive over sand and a camp with simple facilities; the better camps have proper beds and private bathrooms, but ask rather than assume. The Dead Sea resorts are built down a steep slope and many use long stepped paths or a shuttle buggy to reach the shore — worth asking about before booking. Amman is hilly and its pavements are uneven; taxis are cheap and are the sensible way to move. Aqaba is the flattest and gentlest place in the country, which is a good reason to finish there rather than start.

Getting in: what to expect at the border

You need a visa, and there are two sensible ways to get one. The UK Foreign Office states that a single-entry visit visa can be bought on arrival at a Jordanian airport or at the Sheikh Hussein / North Border land crossing for 40 Jordanian dinars, valid for three months. The alternative, and for most tourists the better one, is the Jordan Pass: bought online before you travel, it costs between 70 and 80 dinars depending on which version you choose, it includes the tourist visa fee, and it covers entry to more than forty sites including Petra, Jerash and Wadi Rum. It requires a stay of at least three nights and is valid three months from the date you enter. Because it bundles the visa with the single most expensive ticket in the country, it usually pays for itself on a normal week’s trip. Buy it only from the official site, jordanpass.jo, which is run by the Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities.

Your passport must have at least six months left on it from the date you arrive. If you plan to cross into Israel or the Palestinian territories and come back, the Foreign Office notes that a multiple-entry visa obtained in advance is the way to do it, because the Jordan Pass and the visa on arrival are single entry. There is a registration rule for people staying more than fourteen days, but the Foreign Office is explicit that tourists are exempt from it.

On safety, the Guild’s position is to tell you where to look rather than to reassure you. The UK Foreign Office advises against all travel to within three kilometres of the border with Syria — a corner of the country the tourist route does not go near. Separately, and more importantly for planning, it warns that the wider region is unpredictable, that escalation can come at short notice, and that flight cancellations and airspace closures are a real possibility. None of that is a reason we would tell you not to go, and none of it is something we would leave out. Read your own government’s current advice — it is written for your passport and updated far more often than any guide, ours included — and buy insurance that covers disruption.

Jordan — questions

How many days do I need in Jordan?

Five to seven nights is the honest minimum for the classic route. Two nights in Amman, two in Wadi Musa for Petra, one in a Wadi Rum camp and one at the Dead Sea or Aqaba covers the country without a forced march. Petra alone deserves two days; people who give it one afternoon almost always say it was the mistake of the trip.

Do I need a visa for Jordan, and what is the Jordan Pass?

Yes, you need a visa. The UK Foreign Office states that a single-entry visit visa can be bought on arrival at a Jordanian airport for 40 Jordanian dinars, valid three months. If you are coming as a tourist and staying at least three nights, the Jordan Pass is bought online before you fly, costs between 70 and 80 dinars depending on the version, includes the visa fee and covers entry to more than forty sites including Petra, Jerash and Wadi Rum. Buy it only from the official site, jordanpass.jo. Rules change: check your own government's current advice before you book.

Is Jordan safe to visit right now?

That is a question we will not answer with a flat yes or no, because the honest answer depends on when you are reading this. The UK Foreign Office advises against all travel to within three kilometres of the border with Syria, and separately warns that the wider Middle East is unpredictable, that escalation can happen at short notice, and that flight cancellations and airspace closures are a real possibility. The main tourist route is a long way from that border. Read your own government's current advice, written for your passport and updated far more often than any guidebook, and buy insurance that covers disruption.

Is Petra hard going on foot?

Yes, and it is worth planning for. The walk in through the Siq is well over a kilometre before the Treasury comes into view, the site spreads for miles beyond it, and the Monastery is reached by a long flight of rock-cut steps. Horse, cart and donkey rides are offered and are a matter of personal judgement. Our Wadi Musa guide says which hotels are closest to the gate, which matters more here than almost anywhere.

What is the money, and will my cards work?

The Jordanian dinar, which is pegged to the US dollar and so does not swing about the way some regional currencies do. Cards are accepted in Amman hotels, restaurants and larger shops; carry cash for Bedouin camps, village cafes, taxis and small sites. Jordan is not a budget destination by regional standards and visitors are often surprised by that.
