The Guild's chapter on South America

South America is the continent American travellers most often put off, and most often wish they had seen sooner. The flights are long but they run north and south rather than east and west, which means almost no jet lag to fight: leave Miami after dinner and wake up over Rio. Prices on the ground are gentle, the coastline is enormous, and the cities have a warmth that surprises first-time visitors. The Guild's chapter on South America is one book today — Brazil — and we would rather tell you that outright than dress a single shelf up as a library. Argentina, Peru and Chile are on the writing desk.

BrazilBOOK 1

Brazil is roughly the size of the continental United States and has a coastline to match — Rio's beaches under their granite peaks, São Paulo's restaurants and galleries, the Amazon in the north and the Iguaçu Falls in the far south. Two or three weeks is a fair minimum if you want to see more than one region, and domestic flights rather than buses are how Brazilians themselves cover the distances. One important piece of planning: the State Department is unambiguous that US citizens need a visa to enter Brazil and must apply for and be approved before travelling.

When to go

Most of South America lies south of the equator, so the calendar turns over: December to March is high summer, June to August is winter. The one exception to keep in mind is the tropical north, where the year divides into wet and dry rather than hot and cold.

December to March — the southern summer

Hot, humid and festive along the Brazilian coast, with afternoon thunderstorms and the highest prices of the year. Carnival falls in February or early March — in 2027 it begins on Friday 5 February — and Rio hotel rates roughly double around it, so book far ahead or plan deliberately around it. This is also the only sensible window for Patagonia at the far south of the continent.

April to June — the shoulder

Our pick for Rio and the southeast. The heat and humidity ease off, the sea is still warm, the crowds have gone home and airfares fall away sharply after Carnival. Days are shorter but reliably sunny, and this is when the coastal cities feel most like themselves rather than like a resort.

June to September — the dry winter

The best months for wildlife and for the interior. In the Pantanal wetlands the water drops and the animals concentrate, which is why photographers go then; the Amazon is easier to walk; and the Andean countries to the west are in their dry season, which is the right time for the high mountains. Southern Brazil genuinely gets cold, so pack a coat for São Paulo evenings.

October and November — the quiet stretch

The cheapest time to fly to Brazil and one of the pleasantest to travel. Spring warmth is returning, the summer crowds have not yet arrived and hotels are open to negotiation. Rain is picking up in the southeast by late November, but rarely for a whole day at a time.

Do Americans need a visa for Brazil?

Yes. The US State Department's country information page states plainly that you need a visa to enter Brazil and that you must apply for and be approved for it before you travel. Brazil operates an online e-visa for tourists. Apply well before departure rather than in the last fortnight, and confirm current requirements and fees with the Brazilian authorities, since these rules have changed more than once in recent years.

When is the best time to visit South America?

For Brazil's coast, April to June is the Guild's pick — warm, dry enough, far cheaper than summer and free of the Carnival crush. December to March is high summer: wonderful for the beaches and the festivals, but hot, humid and expensive. June to September is the dry winter, which is the right season for the Pantanal, the Amazon and the Andes further west. Remember that the seasons run opposite to ours everywhere south of the equator.

How long is the flight, and how bad is the jet lag?

Better than you expect. Miami to Rio de Janeiro is roughly nine hours, New York to São Paulo about ten, and both are usually overnight. Because the route runs north to south rather than across the Atlantic, the time difference from the US East Coast is only one to three hours depending on the season, so most travellers arrive tired but not disoriented — a real advantage for anyone who finds European jet lag hard going.
