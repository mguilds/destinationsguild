Santiago’s Best Places to Stay

Where to Stay in Santiago — Best Areas & Hotels

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Santiago lies in a long bowl between the coastal range and the Andes, and the district you choose decides how much of the city you actually see. Do you want the historic centre with the museums and the metro at the door, the glass towers of Las Condes and Vitacura under the mountains with the best restaurants and the best views, or Providencia, the leafy middle ground that is twenty minutes from both? Santiago Centro & Lastarria

The old city: the Plaza de Armas, the presidential palace, the fish market and the two hills you climb for the view. Staying here puts the museums and the metro on your doorstep and costs noticeably less than the eastern districts. The trade is that the centre empties after office hours, parts of it feel tired at night, and the restaurant scene worth crossing town for is elsewhere. Lastarria and Bellas Artes, on the eastern edge of the centre, are the exception — walkable, leafy and busy in the evening.

Hotel Diego de Almagro Santiago Centro

$$ Mid-rangeOn the AlamedaRooftop poolMetro at the door

A straightforward, well-run business hotel on the Alameda, Santiago’s main avenue, with Universidad de Chile metro directly below and the Plaza de Armas a ten-minute walk north. Rooms are plain and quiet enough on the upper floors; the small rooftop pool is a genuine pleasure in January. Best for a traveller who wants the centre, a lift, and no surprises on the bill. The Alameda is a wide, loud road — ask for a room off the front.

Las Condes & Vitacura — the modern east

Where most visitors on business sleep, and where a good many holiday visitors end up too. This is the glass-tower end of the city under the Andes: safe, clean, well served by metro Line 1, and full of the restaurants Santiago is actually proud of. It is also the least Chilean-feeling part of Santiago, and you will be taking the metro into town most mornings. The views of the cordillera from an upper floor on a clear day are the compensation, and they are considerable.

Renaissance Santiago Hotel

$$$$ LuxuryVitacuraSpa and poolAndes views

The grand address of the eastern districts, a twin-tower hotel on Avenida Kennedy in Vitacura with a serious spa, a proper pool and rooms that look straight at the mountains from the higher floors. It sits beside two of the city’s big shopping centres and a short taxi from the Vitacura restaurant streets. Best for a traveller who wants comfort and a view and does not mind being a metro ride from the old city.

Providencia & Bellavista — the middle ground

The district we would send most first-time visitors to. Providencia sits between the old centre and the modern east, on the same metro line as both, and it is a real neighbourhood: tree-lined, residential, with its own cafes and bookshops rather than a tourist strip. Bellavista, across the river at the foot of San Cristóbal hill, holds Pablo Neruda’s house and the late-night bars, and is lively on a Friday in a way that suits some travellers and not others.

What to know before you book

The smog is a winter problem

Santiago sits in a bowl with the Andes behind it, and from roughly May to August the cold air traps the city’s pollution. On the worst winter days the authorities restrict traffic and the mountains disappear entirely. Anyone with asthma or a heart condition should plan a winter visit with that in mind; in summer the air clears and the cordillera is visible most mornings.

Earthquakes are normal here

Chile is one of the most seismically active countries on earth and Santiago feels tremors regularly. This is not a reason to stay away: Chilean building codes are among the strictest anywhere and modern hotels are built for it. Read the card on the back of your door, note which stairwell is nearest, and then forget about it, which is what Chileans do.

Distances are longer than the map suggests

Santiago runs about thirty kilometres east to west and the metro, though excellent, is slow at rush hour. A hotel in Las Condes is forty minutes from the Plaza de Armas on a weekday morning. Pick your district for where you will spend your days rather than for the room.

Taxis, apps and the airport

The airport is about 20 km west of the centre. Official airport taxis and the booked transfer desks inside the terminal are the uncomplicated choice; ride-hailing apps operate but have a designated pick-up area. Agree the fare or use the meter, and have pesos for the toll roads.

Where to stay in Santiago — questions

Which district should a first-time visitor choose?

Providencia, in most cases. It is on metro Line 1 between the old centre and the modern east, so both are fifteen to twenty minutes away, and it is a real neighbourhood rather than a business district or a tourist strip. Choose Las Condes or Vitacura instead if you want the newest hotels, the best mountain views and the restaurant scene, and accept the commute into town. Choose Santiago Centro if your days will be spent in the museums and the historic quarter and you would rather spend the saving on travelling further afield.

Is Santiago safe for visitors?

Broadly yes, with the ordinary care any large Latin American city asks for. Violent crime against visitors is uncommon; opportunistic theft is not, and it concentrates in a few predictable places — the Plaza de Armas, crowded metro carriages, the approaches to Cerro Santa Lucía and the bus terminals. Keep your phone out of sight on the street, do not leave a bag on the back of a chair, and take a taxi rather than walk through the centre late at night. Demonstrations gather on and around the Alameda and Plaza Baquedano; they are usually announced in advance and are best simply avoided.

How many days does Santiago need?

Two full days covers the city itself: the historic centre and the Pre-Columbian art museum on one, a hill and a neighbourhood on the other. Most visitors then use Santiago as a base for a third and fourth day — the Casablanca and Maipo wine valleys are each about an hour away, Valparaíso and the coast about ninety minutes, and in winter three ski areas are closer to the city than that.

Do I need Spanish?

It helps more here than in many capitals. English is common in the eastern districts, in the better hotels and among younger Chileans, but it is far from universal in the centre, in taxis or in ordinary restaurants. Chilean Spanish is also famously fast and full of local idiom, so even confident speakers should expect to ask people to slow down. A translation app and a few polite phrases go a long way.

What does a hotel cost in Santiago?

Less than in most capital cities of comparable size, which is one of the quiet pleasures of travelling here, but the range is wide and rates move with the season and the exchange rate.
