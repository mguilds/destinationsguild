Moshi — The Town Under Kilimanjaro

Where to Stay in Moshi — Area by Area

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Moshi exists, for visitors, because Kilimanjaro is above it. Almost everyone here is about to climb, has just climbed, or is deciding whether to, and where you sleep follows from which of those you are and which gate your route uses.

The town itself is likeable and low-key, with good coffee grown on the slopes you are about to walk up. It is also where you want to be the day you come down, which is the part of the trip people under-book.

The town centre — for the briefing and the gear shop

Moshi is small, walkable and almost entirely organised around Kilimanjaro. The centre has the trekking offices, the kit shops and hire, the coffee houses and most of the mid-priced rooms, and on a clear morning the mountain stands over the end of the street. If your climb starts with a briefing and a kit check — and it should — stay here.

Shanty Town and the northern edge — gardens under the mountain

The residential streets north of the centre, confusingly named, hold the garden lodges and the larger old hotels in grounds, slightly higher and cooler, with the best of the Kilimanjaro views. This is where to spend the night before and the two nights after, because what you want after six days on the mountain is a lawn, a long bath and somebody else cooking.

Machame and the western approach — at the trailhead

Out towards the Machame and Londorossi gates the lodges sit among banana and coffee smallholdings on the mountain’s southern slopes. Staying out here saves an hour of driving on the first morning and puts you to sleep in forest quiet. It also means you are a long way from a pharmacy and anything you forgot.

Marangu — the eastern gate and the waterfalls

Forty minutes east, Marangu is the old gateway village, greener and wetter, with long-standing hotels used by generations of climbers and a set of waterfall and cave walks of its own. It is the right base for the Marangu route, and a pleasant day out from Moshi if you are not climbing at all.

Before you book

Government travel insurance is compulsory

Every visitor to mainland Tanzania and to Zanzibar, residents aside, must hold inbound travel insurance provided by the Tanzanian government — and you must hold it even if you already have a policy of your own from home. You buy it online beforehand or at your port of entry, it covers the length of your stay up to 92 days, and you only buy it for your first port of entry. Arriving in Zanzibar first means buying from the Zanzibar Insurance Corporation; arriving on the mainland first means the National Insurance Corporation. You may be refused entry without it. Source: UK Foreign Office entry requirements for Tanzania, still current at 22 November 2026.

Get the e-visa before you fly

You need a tourist or business visa, and you apply for an e-visa online before you travel — it is no longer possible to get one from the Tanzanian High Commission in London. A single-entry visa is available on arrival at the main ports of entry if you meet the requirements, and you may be asked to show your return or onward ticket; a multiple-entry visa cannot be had on arrival. Your passport needs an expiry date at least six months after you arrive and at least one blank page.

Health screening at the border

Tanzania’s Ministry of Health announced heightened screening on 17 October for travellers arriving from high-risk regions and affected countries, at every point of entry, and every traveller has to complete a Travellers Health Surveillance Form before departure or on arrival. You also pass through mpox screening — body temperature checks — at all points of entry. Check the current list of affected countries before you fly.

Your phone, and what you photograph

This one catches visitors out. It is illegal in Tanzania to take or share photographs or video of protests and violence, and the security services may ask to inspect your phone and other devices. The police have said it is an offence to hold or share images that “cause panic or degrade a person’s dignity”. Keep away from anything that could be read as political, online as much as in public. Source: UK Foreign Office safety and security advice for Tanzania, updated 30 October 2026.

Malaria, and where the hospitals are

Malaria is a risk across Tanzania, and dengue, cholera and mpox are all named in official health advice. Get travel health advice at least eight weeks before you fly and ask about your whole itinerary rather than one city. Medical facilities are limited, particularly outside Dar es Salaam, so insurance that covers medical evacuation is not a luxury here. The emergency number is 112.

Demonstrations, and a change in the mood

The general election of 29 October 2025 was followed by protests and a security response that caused deaths and injuries in many parts of mainland Tanzania, and further demonstrations are possible at any time. Official advice also notes an increase in anti-foreigner rhetoric, and that the authorities have limited internet access during and after protests before now. Write down your airline, insurer and embassy numbers rather than keeping them only online, and leave any crowd you come across.

Questions readers actually ask

Moshi or Arusha for climbing Kilimanjaro?


Moshi, almost always. It sits directly under the mountain, the trekking companies and kit shops are here, and the drive to the southern and eastern gates is short. Arusha is the base for the Serengeti and Ngorongoro safari circuit and is an hour and a half further from Kilimanjaro's gates. Many visitors do both, and the usual order is the climb from Moshi first, then the safari from Arusha, because a safari vehicle is a far better place to be with tired legs.

How many nights should I book either side of the climb?


One before and two after is the pattern we would book. One before gives you the briefing, the kit check and a proper sleep. Two after matters more than people expect: the first day off the mountain is not the day to fly, and an unhurried day with a bath and a lawn is worth a great deal. If your flight home is from Kilimanjaro airport it is forty minutes away, which makes that easy.

Is altitude a problem in Moshi itself?


No. Moshi sits at roughly 800 metres, low enough that nobody feels it. The altitude question belongs to the climb, where the gain is very large and the single biggest factor in whether you reach the summit is how many days your route takes. Official health advice does name altitude sickness as a risk in parts of Tanzania; on Kilimanjaro, pay for the extra day.

What is the weather like for climbing?


The two dry windows are the long one from late June to October and the shorter one from January to early March, and those are when most people climb. The long rains from March to May and the short rains in November make the forest sections wet and the views scarce. Whatever the month, the summit night is below freezing and often well below, and the gap between the bottom of the mountain and the top is the whole point of the kit list.

Do I need the government travel insurance for a climb?


Yes, like every other visitor: Tanzania requires government-provided inbound travel insurance from all visitors to the mainland and Zanzibar except residents, even if you already hold your own policy, bought online or at your port of entry and valid up to 92 days. It is not a substitute for proper climbing cover. Check separately that your own policy covers trekking to the altitude you are actually going to, and covers helicopter evacuation, because many standard policies stop well below the summit.
