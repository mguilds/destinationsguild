Where to Stay in Cayo Coco

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

A chain of flat, scrubby cays off Cuba’s north coast, joined to the mainland by seventeen miles of stone causeway, with some of the finest sand in the Caribbean and no town of any kind. Everything out here is an all-inclusive resort. The casas particulares are back across the water in Morón — and for some travellers that is not a preference but the only lawful option.

Before you book anything

This page carries no booking buttons, and that is deliberate. Our booking partner sells no accommodation in Cuba at all, so there is nothing here for us to sell and nothing here for us to earn. This is a guide to where to stay rather than a shop. Book a casa directly with the household, or through an agency that specialises in Cuba.

American readers — this page needs a harder warning than most. U.S. law does not permit tourism to Cuba; travel must fall within an OFAC licence category, and the widely used Support for the Cuban People category expects you to stay in privately owned casas particulares and spend with private businesses. There are no casas particulares on these cays. There is no town, no private street and no private hotel — every bed on Cayo Coco and Cayo Guillermo belongs to the state. The State Department’s Cuba Prohibited Accommodations List separately bars U.S. persons from lodging at, paying for lodging at, or booking a listed property, wherever the booking is made. If you are a U.S. person and you want these beaches, the route that works is a casa in Morón and a day on the sand. See the full Cuba chapter, and confirm the current position with OFAC and the State Department.

And the 2026 warning: these cays were among the first places in Cuba to lose hotels when the fuel shortage bit. In July 2026 the prime minister told the National Assembly that around 73 per cent of the country’s hotel capacity was closed, and the international chains that managed the big resorts have all gone — Blue Diamond earlier in the year, then Iberostar, Meliá and Barceló in July 2026, leaving their properties under direct state management and in some cases under new names. Whatever you book, get written confirmation that it is open on your dates.

The cays, and the mainland behind them

There are no neighbourhoods here in the way there are in Havana or Varadero. There are four places you might sleep, and the difference between them is the difference between a resort gate, a different resort gate, and a Cuban street forty minutes away.

Cayo CocoTHE MAIN CAY

The biggest of the cays and the one with the airport: a dozen or so large hotels strung along the north shore, with mangrove, scrub and salt flats behind them.

  • The beaches Long, pale and rarely crowded
  • Hotels All-inclusive, all state-owned
  • The airport On the cay itself
  • A town There isn’t one
  • Closures Several suspended since early 2026
Cayo GuillermoWEST, BY CAUSEWAY

Smaller, lower and quieter, joined to Cayo Coco by a short second causeway. Playa Pilar at its western tip is the beach people come to Cuba for.

  • Playa Pilar Dunes, pale sand, shallow water
  • Quiet More so than Cayo Coco
  • Hemingway Islands in the Stream ends here
  • Anything but beach Nothing at all
Cayo Románo & Cayo Paredón GrandeEAST

The empty end. Románo is mostly wild, and the road east leads to the striped Diego Velázquez lighthouse on Paredón Grande.

  • Emptiness Almost total
  • The lighthouse Worth the drive
  • Somewhere to sleep Little or nothing open
Morón & Ciego de ÁvilaTHE MAINLAND

The real answer for a lot of travellers: a provincial town at the mainland end of the causeway, with casas particulares, private restaurants and ordinary Cuban life.

  • Casas particulares Morón has a proper supply
  • Price A fraction of an all-inclusive
  • For U.S. travellers The route that fits the law
  • The beach Forty minutes and a checkpoint away

What kind of stay suits you

The all-inclusive, if you want the beach and nothing else

That is what these cays are for, and there is no shame in it. You will get sand most places cannot match, warm shallow water, and a week in which nothing is asked of you. Understand what you are buying: a gated compound on an island with no town, in a year when the chains that ran these hotels have left and supply is thin. Read recent reviews rather than the brochure, and confirm the property is open.

A casa in Morón is the Guild’s answer

It costs a fraction of the resort, it puts money into a Cuban household, it is the compliant choice for an American traveller, and it gives you something the cays cannot: a town, a market, a private restaurant and people. The beach is forty minutes away by taxi. You give up the sunset drink on your own balcony and you gain a country.

Do not expect to mix the two

This is the practical trap. From a hotel on Cayo Coco there is nowhere to walk, nothing to buy and no restaurant that is not the hotel’s. A taxi to Morón and back is a long, expensive errand. If you want both the sand and some Cuba, base on the mainland and come out, not the other way round.

Think about how long you want on a sandbar

Three or four nights is the sweet spot. Beyond that most people start looking for a street. If you have ten days on the island and you want Havana, Viñales or Trinidad as well, a short stretch here or a couple of day trips from Morón will do everything a week would.

Getting about, and what is genuinely walkable

On foot

Within a resort, everything; outside one, nothing. The hotels sit along a shore road with mangrove and scrub between them, and there is no promenade, no parade of shops and nowhere to walk to. This is the single biggest difference between these cays and Varadero, and it is the thing most people wish they had been told.

The beach is the exception. The sand runs for miles and walking it at first light, before the loungers go out, is the best hour of the day here.

In Morón, the town is small, flat and entirely walkable, which is another argument for sleeping there.

The causeway, the airport and taxis

The causeway. A stone road about seventeen miles long, carried straight out across the shallow Bahía de Perros from the mainland near Morón. It opened in July 1988 and it is still the only road in. There is a checkpoint at the mainland end. The crossing is flat, bright and strange, and flamingos feed in the shallows beside it — look left and right rather than ahead.

Flying. Jardines del Rey airport (CCC) is on Cayo Coco and has taken international flights since 2005, which is how most package travellers arrive. Cuban schedules have been unsettled through 2026 by the fuel shortage; confirm your flight rather than assume it.

Taxis and buses. A tourist bus shuttles between the hotels and the main beaches, and taxis work the cays and the causeway. Agree the fare before you get in, every time. Self-drive is a gamble in Cuba at the moment for fuel reasons, though the roads out here are empty and in decent order.

Things worth doing

The honest summary is that you came for the beach. But the archipelago has more in it than the loungers suggest, and the mainland behind it has a great deal more.

Playa Pilar

At the western tip of Cayo Guillermo, behind high dunes — pale sand, shallow turquoise water and the open Atlantic round the point. Widely called the best beach in Cuba, and named after Hemingway’s boat. Go early; the day boats come mid-morning.

Flamingos on the Bahía de Perros

Wild flamingos feed in the shallow water either side of the causeway and can often be seen from it, though less reliably than they once were. Early morning and late afternoon are the times. Binoculars earn their place in the bag here.

The reef

A long coral reef lies off the north shore of the cays, close enough in that snorkelling from the beach is worth doing and diving is properly good. Ask what the visibility has been doing before you pay for a boat.

The Diego Velázquez lighthouse

East along the cays on Cayo Paredón Grande stands a tall nineteenth-century lighthouse in black and yellow stripes, with almost nothing around it. It is a long, empty, rather beautiful drive.

Morón and the Laguna de la Leche

The town at the mainland end, with its cockerel statue, its railway and its ordinary market, and the big milky-white lagoon just north of it. Half a day, and the part of this trip you will actually remember.

Hemingway’s water

Cayo Guillermo and Cayo Coco are where the last chapters of Islands in the Stream are set, and the same waters sit behind The Old Man and the Sea. Worth reading the first on the plane.

Practicalities

Power, water and the everyday

Power. The tourist cays sit on the priority grid because they earn foreign currency, and the large hotels run generators — but 2026 has brought repeated national grid collapses, and the mainland towns behind the causeway have had it far worse than the resorts. A casa in Morón will see the blackouts the hotels are shielded from. Bring a torch and a power bank either way.

Water. Bottled or filtered for drinking, always. There is no fresh water on the cays themselves; everything is piped across the causeway, which is worth knowing when you are asked not to waste it.

Sun, sea and mosquitoes. There is almost no natural shade on the sand and the sea breeze hides the burn. The mangrove behind the beaches means mosquitoes and, at dusk, sandflies — repellent is not optional here in the way it is in Havana.

Money, food and the booking question

Money. Cash. Since 6 June 2026 Visa and Mastercard have not been processed anywhere in Cuba, for any cardholder of any nationality. Arrive with everything you need for the whole trip, in good condition, and remember there is nowhere on the cays to solve a shortfall.

Eating. Inside a resort you eat what the resort serves, and in a thin supply year that is a real variable. In Morón the private paladares are the good food, and they cost very little. If food matters to you, that is another vote for the mainland.

Confirming a hotel. This is the job on these cays. Properties here were among the first closed to save fuel, and the chains that ran them have gone. Before you pay, get written confirmation that the hotel is open on your dates — and, if you are a U.S. person, check the current Cuba Prohibited Accommodations List, which applies wherever in the world you make the booking.

Cayo Coco — FAQ

Can you stay in a casa particular on Cayo Coco?

No. There is no town on Cayo Coco or Cayo Guillermo and there are no private guest houses on the cays. Every bed out there belongs to a state hotel, nearly all of them all-inclusive and nearly all of them built since the causeway opened. The casas particulares are on the mainland, in Moron about forty minutes away and in Ciego de Avila about an hour and a half, and that is the only way to combine these beaches with a private, Cuban-owned stay.

Can Americans stay on Cayo Coco?

This is the hardest place in Cuba to answer yes to. U.S. law does not permit tourism to Cuba, so travel has to fall within an OFAC licence category, and the most-used one, Support for the Cuban People, expects you to stay in privately owned lodging and spend with private businesses. On these cays there is no private lodging at all. Separately, the State Department's Cuba Prohibited Accommodations List bars a U.S. person from lodging at, paying for lodging at, or booking a listed property, wherever in the world the booking is made. The honest answer is to sleep in a casa in Moron and come out to the beach for the day. Confirm the current lists with OFAC and the State Department before you commit money.

How do you actually get to Cayo Coco?

Two ways. The causeway, a stone road about seventeen miles long built out across the Bahia de Perros and open since July 1988, carries every car, coach and taxi that comes from the mainland; there is a checkpoint at the mainland end. Or you fly into Jardines del Rey airport (CCC), which sits on Cayo Coco itself and has taken international flights since 2005 - though Cuban flight schedules have been unsettled through 2026 by the fuel shortage, so confirm rather than assume.

Is Playa Pilar worth the trip?

Yes, and it is the one excursion we would argue for. Playa Pilar is at the western tip of Cayo Guillermo, reached by a second short causeway from Cayo Coco, and it is widely held to be the finest beach in Cuba: pale sand against high dunes, shallow turquoise water, and the Atlantic on the other side of the point. It is named after Hemingway's boat, and Cayo Guillermo is where the last chapters of Islands in the Stream are set. Go early; the day boats arrive mid-morning.

How many hotels on the cays are open in 2026?

Fewer than the brochures suggest, and the number moves month to month. Hotels on Cayo Coco and Cayo Guillermo were among the first suspended when the fuel shortage bit at the start of 2026, and in July 2026 Cuba's prime minister told the National Assembly that about 73 per cent of the country's hotel capacity was closed. The international chains have gone too: Blue Diamond wound up earlier in the year, and Iberostar, Melia and Barcelo all ended their Cuban operations in July 2026, so the properties they ran are now under direct state management and some have been renamed. We are not going to print a count that will be wrong next month. Get written confirmation that your hotel is open on your dates.

Cayo Coco or Varadero?

Varadero if you want to be able to walk out of your room into a Cuban town, eat in a private restaurant and get to Havana in two hours. Cayo Coco if you want emptier, finer beaches and do not mind that there is nothing else at all. The sand is better here; the trip is narrower. For an American traveller Varadero is also far easier to square with the law, because it has a town full of casas particulares and these cays have none.
