Sweden

Sweden Travel Guide

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Sweden is the largest of the Nordic countries and the one that asks least of you. Ten and a half million people live in a country that runs a thousand miles from the beech woods of Skåne to the reindeer pasture above the Arctic Circle, and nine in ten of them live in the southern third of it — which is why the south feels like a settled, comfortable, well-connected corner of Europe and the north feels like somewhere else entirely. That split explains most of what follows: why the train beats the car, why you should pick one end of the country rather than both, and why the light matters here more than the weather.

Why visit Sweden

The largest of the Nordic countries, and the one that is easiest to travel in without planning your life around it.

Water everywhere, and you are allowed on it

Nearly a hundred thousand lakes, a Baltic coast broken into tens of thousands of islands, and a right of public access that lets you walk, swim, paddle and camp on almost all of it. Few countries hand over their landscape so freely.

Two countries in one, north and south

The south is farm country, beech woods and old university towns an hour apart. Four hundred miles north of Stockholm the map empties out into forest, reindeer, mines and mountains. They are different holidays and most trips sensibly take one.

It works

Trains run on time, the apps are in English, the pavements are swept and nobody expects you to haggle. Sweden is the country to choose if you want the far north without the expedition.

The light is the attraction

North of the Arctic Circle the sun does not set for weeks in summer and does not rise for weeks in winter. Even in Stockholm, a June evening stays blue until well past eleven, and a January afternoon is finished by three.

Sweden at a glance

Capital

Stockholm, built across fourteen islands where Lake Mälaren meets the Baltic, and home with its suburbs to about a quarter of the country.

Money

The Swedish krona (SEK, written kr). Sweden is in the European Union but kept its own currency — euros are not accepted in shops.

Language

Swedish. English is spoken fluently almost universally, and signage in the cities is routinely bilingual.

Borders

In the European Union and in Schengen. Land borders with Norway and Finland, and a bridge and tunnel to Denmark from Malmö.

Getting about

Fast trains down the spine of the country, night trains to the Arctic, local boats through the archipelagos, and short flights north. Little of it needs a car.

Shape of it

Long. About a thousand miles top to bottom, and the sleeper from Stockholm to Kiruna takes the better part of a day and a night. Plan fewer places than you want to.

The regions

Five Swedens, and most trips sensibly take one or two of them.

Stockholm & the archipelago

The capital on its fourteen islands, the thirty thousand islands of the archipelago beyond it, and the old church towns of the Mälaren valley behind. The easiest arrival and the fullest week.

Things to do in Sweden

The Vasa

A warship that capsized in Stockholm harbour on her maiden voyage in 1628, raised almost whole in 1961 and now standing under one roof with her rigging and her carvings. The most extraordinary single object in Scandinavia.

A day in the archipelago

Commuter boats leave central Stockholm for the islands all summer and cost very little. Two hours out and two back tells you more about how Swedes actually spend a summer than any museum.

The night train north

Board in Stockholm in the evening, wake up in the forest, and step off above the Arctic Circle. It is the cheapest sleeping and the best window in the country.

Abisko for the aurora

The valley west of Kiruna sits in a rain shadow that keeps the sky clear when everywhere else is clouded over, which is why aurora watchers go there rather than anywhere prettier.

Visby’s walls

A complete medieval town wall around a Hanseatic port, on a UNESCO World Heritage island, with ruined churches inside it and sea stacks up the coast.

Fika, properly

Coffee and something sweet, sitting down, with other people, as a fixed part of the day rather than a snack. It is not a tourist ritual and you should join in.

Best time to visit & getting there

Sweden has two good seasons and they are nothing like each other. Summer, from the middle of June through August, is when the country is fully itself: the archipelago boats run, the lakes are warm enough to swim in, and north of the Arctic Circle the sun does not set at all. It is also when Sweden goes on holiday — Midsummer, in the second half of June, shuts shops, restaurants and whole small towns for several days, and July empties the cities as the Swedes head for their summer houses.

The shoulders, late May and the second half of August into September, are to our mind the better choice. The light is still long, the water is at its warmest in late August, the mosquitoes of the north have gone, prices step down and the famous places are calm. The price you pay is that some island boats and northern lodges run a thinner timetable or have shut for the year.

Winter is a real season, not a closed one, and it is two different things depending on latitude. In the south it means short grey days, Christmas markets and skating. In Lapland it means the polar night, the aurora, dog sleds, snowmobiles and ice hotels — and a genuine need for proper clothing, which the lodges will lend you. Our month-by-month guide to the best time to visit Sweden goes through it season by season.

Getting there is straightforward. Stockholm Arlanda takes nonstop flights from North America and from across Europe, and the express train into the city takes under twenty minutes. Gothenburg and Malmö are better reached through Copenhagen, which is a thirty-five-minute train ride from Malmö across the Øresund bridge and is often the cheaper gateway to the whole south. For Lapland, fly or take the sleeper to Kiruna or Luleå. If you want both ends of the country, fly into one and out of the other rather than doubling back — airlines will usually price it as an open-jaw without penalty, and it saves you a day.

Sweden uses the Swedish krona (SEK, written kr), not the euro. Sweden is a member of the European Union but kept its own currency, and euros are not accepted in ordinary shops. It is also one of the most nearly cashless countries in the world: cards and phones are taken everywhere, including on buses and at market stalls, and a great many cafés, museums and buses now take no cash at all — so a card is not a convenience here, it is the only way to pay. Wine and spirits, and any beer above a modest strength, are sold only through the state shops, Systembolaget, which keep short hours and are shut on Sundays; weaker beer is in the supermarkets. Tipping is not expected and never chased — rounding up a restaurant bill is plenty. Decline any card machine that offers to charge you in dollars; pay in kronor and let your own bank convert.

Fishing here

Sweden is an angler’s country and an unusually generous one. On the five great lakes — Vänern, Vättern, Mälaren, Hjälmaren and Storsjön — and along the whole of the coast, fishing with hand tackle is free and needs no permit at all, which is close to unique in Europe. Everywhere else you buy a fiskekort, a local fishing card, for the water you are standing in; they are cheap, sold online or in the nearest shop, and the money goes back into the fishery. The headline water is the Mörrum in the south, a salmon river fished and managed for centuries and let by the beat. The archipelagos off Stockholm and Bohuslän have become some of the best pike fly water anywhere, and in Lapland the rivers run to grayling, brown trout and Arctic char. Our nearest guide on the subject is fly fishing in Iceland and Scandinavia, and the whole shelf is in The Guild Fishing Library.

Travelling in your later years

One of the easiest countries in Europe to travel in slowly, with two things worth planning around: the darkness and the ice.

The ground is kind

Level boarding on the fast trains, lifts and ramps as the rule rather than the exception, and accessible lavatories in stations, museums and most hotels built in the last forty years. The Arlanda express into Stockholm is step-free end to end.

The boats do the walking

The archipelago is best seen from a seat on a scheduled boat, with a lavatory and a cup of coffee aboard, and the same is true of the Göta Canal across the country. No walking required for the finest views in Sweden.

Gamla Stan is the exception

Stockholm’s old town is cobbled, narrow and sloping, and some of the older metro stations have stairs rather than lifts. Lovely, and worth a morning; not somewhere to be hurried.

Ice is the real hazard

From December to March pavements freeze and thaw. Swedes wear clip-on studs over their shoes and so should you — they cost very little in any pharmacy and they are the difference between a winter trip and a broken wrist.

Daylight is the thing to plan around

In December Stockholm gets about six hours of light and Kiruna gets none at all. In June the sun barely sets. Neither is a problem if you choose it on purpose, and both are a shock if you do not.

Pharmacies and care are excellent

Standards are high and English is universal among medical staff. Bring your prescriptions in their original packaging with a copy of the prescription, as you would anywhere.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS — the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years — is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today — nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Sweden is the simple case. It is in the European Union and in the Schengen area, so everything above applies here in full, and flying Stockholm to Copenhagen, Helsinki or Oslo is an internal Schengen hop with no passport control. The train over the bridge from Malmö to Copenhagen crosses no border check in the ordinary way, though identity checks have been run on that line from time to time, so carry your passport rather than leaving it in the hotel. Because Sweden is in the EU, customs allowances arriving from another EU country are the generous ones; coming in from Norway they are not.

Sweden travel FAQ

When is the best time to visit Sweden?

It depends which Sweden you want. For the archipelago, the lakes and the long light, late June through August is the season, and north of the Arctic Circle the sun does not set at all. Be warned that Midsummer in the second half of June closes a great deal for several days, and that July is the Swedish holiday month. Late August is our own pick: the sea is at its warmest, the northern mosquitoes have gone and the crowds have thinned. For the northern lights you want the dark half of the year, roughly late September to late March, and you want to be in Lapland.

Is Sweden as expensive as Norway?

No, though it is not cheap. Sweden sits a clear step below Norway on restaurant meals, alcohol and hotels, and a long way below it on alcohol in particular, which is why the ferries and the bridge traffic run as they do. What keeps costs down is that much of the best of Sweden is free: the right of public access, the swimming, the walking, the archipelago boats for the price of a bus ticket. Hotel breakfasts are usually included and substantial, a cafe lunch is good value, and eating one main meal a day out rather than two makes a real difference.

Do I need a car in Sweden?

Usually not. The fast trains link Stockholm, Gothenburg and Malmo in three to five hours, night trains run to the Arctic, and the archipelago and the canal are served by boat. A car earns its keep in Skane, on the west coast and in the far north, where the pleasure is stopping where you like and the buses are sparse. If you do drive in winter, winter tyres are a legal requirement in the cold months and the hire company will fit them.

Does Sweden use the euro?

No. Sweden joined the European Union in 1995 but kept the krona, and a referendum in 2003 went against adopting the euro. Euros are not accepted in ordinary shops. What does surprise visitors is how thoroughly cashless the country has become: a great many cafes, buses and museums take cards only and will politely refuse your notes, so bring a card you can tap and do not bother changing money.

What is the right of public access?

Allemansratten, the right of all men, is a genuine legal right to walk, swim, paddle, pick berries and camp on uncultivated land, including private land, as long as you keep well clear of houses and gardens, light no fires in dry weather, close gates and leave absolutely nothing behind. You may pitch a tent for a night or two in one spot without asking. It is one of the great pleasures of the country and it is taken seriously in both directions: the access is real, and so is the expectation that you behave.

How do I see the archipelago if I only have a day?

Take a scheduled boat rather than a sightseeing cruise. Boats leave from the quays in central Stockholm through the summer for islands an hour or two out; Vaxholm is the easy one, Grinda and Sandhamn are further and quieter. Go out in the morning, have lunch on the island, come back in the evening. It costs a fraction of an excursion and you will be travelling with people going home from work.

Will I see the northern lights?

Possibly, if you go to Lapland in the dark months and give it time. The aurora needs three things at once: darkness, solar activity and a clear sky. Nobody can promise you all three on a given night, and anyone who does is selling something. Give yourself at least three or four nights around Kiruna or Abisko between late September and late March, get away from town lights, and treat a sighting as a gift rather than an itinerary item. The same trip in daylight gives you snow, forest and mountains worth the fare on their own.

How many days do you need?

Five days is enough for Stockholm and a day in the archipelago. A week lets you add Uppsala and either Gothenburg or the south. Ten days gets you Stockholm, the west coast and Skane at a civilised pace, or Stockholm and Lapland, which is a different country again. Sweden is longer than most maps suggest, and the commonest mistake is treating Stockholm and Kiruna as a comfortable day apart when the train alone is the better part of a day and a night.
