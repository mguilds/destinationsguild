Grenada

Grenada Travel Guide

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Grenada is a three-island country at the southern end of the Windward chain — Grenada itself, and Carriacou and Petite Martinique a little to the north-east — about 133 square miles in all and home to something over a hundred thousand people. The main island is a volcanic one: a steep green interior of rainforest and crater lakes, a working capital wrapped round a horseshoe harbour at St George’s, and a long crescent of sand at Grand Anse just south of it. It has been independent since 7 February 1974, it is English-speaking, and it is still the island people mean by the Isle of Spice: nutmeg and mace, cocoa and cinnamon grew the economy and still shape the hillsides. Two storms are part of the story and belong in any honest guide — Hurricane Ivan in 2004, which crossed the main island, and Hurricane Beryl in July 2024, which hit Carriacou and Petite Martinique. Grenada is a place that has rebuilt itself and says so.

Why visit Grenada

Five reasons that hold up after the brochure is closed.

It still smells of spice

Nutmeg, mace, cocoa and cinnamon are not a marketing line here but a crop and a working trade. The estates in the north of the island process cocoa and nutmeg the way they have for generations, and you can walk through the whole process from pod to bar in an afternoon.

Grand Anse

A long, sheltered, gently shelving crescent of pale sand a few minutes south of the capital, with a line of hotels set back behind the trees rather than built over the beach. It is the kind of beach you can swim at every day of a week without getting bored of it.

A capital worth walking

St George’s is built round a horseshoe harbour on a steep hillside, with warehouses in faded ochre and red along the Carenage, a fort above the town and a market that feeds the island rather than the ships. Few Caribbean capitals reward an hour on foot as well.

The interior

Grand Étang National Park sits in the crater of an extinct volcano in the middle of the island, with a lake, rainforest trails and mona monkeys. Annandale and Concord falls are close to the road; the longer walks need a guide and a reasonable pair of legs.

It is not overrun

Grenada has no mass-market strip and no fleet of jet skis. The hotels are small by Caribbean standards, the island closes gently in the evening, and on most days the beach has room on it. That is the whole point of coming here rather than somewhere busier.

Carriacou

Twenty minutes by air and a different pace again: a dry, hilly island of boatbuilders, small beaches and guest houses, where a week passes without a resort in sight. Read our Carriacou page before you book, because Beryl changed what is open.

Grenada at a glance

Where it is

The southern end of the Windward Islands, about twelve degrees north, with Saint Vincent and the Grenadines to the north and Trinidad and Tobago to the south. Three inhabited islands: Grenada, Carriacou and Petite Martinique.

Getting there

Maurice Bishop International Airport (GND) on the Point Salines headland at the south-west tip, minutes from the main hotel coast. Carriacou has its own small airport and a ferry from St George’s.

Language and money

English, with a Grenadian creole spoken alongside it. The Eastern Caribbean dollar, pegged at EC$2.70 to the US dollar. US dollars widely accepted; change often comes back in local money.

Driving

On the left. Visitors need a temporary local permit, which hire companies arrange. Minibuses out of St George’s are cheap and frequent; the Grenada Ports Authority says buses run from the Melville Street terminal on weekdays from 7am to 7pm.

Independence

7 February 1974. Independence Day is the national holiday and the flags go up across the island in the week before it.

Hurricane season

1 June to 30 November. Grenada sits towards the southern edge of the belt, which lowers the odds without removing them — see the question about Ivan and Beryl below.

The three islands, and which part is which

Grenada is small enough to see in a week and varied enough that where you sleep changes the holiday.

St George’s and the Carenage

The capital, the port and the cruise terminal, built up a steep hillside around an almost circular inner harbour. Markets, forts, ferries and the island’s business. Very few visitors sleep here, and almost all of them walk through it.

Grand Anse and Morne Rouge

Immediately south of town: the main beach, the main cluster of hotels, the shopping centres and most of the restaurants a visitor will use. Ten minutes from the capital, fifteen from the airport.

The south coast

Point Salines, True Blue, Lance aux Épines, Prickly Bay and Mount Hartman: a fretted coastline of small bays and peninsulas, yachts, dive shops and small hotels. Greener and quieter than Grand Anse, and a few minutes further from the sand.

The interior and the east

Grand Étang, the falls, the spice and cocoa estates, and the Atlantic coast towns of Grenville and Sauteurs. This is the day-trip half of the island rather than the sleeping half, and it is why hiring a car for a day or two pays.

Carriacou

Thirteen square miles of hills, beaches and boatyards with Hillsborough as its one town. Guest houses and cottages, no resorts, and a pace that takes a day to get used to and a week to leave.

Petite Martinique

Smaller again, a mile or so off Carriacou, about five hundred people, boatbuilding and fishing. There is almost nothing for a visitor to book here, which is exactly what people who come for the day like about it.

Things to do in Grenada

The things most people are glad they did, and one or two they wish they had skipped.

Walk St George’s

The Carenage, the market square, the tunnel under the hill and the fort above the town, in a morning, on foot. Steep in places and worth every step. Go early, before the heat and before the ships.

Grand Étang

The crater lake and the rainforest in the middle of the island, twenty minutes up from the capital on a road of hairpins. Short walks from the visitor centre, longer ones with a guide, and mona monkeys that have learned what a bag of fruit looks like.

A waterfall

Annandale is a few minutes off the main road and the easiest; Concord takes a short walk; the Seven Sisters is a proper muddy hike and the best of the three. Wear something you do not mind ruining.

Spice and chocolate

The nutmeg processing stations and the cocoa estates in the north of the island run tours that are genuinely about the work rather than the gift shop. An afternoon here explains the whole island.

Under the water

Grenada is a diving island: reefs, an unusually large wreck off the capital, and the underwater sculpture park at Molinere, which snorkellers can see as well as divers. Dive shops are concentrated along the south coast.

Take the ferry

The crossing to Carriacou is a real sea passage rather than a harbour hop and can be lively; the small plane takes about twenty minutes. Either way it is the best day out on the itinerary if you have the time for it.

When to go

The dry season runs from about January to May and is the reliable half of the year: sunshine, a steady trade wind, lower humidity and the highest room rates. June to November is hotter and wetter — heavy showers that clear rather than days of grey — and it carries the Atlantic hurricane season, 1 June to 30 November. The two best-value windows are the first half of December, before the Christmas rates start, and late April into May. Spicemas, the island’s carnival, falls in the second week of August: Spicemas Corporation publishes J’Ouvert and Monday Night Mas on Monday 9 August 2027 and Fancy Mas on Tuesday 10 August 2027. Our best time to visit Grenada page goes through it month by month, with the festival dates we could source and the ones nobody has published yet.

Arriving by ship

Grenada is an easy port day and a rare one: the terminal is in the town, not beside it. The Grenada Ports Authority describes the Melville Street Cruise Terminal as being on the western shoreline of the city, with an Immigration and Welcome Centre and a duty-free mall inside, a landscaped public square outside and the market “just a few steps across Melville Street”. It gives the Carenage — the inner harbour, with its warehouses and restaurants — as a ten-minute walk, and says buses leave the Melville Street terminal on weekdays from 7am to 7pm. The Authority puts the depth alongside at 9.8 metres on the North Berth and 10.5 metres on the South Berth, and describes the Caribbean cruise season as running from October to April. With one day ashore, the sensible choices are the town and Grand Anse on foot and by water taxi, or a drive up to Grand Étang and a waterfall. Travelling in your later years

Grenada is kinder than it first looks, as long as you choose your base. The airport is close to the hotels, which spares you the long transfer that tires people out on bigger islands. Grand Anse is the flattest, easiest place to stay: a level beach, a gentle entry into the water, shops and restaurants on one road and a short taxi into town. The south-coast hotels sit on headlands and slopes, so ask about steps, buggies and the walk from the room to the water before you book — our city pages say what each hotel’s own site states. St George’s itself is steep, and the walk up to the fort is a climb; a taxi up and a slow walk down is the civilised way round it. Minibuses are cheap but crowded and quick to pull away; taxis are plentiful and fares are set by zone, so agree the figure before you set off. On Carriacou, roads are rough and medical facilities are limited, which is worth a thought if you travel with a condition that needs watching.

Getting in

For British passport holders the UK Foreign Office states that you can visit Grenada without a visa, for a maximum period of three months granted on entry, and that your passport must have an expiry date at least six months after the date you arrive. It makes a particular point of the stamp: make sure the border officer stamps your passport, because without the stamp you will be considered an illegal visitor. A yellow fever certificate is required if you are coming from a country listed as a transmission risk, and the Foreign Office notes that Grenada has strict rules on what you may bring in. Working or studying, or staying longer than the three months, means meeting the Grenada Immigration Department’s own requirements. We print no arrival form, departure tax or proof-of-funds rule here because that page states none — and travellers on other passports should check their own government’s current advice, which is the only version that counts at the desk.

Fishing here

Grenada is blue-water fishing rather than flats fishing. The shelf falls away quickly off the south and west coasts and the charter boats out of Prickly Bay and True Blue go after blue marlin, sailfish, wahoo, dorado and tuna within a few miles of land; the island’s billfish tournament is a fixture of the local calendar. What Grenada does not have is the broad shallow sand flats that bonefish country is built on — for that you want the Bahamas or the Caribbean’s flats proper, which is the subject of our guide to fishing the flats. Questions travellers ask about Grenada

When is the best time to visit Grenada?

January to May is the dry half of the year: steadier sunshine, lower humidity, a reliable trade wind and the dearest rooms. June to November is hotter and wetter, with heavy afternoon showers rather than all-day rain, and it is the Atlantic hurricane season, which runs from 1 June to 30 November. Our own picks are the two edges of the dry season - the first half of December, and late April into May - when the weather is good and a room costs noticeably less than in February. If you want Spicemas, the carnival, you are going in the second week of August and you should book early.

Is Grenada outside the hurricane belt?

No, and anyone who tells you it is has been reading an old brochure. Grenada lies at about twelve degrees north, towards the southern edge of the belt, so in most years the big storms pass well to the north of it. But it is not immune and it has twice been badly hurt: Hurricane Ivan crossed the island in September 2004 and damaged the great majority of its buildings along with the nutmeg trees, and Hurricane Beryl hit Carriacou and Petite Martinique on the first of July 2024. The Grenada Tourism Authority still carries a notice saying those two islands were affected by Beryl and have started welcoming visitors once again. If you travel between June and November, take insurance that covers weather disruption and watch the forecast in the week before you fly.

How far is the airport from the hotels?

Closer than on most Caribbean islands, which is one of Grenada's quiet advantages. Maurice Bishop International sits on the Point Salines headland at the south-western tip, and the whole hotel coast is within a short drive of it: Grand Anse is minutes away, and True Blue Bay's own website describes itself as a convenient five-minute drive from the airport. Calabash, out on the Lance aux Epines peninsula, puts the drive at fifteen to twenty minutes on its own FAQ page. St George's town is about twenty minutes. Nothing on the main island is more than an hour from the terminal.

Should I stay at Grand Anse or on the south coast?

Grand Anse is the long sweep of sand south of St George's and is where most first visits are spent: the widest choice of hotels, the easiest swimming, a short taxi into town and the shops and restaurants of the Grand Anse strip. The south coast - Lance aux Epines, True Blue, Prickly Bay - is quieter, greener and more residential, built around small bays and a yachting crowd, with several of the island's best-known small hotels and a free or cheap hop over to Grand Anse when you want the big beach. Pick Grand Anse for sand and convenience; pick the south coast for calm, boats and a sense of being somewhere rather than at a resort.

Is Carriacou worth the trip?

If you have a week or more, yes, and it is a genuinely different holiday rather than more of the same. Carriacou is a small, dry, hilly island of beaches and boatbuilding about twenty minutes by air from Grenada - the Green Roof Inn, on Carriacou, gives that figure on its own site - or a longer ferry crossing that can be rough. There are no large resorts; you stay in guest houses and cottages, eat where the island eats, and the day's entertainment is a beach, a boat or a walk. Bear in mind that Hurricane Beryl struck the island in July 2024 and that recovery has taken time, so ask any property directly what is open before you book.

Do I need to hire a car in Grenada?

Not necessarily. Minibuses run the main routes out of St George's cheaply and often, taxis are everywhere and fares are set by zone rather than by meter, and water taxis cross the harbour. Against that, driving is on the left, the roads are narrow and steep away from the coast, and visitors need a temporary local driving permit, which hire companies arrange when you collect the car. Hiring for two or three days to see the north of the island - the falls, the rum distillery, the chocolate estates - is a sensible middle course. Agree every taxi fare before you set off.

What money do they use?

The Eastern Caribbean dollar, shared with several neighbouring islands and pegged to the US dollar at EC$2.70, so divide a local price by rather less than three. US dollars are accepted in most hotels, restaurants and taxis, though change often comes back in Eastern Caribbean dollars and you will usually do a shade better paying in local money. Cards work in hotels, supermarkets and the larger restaurants; carry cash for minibuses, market stalls and the small bars. Expect a government tax and a service charge to be added to hotel and restaurant bills, so ask what is included when you book.

Is Grenada a good island for a cruise day ashore?

One of the best, because the ship puts you in the middle of the town rather than at an industrial berth miles away. The Grenada Ports Authority describes the Melville Street Cruise Terminal as being on the western shoreline of the city, with a landscaped public square outside it and the market a few steps across Melville Street, and it puts the Carenage - the horseshoe of warehouses and restaurants around the inner harbour - at a ten-minute walk. With one day, the honest choice is either the town and Grand Anse on foot and by water taxi, or a drive into the interior for Grand Etang and a waterfall. Trying to do both is how people end up seeing a minibus window.
