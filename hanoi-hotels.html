Where to Stay in Hanoi

Where to Stay in Hanoi — Best Hotels & Areas

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated August 2026

Hanoi rewards the traveller who chooses their street carefully. The Old Quarter is the thing everyone comes for — thirty-six ancient trade lanes, pho stalls, the night market — but its pavements are narrow, uneven and busy until late. The French Quarter just south is the sensible base for most: wide boulevards, flat kerbs, the grand hotels and the Opera House, with Hoan Kiem Lake five level minutes away. West Lake is the quiet alternative, leafy and spacious, at the cost of a taxi into town. Below are Hanoi’s most enduring hotels by style, each with an honest note and a Book Me link straight to Trip.com.

Choosing Your Neighbourhood

Where you sleep shapes the whole trip. Here is the honest lay of the land.

Old Quarter, French Quarter or West Lake?

If you are steady on your feet and want to be inside the picture, take the Old Quarter — but ask for a room off the street, because Ma May and Hang Bac are lively until midnight. For most visitors, and particularly for anyone who values a flat, wide pavement and a short walk to a taxi, the French Quarter around the Opera House and Trang Tien is the better answer: it is quieter, greener and still within five to ten minutes’ walk of Hoan Kiem Lake and the Old Quarter’s edge. Tay Ho (West Lake), fifteen to thirty minutes out, is where the city’s foreign residents live — lake breezes, good coffee, more room for your money, and the right choice for a longer stay, a family needing space, or anyone travelling with a dog. One practical note: Hanoi’s winter, from December to February, is cooler and greyer than most Americans expect, and an outdoor pool is decorative in those months. If a swim matters in winter, choose a hotel with an indoor one.

Luxury

Hanoi’s top tier is small and unusually distinctive — a choice between colonial history and contemporary theatre, with none of the interchangeable glass towers of other Asian capitals.

Sofitel Legend Metropole Hanoi

$$$$ LuxuryFrench Quarter1901 landmarkBamboo Bar

Opened in 1901 and still the emotional centre of gravity of Hanoi hospitality — a white colonial building with green shutters, ceiling fans and dark wood floors that creak exactly as you hope they will. Ask for the Historical Wing rather than the newer Opera Wing; the rooms are taller and quieter, and the corridors carry photographs of Graham Greene, Charlie Chaplin and Jane Fonda, all genuine former guests. The wartime bomb shelter rediscovered beneath the terrace in 2011 is shown to guests on a short daily tour that is among the most affecting twenty minutes in the city. Le Beaulieu for a proper French lunch, the Bamboo Bar for a gin and tonic under the frangipani, and a shaded, shallow, blissfully unfashionable pool.

Capella Hanoi

$$$$ LuxuryHoan Kiem47 suitesBill Bensley design

Bill Bensley designed this as an imagined 1920s opera house, and every one of the forty-seven suites is dressed as a performer’s dressing room — sheet music, feathers, velvet, theatrical clutter. It ought to be ridiculous and is instead enormously charming. The real luxury is the scale: with fewer than fifty keys the staff know your name by the second morning, and the hush is a genuine relief after the scooters outside. Backstage does a serious Vietnamese tasting menu, and the small underground spa and pool are the place to hide from an August afternoon. Two minutes’ walk from the Opera House.

Boutique

Hanoi’s boutique scene is built on converted shophouses and grand old corners — charm, rooftop bars and doting service, sometimes at the price of small rooms and steep stairs. Confirm lift access before you book.

Hotel de l'Opera Hanoi - MGallery Collection

$$$ BoutiqueFrench QuarterBeside the Opera HouseSatine restaurant

A boutique hotel that commits: jewel colours, opera-costume motifs, velvet drapes and a lobby lit like a stage set, all conceived around the neoclassical Opera House next door. Rooms are generous by Hanoi standards and genuinely well soundproofed, which matters on Trang Tien — ask for a high floor facing the theatre. Satine handles both a very good breakfast and a French-Vietnamese dinner, and the ground-floor bar is a civilised place to end an evening without leaving the building. Hoan Kiem Lake is five flat minutes away, which makes this one of the easiest bases in Hanoi for anyone who would rather not negotiate the Old Quarter’s pavements.

La Siesta Classic Ma May Hanoi Hotel

$$ BoutiqueOld QuarterRooftop barRed Bean restaurant

The La Siesta group has quietly become the most reliable mid-market operator in Hanoi, and the Ma May house is the one to book — sixty-five rooms, dark timber and brass, and a service culture that borders on doting. Staff will carry bags up the tight stairs, arrange your Ha Long transfer and remember how you take your coffee. Red Bean on the ground floor serves modern Vietnamese well enough that outsiders come to eat there, and the rooftop bar looks out toward Long Bien Bridge. Rooms are compact, as converted shophouses are, so pay up a category if space matters, and ask for one off the front.

Budget & Value

Value in Hanoi is extraordinary by American standards — genuine four-star comfort well under a hundred dollars. The one real risk is name confusion, since local groups run several near-identically named houses; book the exact name.

The Oriental Jade Hotel

$$ ValueOld QuarterRooftop poolSkyline bar

Ranked among Hanoi’s best-value hotels for several years running, and it earns it: a full four-star operation with rooftop pool, skyline bar, lift and proper bell staff, at a price that in most Western capitals would buy an airport motel. The rooftop is the draw — a small heated pool and a view over the Old Quarter tile roofs toward the lake, best at about six in the evening. Rooms are contemporary rather than characterful, but they are quiet, well lit and easy to move around in, which is not always true nearby. A sensible pick if you want Old Quarter walkability without Old Quarter compromises.

Family-friendly

Hanoi is not a resort city, so family strength here means practical infrastructure: interconnecting rooms, a usable pool, a lift and somewhere safe for children to move. Both picks sit outside the Old Quarter for exactly that reason.

InterContinental Hanoi Westlake by IHG

$$$ FamilyTay HoPavilions over the waterSunset bar

The layout is the point: guest pavilions stand on stilts over West Lake, joined by wooden walkways, so children have somewhere safe and interesting to roam and grandparents have a lake breeze and a bench at every turn. It is about fifteen minutes by car from the Old Quarter, which is the trade — but on a three-generation trip the calm is worth more than the proximity. The outdoor pool is a real one, the Sunset Bar is among the best places in Hanoi to watch the light go, and the eleventh-century Tran Quoc Pagoda is a short stroll along the causeway. Interconnecting rooms are available.

Lotte Hotel Hanoi

$$$ FamilyBa DinhIndoor poolObservation deck

A tower hotel on the upper floors of the Lotte Center, which sounds impersonal and proves extremely practical with family in tow: a department store, supermarket and food hall sit downstairs in the same building, so nobody has to cross a Hanoi street to buy a bottle of water. The heated indoor pool is a genuine advantage in the damp, cool winter months, when outdoor pools are unusable. The sixty-fifth-floor observation deck and its glass floor is a reliable hit with grandchildren and gives everyone else the clearest sense of the city’s geography they will get. You will taxi to the Old Quarter.

Adults-only

We could not verify a single genuine adults-only hotel in Hanoi, and we will not invent one. Every Hanoi property carrying that label is a hostel or a capsule hotel, where the designation is a dormitory convention rather than a hospitality idea — Vietnam’s adults-only resorts are on the coast. For a quiet, grown-up stay in Hanoi, the low room counts of Capella or the boutique houses above achieve the same end. The live listings are here if you would like to check for yourself.

Pet-friendly

Pet acceptance in Hanoi is essentially an expatriate serviced-apartment matter, concentrated in Tay Ho by West Lake, and published policies are inconsistent enough that written confirmation from the property is essential before you fly. We have named the two we could verify; both are residences rather than conventional hotels, which is precisely why they accept animals.

Somerset West Point Hanoi

$$$ Pet-friendlyTay HoFull kitchensLakefront

A lakefront serviced residence in Tay Ho, the district where Hanoi’s foreign residents live — which explains both the tolerance of animals and the quality of the coffee within walking distance. Apartments come with full kitchens and separate sitting rooms, a real advantage on a longer stay or if dietary needs make restaurants every night unappealing. There is an outdoor pool, a gym, and lake walking paths straight from the door, which is exactly what a dog needs after a long flight. Service is residence-style: less ceremony, more competence. Confirm the pet policy in writing when you book.

Hanoi Hotel FAQ

The questions travellers ask most before booking a room in Hanoi.

What is the best area to stay in Hanoi?

For most visitors, the French Quarter around the Opera House and Trang Tien — wide, flat pavements, the grand hotels, the museums, and Hoan Kiem Lake within five to ten minutes’ walk. Choose the Old Quarter if you want to be inside the bustle and do not mind narrow, uneven footpaths and noise until late, and Tay Ho (West Lake) if you want quiet, space and greenery and are content to taxi into the centre.

Is Hanoi expensive for hotels?

No — it is one of the great bargains in Asia. A clean, well-run three-star with breakfast can be had for well under sixty dollars, a genuine four-star with a rooftop pool for around a hundred, and even the city’s two landmark luxury hotels cost a fraction of their equivalents in Tokyo or Singapore. The sensible strategy is to spend at the upper-value end rather than the true budget floor, where windowless rooms become common.

Are Hanoi hotels good for older travellers?

Many are, with two things to check before booking. First, lift access: the Old Quarter’s converted shophouses often have steep, narrow stairs. Second, which street the room faces, since the central lanes are noisy well past midnight. Hotels in the French Quarter and around West Lake are generally the easiest going, with wider corridors, level entrances and quieter surroundings.

Do any Hanoi hotels have an indoor pool?

Yes, and it is worth seeking one out if you are visiting between December and March, when Hanoi is cool, damp and often grey and outdoor pools go unused. The Lotte Hotel Hanoi and Oakwood Residence Hanoi both have heated indoor pools. In the warmer half of the year, from April to October, an outdoor or rooftop pool is a considerable pleasure.

Does Hanoi have adults-only hotels?

Not in any meaningful sense. The properties that appear under that filter on booking sites are hostels and capsule hotels, where ‘adults only’ is a dormitory rule rather than a style of hospitality. Vietnam’s genuinely adult-oriented resorts are on the coast — around Da Nang, Nha Trang and Phu Quoc. For a peaceful, grown-up stay in the capital, book one of the small, low-room-count boutique hotels instead.
