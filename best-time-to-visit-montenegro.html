Best Time to Visit Montenegro

Best Time to Visit Montenegro

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Montenegro is two countries on one calendar. The coast keeps a Mediterranean year — hot dry summers, a famously wet winter, and a season that switches on and off with the beach restaurants. The mountains in the north keep an alpine one, with deep snow from around December and the best walking in the weeks either side of August. Nearly every mistake people make planning a trip here comes from booking for one of those calendars and travelling in the other. What follows is month by month, then region by region, with the Guild's own picks said plainly.

The short answer

September, then May and June. September gives you a sea that has had all summer to warm up, coastal towns that have emptied of their August crowds, prices well down from the peak, and mountain walking at its most reliable before the autumn weather arrives. May and June are the greener, fresher version of the same bargain, with a cooler sea and snow still lying on the high Durmitor routes into early June. July and August are the full Adriatic summer — hot, crowded, expensive, and genuinely the point if nightlife or a guaranteed beach holiday is what you are after. Avoid late October to March on the coast unless you want rain and shut restaurants, and treat December to March as the mountain season instead.

Montenegro month by month

January and February

The coast is wet and mild, with the Bay of Kotor living up to its reputation as one of the rainiest places in Europe. Most seasonal restaurants and apartments are shut, and the towns belong to the people who live in them. The mountains are in full winter: skiing at Žabljak and Kolašin, deep snow on the plateau and roads over the passes closed. Herceg Novi's mimosa festival in February is the one genuine coastal event of the winter.

March and April

The thin season at both ends of the country. The coast greens up and the rain eases, but the sea is cold, a good deal is still closed and the weather is unsettled. In the mountains the snow is going, which makes the high routes unreliable and the lower walking muddy. Easter brings a brief surge of domestic travel. Cheap, quiet and a gamble.

May

The first month we would recommend without qualification for sightseeing. The coast is warm, green and still inexpensive, the old towns are pleasant to walk in the middle of the day, and the cruise season has not yet reached its weight. The sea is swimmable for the hardy rather than the comfortable. In the north the lower trails open up while snow still lies above about two thousand metres, and the Tara is high and fast, which is the best of the rafting.

June

Arguably the best all-round month. Long days, a sea that has warmed enough for most people, the coast busy but not yet full, and the mountains clear of all but the last patches of snow. Prices rise through the month towards the July step change. If you want one month that does everything the country has to offer, this is it.

July and August

Peak season, and a different holiday. The coast is hot, full and at its most expensive, Budva's nightlife is at full volume, the cruise ships are at their thickest in Kotor and parking is a daily problem. Podgorica is fiercely hot. The compensation is a warm sea, everything open, boats running everywhere, and the mountains at their most comfortable — Žabljak in August is warm by day and fresh at night, and is the sensible place to be if the heat defeats you. Book coastal rooms months ahead.

September

The Guild's pick. The sea is at its warmest of the year, the crowds thin out sharply after the first week or two, prices fall, and the light is better than in high summer. Walking in Durmitor is at its most dependable. The only real caveat is that the very end of the month starts to bring the first unsettled weather, and some seasonal places begin to close.

October

The first half is still lovely on the coast — warm days, a sea that holds its heat, almost no crowds and low prices — and is the last comfortable walking in the mountains before the weather turns. The second half tips into the wet season, and the closures accelerate. A good month for sightseeing and a poor one for a beach holiday booked in advance.

November and December

The rain arrives properly on the coast and most of the resort infrastructure shuts down; Herceg Novi and Kotor keep working, Budva's strip does not. The mountains get their first reliable snow and the ski season starts when it does, which varies year to year. Christmas and the Orthodox New Year bring domestic travel and a brief lift in the towns. Come for the towns and the mountains, not for the sea.

Coast or mountains — they keep different calendars

The Bay of Kotor

Mild, dramatic and very wet in winter — the mountains round the bay wring the rain out of the weather coming off the Adriatic. Summer heat is held by the stone and the walls. May, June, September and early October are the comfortable months, and they are also the months when the cruise crowds in Kotor are manageable. The bay's own microclimate means Herceg Novi at the mouth is reliably sunnier and drier than Kotor at the head of it.

The open coast and the riviera

A straightforward Mediterranean pattern: hot dry summers, wet mild winters, and a sea that is comfortable from about June to late September and warmest in August. This is the part of the country with the sharpest season — full and expensive in July and August, almost shut from November to March. The shoulder months here are the best value in Montenegro.

Podgorica and Lake Skadar

Inland and hot, with no sea breeze: the capital is regularly among the hottest cities in Europe in midsummer and is uncomfortable for sightseeing then. Spring and autumn are the seasons here, and spring in particular for the lake, when the water is high and the birds are in. Winter is mild, grey and wet, and the city carries on as usual.

The Durmitor mountains

A full alpine calendar at fourteen to twenty-five hundred metres. July and August are the walking months and the only ones when everything is reliably open; June and September are quieter and lovelier with some risk of snow on the high routes; December to March is the ski season, with roads over the passes closed and winter tyres a legal requirement. Spring and late autumn are the two periods to avoid — too much snow to walk, not enough to ski.

Festivals, holidays and when the country fills up

The Mimosa Festival, Herceg Novi

Several weeks around February, when the mimosa is in flower — processions, concerts and a great deal of eating, and the one time of year the coast has something going on in the depths of winter. Dates move; check locally before planning a trip around it.

Summer festivals on the coast

Kotor, Budva and Tivat all run theatre, music and carnival events through the summer, and Kotor's traditional summer carnival and its Boka Night, with decorated boats on the bay, are the best known. Programmes are published close to the time rather than far ahead.

Public holidays

Montenegro keeps the usual New Year and Labour Day holidays, its Statehood and Independence Days, and Orthodox religious holidays including Christmas in early January and a movable Easter. Banks and offices close; on the coast in summer, restaurants and shops mostly do not. Orthodox Easter rarely falls on the same weekend as the western one, so if it matters to your plans, look up the year you are travelling rather than assuming.

What this means for booking

Two things fill the coast: the second half of July through the third week of August, and any long weekend attached to a regional public holiday, when visitors arrive in numbers from Serbia and Bosnia. The mountains fill over the Christmas and New Year ski weeks. Outside those windows you can usually book fairly late; inside them, several months ahead is not excessive.

Getting in: Montenegro is outside the EU and outside Schengen

Montenegro uses the euro but is not in the European Union and not in the Schengen area. That has one very useful consequence for a traveller: days spent here do not count against the ninety days in any hundred and eighty that Schengen allows, and the reverse is also true. Citizens of the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, Australia and the European Union do not need a visa for a short holiday, with ninety days in any hundred and eighty the usual allowance — confirm your own nationality’s terms with your own foreign ministry rather than with any website. Your passport should have been issued within the last ten years and be valid for at least three months beyond your departure, and you should make sure it is stamped on the way in and out, because those stamps are the record of your stay.

Every stay must be reported to the police. Montenegro’s own government says that duty falls on the accommodation provider, and hotels do it automatically at check-in; British government advice tells visitors to make sure they are registered within twenty-four hours and warns of a fine if they are not. In a hotel it is handled for you. In a private flat, ask your host to confirm it is done and keep the slip. Crossing to or from Croatia is a Schengen border with full checks and, in high summer, queues — and if you are driving a hire car across it, confirm first that the rental company permits it.

Best time to visit Montenegro — questions

What is the single best month?

September, for most people and most itineraries. The sea has had the whole summer to warm and is at its best of the year, the August crowds have gone home, coastal prices drop a long way, and the mountain walking is at its most reliable. June is the close second and is greener and fresher, with a cooler sea. If you are coming mainly for nightlife or travelling with children tied to school holidays, July and August are the months and the trade-off is crowds and cost.

When is the sea warm enough to swim?

Broadly June to September, warmest in August and still comfortable for most people through September. May and October are swimmable if you do not mind a bracing start. We would rather put it that way than print a water temperature — the Adriatic varies from year to year and a figure written in advance is a guess.

Is it really that rainy in winter?

On the Bay of Kotor, yes. The bay is one of the wettest places in Europe, and the great majority of that rain falls between October and April. It comes in heavy bursts rather than drizzle, so a winter week is not continuous rain, but you should plan a wet-weather itinerary rather than a beach one. The open coast south of Budva is somewhat drier, and the interior drier again.

Can I ski in Montenegro?

Yes, at Savin Kuk and Javorovača above Žabljak and at Kolašin, which has the more developed area. It is small-scale by Alpine standards — a handful of lifts, simple facilities, short and snow-dependent seasons — and priced accordingly. The season usually runs somewhere between December and March depending on the snow, and the resorts publish opening dates close to the time rather than far in advance.

Can I do the coast and the mountains in the same trip?

Yes, and from about late May to early October you can do both comfortably. The thing to plan around is the drive: Žabljak is most of a day from Kotor or Budva, rather less from Podgorica now that the motorway has taken out the worst of the Morača canyon road. Give the north at least two nights rather than trying to see it from the coast and back. Outside that window you are choosing between a wet coast and a snowbound plateau, which is a different kind of holiday rather than a worse one.
