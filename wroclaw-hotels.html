Wroclaw

Where to Stay in Wroclaw

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Wroclaw is the city we most often find ourselves recommending to people who think they have seen Poland. It has one of the three or four finest market squares in central Europe, a dozen islands in the Oder joined by a hundred bridges, a cathedral quarter still lit by gas lamps, and a fraction of Krakow’s crowds and prices. It is also compact: almost everything below is within twenty-five minutes’ walk of the square.

Where to stay in Wroclaw, area by area

Four parts of the city, all close together, and the trade-off each one asks.

The Guild does not name a hotel until an Explorer has confirmed it on the hotel’s own website, so this guide sets out the areas and sends you to the full, current list for each one rather than printing a shortlist we cannot stand behind yet. Named picks will be added to this page as they are checked.

Stare Miasto — the Rynek and the streets around it

Most centralWalk everywhereSome weekend noise

The Rynek is the point of Wroclaw: a huge market square with a block of buildings in the middle of it rather than an open space, dominated by a Gothic town hall of astonishing elaboration, and ringed by tall painted merchant houses. Staying on or just off it puts you within a few minutes of everything — the cathedral quarter, the university with its great baroque hall, the Panorama, the riverside — and in the middle of the eating and drinking. There are more hotels here per square metre than anywhere else in the city, from small townhouse places to international chains, and they are priced well below their Krakow equivalents. The square has bars that run late, so a courtyard room is worth asking for at weekends; and like every Polish old town it is cobbled.

Ostrów Tumski and the islands — the quiet, beautiful side

QuietestPrettiestFew hotels

North-east of the square, across a couple of bridges, the oldest part of Wroclaw sits on islands in the Oder: the cathedral with its two spires and a lift up one of them, the botanical garden, the sand island with its churches, and a scatter of university buildings. A lamplighter still lights the gas lamps here by hand at dusk, which sounds like a tourist invention and is not. It is a fifteen-minute walk from the Rynek and feels a world away — green, hushed, and the best part of the city to be in early in the morning. There are only a handful of places to stay, mostly small guesthouses and church-run houses, so book early; what you get for the trouble is the quietest and loveliest base in Wroclaw.

Nadodrze — north of the river, cheap and on the way up

CheapestLocalTwenty minutes on foot

Nadodrze, immediately north of the Oder, is a district of tall pre-war tenements that came through the war better than most of Wroclaw and then spent fifty years being neglected. In the last fifteen years it has filled up with workshops, galleries, cafes and the kind of small businesses that follow cheap rent, while remaining an ordinary residential quarter rather than a visitor one. It is about twenty minutes’ walk or a short tram ride from the Rynek. Rooms and apartments are the cheapest in the city, the eating is good and unpretentious, and you will see how Wroclaw actually lives. It is plainer than the centre and some streets are still shabby; neither is a reason to avoid it.

Around the main station — arrivals, chains and value

TransportModern roomsTen minutes to the square

Wroclaw Główny is a splendid thing in its own right, a long castellated Victorian station in orange and cream, and the quarter around it holds most of the city’s modern mid-market hotels along with the Galeria Dominikańska shopping centre. It is a flat ten to fifteen minutes’ walk to the Rynek and the terminus for the airport bus. The rooms are newer, larger and better equipped than the old centre can manage, with lifts and air conditioning, and they are usually the cheapest comfortable beds in town. Choose it for an early train, a late arrival, or if cobbles and stairs are a concern. The streets have no charm to speak of.

Getting around

Wroclaw is a walking city with a good tram network behind it. The old town is flat and compact, and the walk from the station to the Rynek to Cathedral Island takes under forty minutes end to end. Trams and buses share one time-based ticket, bought from machines at the stops or on board and validated as you get on; the network is dense and the trams are frequent enough that you rarely wait long.

The airport is about twenty minutes west of the centre, served by a city bus to the main station and by taxis and ride-hailing apps at modest fares. Intercity trains to Krakow, Warsaw, Poznan and Berlin all leave from Wroclaw Główny.

Two Wroclaw particulars. The city is built on islands and the river channels are crossed by a great many small bridges, so the shortest route on a map is not always the shortest on foot — allow a little extra. And look down as you walk: there are several hundred small bronze dwarf figures set into pavements and doorways across the centre, each about the height of a hand, and finding them is a genuinely good way to get children round an old town.

When to come, and what it costs

Wroclaw is an all-year city break and a notably good-value one. May, June and September are the best months: warm, long days, tables out across the Rynek and the river walks at their best. July and August are hot and busy but never Krakow-busy, and you will still get a table. December brings a very good Christmas market to the square, with the town hall as a backdrop. January to March are cold and often grey, and the city is cheap, quiet and perfectly agreeable if you plan around the indoors — the Panorama, the university hall, the churches and some excellent museums.

On price, Wroclaw is consistently cheaper than Krakow and Warsaw for a comparable room and a comparable meal, which is a large part of its appeal. Our month-by-month guide to Poland covers the year, and the Poland travel guide puts Wroclaw alongside the rest of the country.

Poland uses the Polish zloty (PLN, written zł), not the euro. Poland is a member of the European Union but has kept its own currency and has set no date for changing it, so euro notes are not legal tender and the few places that take them give a poor rate. Pay in zloty. Cards and phones are accepted almost everywhere, including on trams, buses and at ticket machines, and you need very little cash — though a small amount is handy for market stalls, church donation boxes and the odd rural bus. Prices in shops include tax. Tipping is modest and not chased: rounding up, or ten per cent for good table service, is plenty. Decline any card machine that offers to charge you in dollars; pay in zloty and let your own bank convert. Beware the standalone currency-exchange booths at the airports, which are much worse than the ones in town or simply drawing cash from a bank machine.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS — the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years — is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today — nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Poland is a straightforward case. It has been in the European Union since 2004 and in the Schengen area since 2007, so everything above applies here in full, and flying in from Berlin, Vienna or Amsterdam involves no passport control at all. What Poland has not joined is the euro, so the currency is the zloty. Note too that Poland’s eastern border — with Belarus and with the Russian enclave at Kaliningrad — is an external Schengen frontier and is not a casual crossing; the crossings into Ukraine are open but busy. None of this affects an ordinary trip to the cities in this guide.

Staying in Wroclaw — questions

Which area is best for a first visit to Wroclaw?

On or just off the Rynek, the great market square. Wroclaw's centre is small and flat, so staying by the square puts the cathedral island, the university, the riverside and the restaurants all within a short walk, and the hotels there cost appreciably less than their equivalents in Krakow. If you want quiet and beauty over convenience, try the handful of small places on Cathedral Island. If you want the lowest price for a modern room with a lift, stay near the main station.

Is Wroclaw cheaper than Krakow?

Yes, and noticeably so. A comparable hotel room, restaurant meal, museum ticket or taxi ride costs less in Wroclaw than in Krakow or Warsaw, and the gap is widest in summer when Krakow prices climb hard and Wroclaw's barely move. It has a market square to rival Krakow's, a cathedral quarter, good museums and far fewer visitors, which together make it the best value city break in Poland and arguably in central Europe.

What are the Wroclaw dwarves?

Several hundred small bronze figures, each about the size of a hand, set into pavements, walls and doorways across the city centre. They began as a nod to the Orange Alternative, a movement that used absurdist street art to needle the communist authorities in the nineteen-eighties, and the city has been adding them ever since. There are maps and apps for finding them, and they are the single best device we know for getting children happily round an old town.

Is Wroclaw walkable?

Very. The old town is flat and takes about twenty minutes to cross, the station is a ten to fifteen minute walk from the square, and Cathedral Island is fifteen minutes the other way. The one thing to allow for is the river: the city sits on a dozen islands and you cross a good many small bridges, so routes wander more than a map suggests. The old centre is cobbled in the usual Polish way, which matters more than any gradient.

How long do you need in Wroclaw?

Two full days covers the Rynek, the town hall, Cathedral Island, the university's baroque hall and the Panorama of the Battle of Raclawice without rushing. Three lets you add the museums, the zoo with its Africarium, and a walk or a boat along the Oder. It also works well as a stop of a night or two between Krakow or Warsaw and Berlin or Prague, all of which are direct trains.

Does Wroclaw get busy in summer?

Busier than it used to be, and still nothing like Krakow. The Rynek fills up on warm July and August evenings and the main sights have queues at midday, but you will get a restaurant table without booking and a hotel room at a sane price, neither of which is reliably true in Krakow in the same weeks. If you want the square to yourself, come in May, September or on a midweek morning at any time of year.
