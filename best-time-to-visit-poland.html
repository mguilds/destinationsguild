Best Time to Visit Poland

Best Time to Visit Poland

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Poland has a continental climate, which is a dry way of saying that its summers and winters are both the real thing and that the pleasant bits in between are short and worth catching. There is no single best month, but there is a clear best pair — late spring and early September — and there are two or three weeks most visitors would be happier avoiding. What follows is the year set out month by month, with crowds, prices and what is actually open treated as seriously as the temperature.

The short answer

Three good answers, depending on what you came for.

For the cities

Late May to June, or September. Long days, warm but not hot, tables out in the squares, and the queues manageable.

For value & quiet

November, or January after the first week. Cold and dark, genuinely cheap, and the museums almost empty.

For snow or sea

February in the Tatras; late August for the Baltic. Both are proper seasons with proper crowds.

Month by month

Weather, crowds, prices and what is actually on. Poland has a continental climate, so the extremes at either end of the year are real.

January

The coldest month, often below freezing all day, with snow likely in the cities and certain in the mountains. Dark by four. After the first week it is the cheapest time of year in Warsaw and Krakow, and the ski season is in full swing.

February

Still properly cold but the light is coming back. The best snow of the year in the Tatras, and the busiest, with Polish school holidays spread across the month. Cities remain quiet and inexpensive.

March

Thaw and mud, grey more often than not, and not Poland’s most flattering month — but the cities are cheap, uncrowded and perfectly pleasant indoors. Skiing usually holds in the high Tatras to the end.

April

Spring arrives, unreliably. Warm days and cold days in the same week, blossom in the parks, and Easter — a major holiday here, with much closed on the Sunday and Monday. Good value either side of it.

May

Lovely, and our joint pick with September. Green, long days, comfortable walking weather. The first days of the month carry two public holidays that often merge into a long weekend, which fills the mountains and the coast; after that it calms down.

June

Warm, long and bright, with the solstice giving Gdansk nearly seventeen hours of light. The school holidays have not started, so the cities are busy but not full. Thunderstorms in the afternoons are common and pass quickly.

July

High summer. Hot, lively and the busiest month on the Baltic and in Krakow, where the main square is very full indeed. Polish families are on holiday, so the coast and the Masurian lakes are booked and dear while Warsaw can feel oddly empty.

August

As July, easing slightly after the middle of the month. The sea is at its warmest and the lakes are at their best. Expect at least one heatwave and check whether your hotel has air conditioning.

September

The best month of the year, to our mind. Warm into the second week, clear light, the sea still swimmable, the crowds gone and prices down. The one caveat is that rain becomes more likely as the month goes on.

October

Autumn proper, and very beautiful in the forests and the Tatra foothills. Cool, often bright, sometimes wet. Prices are low and the cities are at their most liveable. All Saints on 1 November closes things and fills the trains.

November

The month we would skip. Short days, grey skies, cold damp air and little on until the Christmas markets open in the last week. It is, however, the cheapest time to see Warsaw and Krakow.

December

Dark and cold, redeemed entirely by the Christmas markets in every main square from the end of November. Book ahead for the weekends before Christmas. Christmas Day is very quiet and New Year is expensive, particularly in Zakopane.

Which part of Poland, and when

The cities — Krakow, Warsaw, Wroclaw, Poznan — work in any month, and that is their advantage. They are at their most pleasant in late spring and September and at their cheapest in November and January, and the museums that are the real reason to come are indoors and open all year. Krakow is the one place where high summer is genuinely too busy for comfort.

The Baltic coast has a short season. Gdansk itself is a year-round city break, but Sopot, Gdynia and the Hel peninsula really only come alive from late June to early September, and the sea is not warm until August. Out of season the coast is bracing, half-shut and rather wonderful if that appeals.

The mountains run two seasons with a gap between them. Zakopane and the Tatras are for skiing from late December to March and for walking from June to September; May and November are thaw and mud respectively, and the cable cars may be down for maintenance.

The Masurian lakes are a July and August proposition, when the sailing, the swimming and the fishing are all on and the little towns are full. In spring and autumn the water is cold and much of the hire fleet is laid up, though the birdwatching is excellent.

Public holidays worth planning around

Poland keeps its holidays seriously, and several of them close shops, museums and restaurants or fill every train in the country. The ones that catch visitors out are Easter Sunday and Monday, when much of the country shuts; 1 and 3 May, two holidays close enough together that the gap between them is often taken as leave, filling the mountains and the coast; Corpus Christi in late spring, with processions through many town centres; 15 August; All Saints on 1 November, when families travel to tend graves and the cemeteries are lit with candles after dark — a genuinely moving sight and a very bad day to need a train ticket; and 25 and 26 December together with 1 and 6 January.

One quieter thing to know: most Sundays are now trading-free by law, so the larger shops and shopping centres close, though small shops, bakeries, petrol stations, restaurants and museums stay open. A few Sundays before Christmas and at the start of a month are exceptions. It rarely spoils a trip but it does surprise people looking for a supermarket.

Poland uses the Polish zloty (PLN, written zł), not the euro. Poland is a member of the European Union but has kept its own currency and has set no date for changing it, so euro notes are not legal tender and the few places that take them give a poor rate. Pay in zloty. Cards and phones are accepted almost everywhere, including on trams, buses and at ticket machines, and you need very little cash — though a small amount is handy for market stalls, church donation boxes and the odd rural bus. Prices in shops include tax. Tipping is modest and not chased: rounding up, or ten per cent for good table service, is plenty. Decline any card machine that offers to charge you in dollars; pay in zloty and let your own bank convert. Beware the standalone currency-exchange booths at the airports, which are much worse than the ones in town or simply drawing cash from a bank machine.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS — the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years — is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today — nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Poland is a straightforward case. It has been in the European Union since 2004 and in the Schengen area since 2007, so everything above applies here in full, and flying in from Berlin, Vienna or Amsterdam involves no passport control at all. What Poland has not joined is the euro, so the currency is the zloty. Note too that Poland’s eastern border — with Belarus and with the Russian enclave at Kaliningrad — is an external Schengen frontier and is not a casual crossing; the crossings into Ukraine are open but busy. None of this affects an ordinary trip to the cities in this guide.

When to visit Poland — questions

What is the best month to visit Poland?

September, and early September in particular. The weather usually holds warm and clear into the middle of the month, the Baltic is at its warmest of the whole year after a summer of heating up, the summer crowds have gone home and room rates step down from their August peak. If September does not suit, late May and June are nearly as good, with long days and the squares in full use. The one month we would steer a first-time visitor away from is November, which is dark, damp and short on compensations.

How cold does Poland get in winter?

Cold enough to matter. January and February in Warsaw and Krakow typically sit around or a little below freezing by day and well below it at night, and spells of minus ten or lower are entirely normal. The east and the mountains are colder than the coast. Snow is likely rather than certain in the cities and reliable in the Tatras from about late December to March. None of this is a reason to stay away, but it is a reason to bring a proper coat, a hat and boots with grip, because the pavements ice up.

When are the Christmas markets?

From the last week of November until just after New Year in most cities, with the biggest in Krakow's main square, Warsaw's old town, Wroclaw's Rynek and Gdansk. They are genuinely good ones: mulled wine, grilled sheep's cheese, gingerbread from Torun and a lot of woollens. The weeks before Christmas are busy at weekends and quiet midweek. Note that Christmas Day itself is very quiet indeed, with almost everything shut, and that 6 January is also a public holiday.

Is there a ski season?

Yes, and a serious one. Zakopane and the Tatras run from roughly late December to late March, with the most dependable snow in February. It is far cheaper than the Alps and correspondingly busier with Polish families, especially at New Year and in the February school weeks. The skiing is good rather than vast, and the mountains are beautiful. There is also smaller-scale skiing in the Beskids and the Karkonosze.

When is the Baltic coast warm enough to swim?

July and August, and the first half of September. The Baltic is shallow and brackish and it takes the whole summer to warm up, so it is at its most swimmable in late August and early September rather than in June, when it is still cold. Sopot, Gdynia and the Hel peninsula are the busiest stretches, and the whole coast is booked out by Polish holidaymakers in those two months, so reserve early if you want the sea in high summer.

Which months are cheapest?

November, and January after the first week. Those are the two genuinely low months, when city hotels discount hard and you will have the museums largely to yourself. March and early April are also good value. The expensive weeks are Christmas and New Year, Easter, the first days of May when two public holidays often join into a long break, and all of July and August on the coast and in Krakow.

Does Poland get very hot in summer?

It can. July and August days of thirty degrees Celsius, in the high eighties Fahrenheit, are common now, and heatwaves pushing higher happen most summers. The cities are the worst of it, and older hotels in the old centres often have no air conditioning, which is worth checking before you book a top-floor room in August. It cools off properly at night, and the lakes and the coast are the obvious escape.
