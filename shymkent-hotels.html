Where to Stay in Shymkent — the Centre, the Parks, the Bazaar Side

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Shymkent is the third city of Kazakhstan and the warm end of it: about a million people in the south, close to the Uzbek border, in country that has been settled far longer than the steppe to the north. Since 2018 it has been a city of republican significance, counted alongside Almaty and Astana rather than inside a region. Most visitors come for what it opens onto — Turkistan and the mausoleum of Khoja Ahmed Yasawi, the ruins at Otrar, the western Tian Shan at Aksu-Zhabagly — but the city itself is a pleasant, green, low-rise place to spend a day, and the food in the south is the best argument for stopping.

The centre along Tauke Khan Avenue

Tauke Khan Avenue runs through the middle of Shymkent, and the blocks around it and Ordabasy Square are the practical place to stay. This is where the restaurants, the banks, the pedestrian stretch and most of the hotel rooms are, and where a taxi to anywhere else in the city is a short and cheap one. Nothing in central Shymkent is far from anything else. If you are stopping for a night on the way to Turkistan, stop here and keep the logistics simple.

The parks and the newer north

North and east of the centre the city is newer and more spread out, built around Independence Park and the wide boulevards that run out toward the edges. It is quieter than the middle, the parks are genuinely good — Shymkent takes its green space seriously, which you notice in a southern summer — and rooms tend to be newer. The trade is that you will ride into the centre for dinner rather than walk.

The old town and the bazaar side

West of the centre is the older quarter and the big bazaar, which is the part of Shymkent that feels closest to the Silk Road cities over the border. It is busy, cheap and the best eating in the city, and it is where you go in the morning rather than where most visitors sleep. Rooms on this side are plainer and less expensive. Take them if you want the bazaar at walking distance and do not mind the noise that comes with it.

Shymkent questions, answered

Is Shymkent worth a stop in its own right?

For a day, yes — and for most travellers that is exactly what it is. The centre is green and walkable, the bazaar is the liveliest in the south, and the food is better and more varied than in the northern cities. But the strong reasons to be here are within reach rather than in town: Turkistan, Otrar, and the mountains at Aksu-Zhabagly.

How do you get from Shymkent to Turkistan?

By road or by rail, northwest, in a few hours either way; plenty of visitors do it as a long day trip from Shymkent and come back the same night. The mausoleum of Khoja Ahmed Yasawi is the reason to go — left unfinished when Timur’s masons stopped work in 1405, and on the UNESCO World Heritage list since 2003. We are not printing train times here, because they change with the season and you should have the real ones from the railway’s own site.

Where is the best area to stay in Shymkent?

The centre, around Tauke Khan Avenue and Ordabasy Square, for a first or a short stay: everything practical is there and taxis across town are quick and cheap. The newer north near Independence Park is quieter with newer rooms; the old town by the bazaar is cheaper and livelier. The city is small enough that none of these is a wrong answer.

When is it too hot for Shymkent?

High summer is genuinely hot here — this is the warm south, not the steppe — and July and August can be hard going in the middle of the day. Spring and autumn are the comfortable seasons, and they are also when the hills around Aksu-Zhabagly are at their best. Winters are mild by Kazakh standards.
