Antwerp

Where to Stay in Antwerp

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Antwerp is the biggest city in Flanders and the least preserved-in-aspic of the Belgian cities, which is rather the point of it. It was one of the richest ports in sixteenth-century Europe, it is still one of the largest ports on the continent, and it has spent the last thirty years turning that money and that confidence into fashion, design and a serious restaurant scene. The result is a city with a cathedral, a Rubens house and a medieval guild square at its centre, a nineteenth-century railway station that people travel to look at, and whole districts of galleries and shops that have nothing to do with sightseeing at all. It is bigger than Bruges and Ghent and feels it.

The historic centre — Grote Markt and the cathedral

Most centralOn footBusiest by day

The Grote Markt with its guild houses and the Cathedral of Our Lady beside it are the old heart, and the Rubens altarpieces inside the cathedral are the reason many people come to Antwerp at all. The streets around are narrow, shopping-heavy and busy in daylight, and you can walk from here to the river, to the Rubens House and to the station end in well under half an hour. Hotels run from converted merchant houses to modern mid-range places on the side streets. It is the easiest base for a short first visit; it is also where the crowds and the pricing concentrate.

Het Eilandje and the MAS — the old docks

WatersideNewest quarterTen minutes out

North of the centre, the first of the old dock basins has been rebuilt into a quarter of warehouses, apartments and waterfront restaurants, with the red sandstone MAS museum standing over it. The free escalator to the MAS roof gives the best view in the city and costs nothing. Hotels here are mostly newer and more spacious than in the old centre, prices are usually a little lower, and the evenings are calm. It is a ten to fifteen minute walk or a short tram ride to the Grote Markt, which is the trade you are making.

Het Zuid — museums, galleries and the eating

Where locals eatGalleriesQuiet streets

The south of the city is where a good many residents would send you. Het Zuid is wide nineteenth-century streets, the Museum of Fine Arts, a cluster of contemporary galleries and photography collections, and the restaurants, bars and Sunday market that go with a neighbourhood people actually live in. It is about twenty minutes on foot from the cathedral or a few minutes by tram. For a second visit, or for anybody who would rather eat well than sleep next to the belfry, this is the part of Antwerp we would choose.

Around Central Station and the diamond quarter

Easiest arrivalBest valuePlainest streets

Antwerp Central is worth seeing whether or not you sleep near it — a domed, marbled, four-level station that is routinely called one of the handsomest in the world. The diamond trade works the few blocks beside it, the zoo is next door, and the main shopping street runs from here down to the cathedral in about fifteen minutes on foot. Hotels around the station are the best value in Antwerp and the most convenient if you are arriving from Brussels or Amsterdam with luggage. The immediate streets are workaday rather than pretty and quiet at night.

Getting around

Antwerp is bigger than the other Flemish cities but the parts a visitor wants are still a walkable band about three kilometres long, from Het Eilandje in the north to Het Zuid in the south. De Lijn runs trams and buses, including a set of underground tram stops through the middle that function as a small metro; a contactless card or a ticket from the machine covers it. Antwerp Central has fast trains to Brussels in about forty-five minutes and direct services to Amsterdam and Rotterdam. The city is flat and heavily cycled, and the pedestrian tunnel under the Scheldt, with its wooden escalators from the 1930s, is a free and slightly eerie way to reach the far bank for the classic view back at the skyline.

When to come, and what it costs

May, June and September are the best months, with long evenings and the terraces out along the river and in Het Zuid. July and August are warm, a little quieter than Brussels, and the time of several street festivals. Winter is grey and wet but Antwerp carries it better than the small towns, because so much of what it offers — the museums, the fashion shops, the restaurants and the cafes — is indoors anyway, and December brings the Christmas market along the Grote Markt and the Scheldt. Hotel prices follow the business week more than in Bruges, so weekends are often the cheaper booking, and the exception is any week with a trade fair, when the whole city fills.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS — the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years — is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today — nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Belgium is about as straightforward as Europe gets. It is a founding member of both the European Union and the Schengen area, it uses the euro, and its land borders with the Netherlands, Germany, Luxembourg and France are internal Schengen crossings that you will cross on a train or a motorway without noticing. Arriving by air from another Schengen country means no passport control at all. The one border that is still a proper frontier is the Channel: the Eurostar to and from London is checked at the station before you board, so allow the time the operator asks for rather than turning up as you would for a domestic train.

Staying in Antwerp — questions

Antwerp or Bruges, if I only have time for one?

They are not alternatives so much as opposites. Bruges is small, medieval, extremely pretty and heavily visited, and you see it on foot in a day. Antwerp is a working city five times the size, with better eating, better shopping, more and larger museums and no particular interest in looking like a postcard. Choose Bruges for the picture and the quiet evenings; choose Antwerp if you would rather spend two days in galleries, restaurants and shops with a cathedral in the middle of it.

Is the diamond district something you can visit?

Not really, and it is worth saying so plainly. The trade works out of secure offices and bourses in a few streets beside Central Station, and it is not an attraction with a door you walk through. There are shops selling diamonds, and the DIVA museum nearby tells the story of the trade properly, but the district itself is offices. If you are thinking of buying, that is a serious transaction and not a souvenir; take advice, insist on certification, and do not buy from anyone who approaches you on the street.

How long does Antwerp need?

Two days is the honest answer for a first visit: one for the cathedral, the Grote Markt, the Rubens House and the riverside, and one for the Museum of Fine Arts and Het Zuid or the MAS and the docks. A single day will give you the centre and little else. Three or four days is not excessive if you are shopping, eating or working through the museums, which is how a lot of people use the city.

Is it easy as a day trip from Brussels?

Yes. Trains run several times an hour and take about forty-five minutes, and Antwerp Central is a fifteen-minute walk from the cathedral, so a day trip works well. The argument for staying instead is the evening: Antwerp has far more going on after dark than its size suggests, and the restaurants in Het Zuid and around the centre are a real part of what the city is for.

What language is spoken in Antwerp?

Dutch. Antwerp is in Flanders and everything is signed in Dutch, including the city’s own name, Antwerpen, on departure boards. English is spoken easily in hotels, restaurants, museums and shops, and French rather less willingly than you might expect given that the capital is forty-five minutes away. Open in English rather than French and you will have an easier time of it.
