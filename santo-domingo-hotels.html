Where to Stay in Santo Domingo

Where to Stay in Santo Domingo — Best Hotels & Areas

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated August 2026

Santo Domingo is the oldest continuously inhabited European city in the Americas, founded in 1498, and its Zona Colonial has been a UNESCO World Heritage site since 1990. The first cathedral in the New World is here, and the first paved street, and the palace of Christopher Columbus’s son. For a traveller who wants history rather than a wristband, it is the most rewarding few days in the Dominican Republic.

Where you sleep decides what kind of trip you have. The Zona Colonial is flat, compact — barely a square kilometre — and by a wide margin the best base for anyone who would rather walk than be driven. The Malecón, the long seafront boulevard, offers big hotels with sea views and a wide, level promenade. Piantini and Naco are the modern business districts: excellent restaurants and shopping, four miles from the history.

Santo Domingo is a working capital of some three million people, not a resort, and we have written the practical sections below accordingly — including where the cobblestones and the stairs will catch you out, and where a taxi is simply the sensible choice.

The Practical Side of Santo Domingo

Where to walk, where to take a car, and what the old town will ask of your knees.

Where you can genuinely walk

Zona Colonial: yesBetween districts: no

The Zona Colonial is flat and only about a square kilometre, and Calle El Conde is a pedestrianised spine running through the middle of it. This is the one part of Santo Domingo where a visitor can happily leave the car behind for two or three days. The Malecón is a long, level seafront promenade — a fine walk in daylight from the Sheraton, Catalonia and Jaragua cluster. Piantini has decent pavements by local standards and a dense knot of restaurants and banks, but it is a business grid rather than a stroll.

What is not walkable: the distances between districts. Piantini to the Zona Colonial is about four miles — always a taxi. Gazcue away from the seafront has broken pavements, high kerbs, open drainage gaps and cars parked across the footway. Across the city, pavements are narrow and interrupted, crossings are unsignalled, and drivers do not reliably give way. Take that seriously if walking is at all difficult for you.

The old town’s surfaces. The historic streets are cobbled or laid with irregular stone, the kerbs are high, and the great sights are five-hundred-year-old buildings without lifts. Flat rubber-soled shoes, mornings before the heat, and a taxi to the far end of the zone so that you walk back rather than both ways — that is how to enjoy it.

Getting around, and staying safe

Uber works wellDon’t rent a car

Do not rent a car for a Santo Domingo stay. The Dominican Republic has one of the highest road-fatality rates in the world, and official guidance for visitors is to hire a professional driver rather than drive themselves. Add aggressive traffic, near-zero parking in the old town and unpredictable motorbikes, and it is simply not worth it. If you are heading on to Samaná or the north coast, collect a car on the way out of the city, not on arrival.

Uber operates throughout Santo Domingo, including pickups at Las Américas airport, and is the simplest option for American visitors. Otherwise ask the hotel to call a car rather than hailing one on the street. Official advice is to avoid unregulated taxis and the guaguas — the public minibuses — and we would add the motoconcho motorbike taxis to that list without hesitation.

Sensible precautions, not fear. Keep phones and jewellery out of sight on the street; motorcycle snatch-and-grab is the commonest problem. Use the hotel safe and carry a photocopy of your passport. Withdraw cash inside a bank branch or hotel lobby in daylight. Walk the Zona Colonial freely by day, and take a car after dark — including for the short hop between the old town and the Malecón.

The Zona Colonial’s great sights — and how hard each one is

UNESCO since 1990Access notes

Catedral Primada de América — the first cathedral built in the Americas, begun in the sixteenth century. Level entry from Parque Colón and a largely flat stone floor inside; comfortably the easiest of the major sights.

Alcázar de Colón — the palace of Diego Colón, Christopher Columbus’s son, first built in 1510 and now a museum of period rooms. The approach across Plaza España is open cobble and the upper floors are reached by stone stairs only.

Calle Las Damas — laid out in 1502 and the oldest paved street in the Americas, a short and atmospheric run past colonial mansions. The original paving is uneven and slippery after rain.

Fortaleza Ozama — the oldest formal European military fortification in the Americas, above the river. The grounds are level and easy; the keep is reached by narrow, steep, unrailed spiral stairs and is not for anyone unsteady.

Museo de las Casas Reales and the ruined Monasterio de San Francisco reward a visit too — the first has stairs between floors, the second is an open-air ruin on uneven ground that hosts wonderful evening music. And when the heat wins, Parque Colón and Calle El Conde are flat, shaded and full of cafés.

Frequently Asked Questions

The questions Guild readers ask most about staying in Santo Domingo.

Which area of Santo Domingo should I stay in?

For a first visit, the Zona Colonial, without hesitation. It is flat, barely a square kilometre, contains almost everything you have come to see, and is the only district where you can comfortably walk everywhere. The Malecón suits travellers who want a sea view and a big hotel with a pool, a short taxi from the history. Piantini and Naco are for business stays and serious restaurants, about four miles out.

Is Santo Domingo safe for visitors?

The US State Department rates the Dominican Republic Level 2 — Exercise Increased Caution, the same level as France, Italy and the United Kingdom, and no part of the country is flagged ‘do not travel’. The Zona Colonial’s tourist core is patrolled and busy by day and is fine to walk. After dark, take a taxi or Uber rather than walk, including short hops. Keep phones and jewellery out of sight on the street, as motorcycle snatch-and-grab is the commonest problem.

Is the Zona Colonial easy to walk if I am less mobile?

Partly. The district is flat and small, and Calle El Conde is pedestrianised and smooth. But the surrounding streets are cobbled or laid with irregular stone, the kerbs are high, and the great buildings are five centuries old with no lifts — the Alcázar’s upper floors and the Fortaleza’s keep are stairs only. Our advice: flat rubber-soled shoes, go in the morning before the heat, and take a taxi to the far end so you walk back one way rather than both.

Should I rent a car in Santo Domingo?

No. The Dominican Republic has one of the highest road-fatality rates in the world and official guidance for visitors is to hire a professional driver rather than drive. Parking in the old town is effectively non-existent, traffic is aggressive and motorbikes are unpredictable. Uber works well across the city including at Las Américas airport, and hotel-called taxis are the reliable fallback. If you are continuing to Samaná or the north coast, pick a car up as you leave the city.

Are there any all-inclusive resorts or beaches in Santo Domingo?

No. Santo Domingo is a working capital city of about three million people, not a resort destination, and it has no resort beach. Many visitors pair two or three nights in the Zona Colonial with a week on the coast — Punta Cana is roughly two and a half hours east, and La Romana and Bayahíbe are about two hours, both on good modern toll roads. Boca Chica and Juan Dolio beaches are within an hour if you want a day by the sea.

How many days do I need in Santo Domingo?

Two full days covers the Zona Colonial comfortably — the cathedral, the Alcázar, Calle Las Damas, the Fortaleza and a museum or two, with time to sit in Parque Colón and eat well. Three days lets you add the Malecón, the modern city and an evening of live merengue or bachata without rushing. Most travellers treat the capital as the cultural bookend to a beach week rather than the whole trip.
