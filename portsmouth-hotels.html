Portsmouth & Cabrits

Where to Stay in Portsmouth & Cabrits

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Portsmouth is Dominica’s second town, set on a wide west-facing bay in the north-west of the island, with a national park on the headland above it and a restored eighteenth-century garrison inside that. It is a working town rather than a resort, and the places to stay are scattered through the hills and along the coast around it — which is why choosing a base here matters more than it does in the capital. This page covers the areas, Cabrits and Fort Shirley, the three hotels we were able to read on their own websites, and the large resort we have deliberately left off.

The areas, and which one suits you

Portsmouth is the island’s second town, on a wide bay in the north-west, with a national park on the headland above it. The places to stay are scattered round it rather than in it — which part you pick changes the holiday a good deal.

The bay front and the town

Portsmouth sits on a broad west-facing bay in the north-west of the island. It is a working town rather than a resort — shops, boats, a seafront — and the hotels proper are in the hills and headlands around it. Good for eating where the town eats and for being somewhere real; not, in itself, a place that will sell you a holiday.

Picard, in the hills above

The slope behind the bay, where the island’s small boutique places are. One hotel’s own site describes itself as perched in the hills of Picard, which tells you both the attraction — the view down over the bay — and the catch, which is that you are up a hill and will want wheels or a taxi.

The Cabrits peninsula

North of the town, and the reason most people have heard of Portsmouth: a national park on a twin-hilled headland with a restored eighteenth-century garrison on it. See the section below — it is the best half-day in this part of the island and it is almost entirely level once you are up there.

Tibay and the coast north

Beyond the park the coast turns wilder and the properties get further apart and more private. The island’s best-known luxury resort gives its address simply as Tibay, Dominica, and describes itself as being on Dominica’s rainforest coast. This is the end of the island for seclusion rather than for convenience.

Under the water

The north-west is dive country. A scuba diving centre opened inside Cabrits National Park in 2018, which tells you where the operators think the water is worth going to. If diving is why you are coming, choose the hotel after you have chosen the operator and asked where they put to sea from.

Getting about

One hotel here states frankly on its own site that a four-wheel drive is the only way to reach it, which is a useful warning about this whole corner of the island. The UK Foreign Office says Dominica’s roads are sometimes narrow and in poor condition with few road signs, that car hire should be arranged before you arrive, and that the main highway from Douglas–Charles Airport is prone to landslides after bad weather with sheer drops where the road has fallen away — and it says plainly not to drive that road at night.

Cabrits National Park and Fort Shirley

This is the set piece of the north, and the history is unusually well documented. Cabrits National Park was established in 1986 and covers 1,313 acres, a little over five square kilometres, on the headland just north of Portsmouth. On it stands Fort Shirley: the British began building it in 1765, on the order of Governor Thomas Shirley, as a garrison to defend the north of Dominica; the French extended it during their occupation of the island from 1778 to 1784; and it was abandoned in 1854. It would be a ruin in the forest today but for one man — Dr Lennox Honychurch began its restoration in 1982, and the result is that several of the buildings are now completely restored while the ruins of the rest lie scattered around the peninsula, which is a more interesting thing to walk through than a single tidy monument. Since the 2010s the park, the town and the island have been building up the tourist infrastructure around it, and a scuba diving centre opened within the park itself in 2018. Allow a half-day, go in the morning before the heat, and treat the walk between the restored buildings and the overgrown ones as the point rather than a detour.

Hotels we could check — and how we checked them

Every hotel named here was read on the hotel’s own website, and we repeat only what the hotel itself states. The biggest resort in this part of the island is missing, and the reason is in the last card — we think you should know what we could not check as well as what we could.

Secret Bay

Tibay27 villasAll-villa, plunge pools

Secret Bay gives its address as Tibay, Dominica, and describes itself as being on Dominica’s rainforest coast. The number is on its own site: twenty-seven private villas, each with a plunge pool, in seven named villa types including the Zabuco, Ti-Fèy and Ylang Ylang villas. It names its restaurant as Zing Zing and its spa as the Gommier Spa, and it calls itself a two MICHELIN Key all-villa Caribbean resort and Dominica’s only Green Globe certified property. We should be clear that the awards and rankings on that site — including a well-known magazine’s number-one Caribbean resort placing — are the hotel’s own claims repeated from its own page, not something the Guild has verified independently. What is not in doubt is the shape of the thing: villas rather than rooms, privacy rather than a lobby, and a price to match. Ask what is included, because at this end of the market the difference between a villa rate and a villa rate with meals is substantial.

Hotel The Champs

The site writes the name as Hotel The Champs, and its own footer as The Champs — Hotel, Restaurant & Pool. The address it gives is Banana Trail, Blanca Heights, Picard, Portsmouth. It calls itself a cosy seven-room boutique hotel and describes itself as perched in the hills of Picard. Seven rooms is the fact that matters most here: this is a small, personal place where the restaurant and the pool are part of the proposition rather than facilities you queue for, and it will be booked out by a single group more easily than a larger hotel. The hill position buys you the view over Prince Rupert Bay and costs you the walk, so ask about transport into town and down to the water when you book.

Manicou River Eco Resort

North coast, above PortsmouthCottages4x4 access only

The site uses two forms of the name — Manicou River on its home page and Manicou River Eco Resort on its property page — and we have used the longer one. It places itself on the north coast of Dominica, with views down over what it calls the small town of Portsmouth below, and across Douglas Bay and Cabrits National Park. The accommodation is described as hand-crafted wooden cottages, including tree-house cottages, set in nine and a half acres of mountainside woodland, with a Bar & Bistro on site. No room count is published, so we have not guessed one. The single most important line on that site is the access: it says a four-wheel drive is the only way to reach the property. Take that literally when you plan, and ask them directly how they get guests up from the airport or the town — this is not a place to arrive at after dark in a small hire car.

Travelling in your later years

Be more careful choosing a hotel here than almost anywhere else on the island, because the difference between the options is not comfort but terrain. The honest summary is this: the places with the best views are up hills, and one of them says on its own site that a four-wheel drive is the only way to reach it. If steps, slopes and unlit drives are a problem, say so when you enquire and ask three specific questions — how far is the room from the car, how many steps are there, and who drives guests to the airport. Cabrits and Fort Shirley, by contrast, are a genuinely good outing for anyone with reasonable mobility: once you are on the headland the restored garrison is largely level walking, and it is shaded and interesting rather than a scramble. On the practical side, the Foreign Office says the government hospital in Roseau can cope with many types of treatment but that serious cases may require emergency evacuation, and Roseau is the length of the island away from here, so take insurance that covers evacuation and keep your medicines with you rather than in a case. For the airport road itself, the Foreign Office’s own advice is the right advice: do not drive it at night.

Questions about staying in Portsmouth

Should I stay in Portsmouth or Roseau?

Roseau if it is your first visit and you want one base for the whole island, because the capital is central and the mountain parks are day trips from it. Portsmouth if you are here for the north specifically - Cabrits, Fort Shirley, the diving off this coast - or if you want the small, private, expensive end of the island's hotels, which is mostly up here. Portsmouth is the island's second town and a working one; the places to stay are in the hills and on the headlands around it rather than on a strip in it. A week split between the two is a better holiday than a week in either.

What is there to do in Portsmouth?

Cabrits National Park is the answer, and it is a good one. The park was established in 1986 and covers 1,313 acres on the headland north of the town, and on it stands Fort Shirley, which the British began in 1765 under Governor Thomas Shirley, the French extended during their occupation from 1778 to 1784, and which was abandoned in 1854. Dr Lennox Honychurch began restoring it in 1982; several buildings are now completely restored and the ruins of the others are scattered round the peninsula. Beyond that this is a diving coast - a scuba centre opened inside the park in 2018 - and a base for the north of the island.

Which hotels near Portsmouth can the Guild vouch for?

Three, each read on its own website: Secret Bay at Tibay, which states twenty-seven private villas each with a plunge pool; Hotel The Champs at Picard, which calls itself a cosy seven-room boutique hotel in the hills; and Manicou River Eco Resort on the north coast, which describes hand-crafted wooden cottages in nine and a half acres of mountainside woodland. The large international-brand resort on this coast is not on the page because its own website was not reachable when we wrote it - only a brand directory listing was, and that is not enough to put a hotel in front of a reader.

Do I need a four-wheel drive?

For one of the hotels on this page, yes, and not as a suggestion - Manicou River says on its own site that a four-wheel drive is the only way to reach the property. For the coast generally, read what the UK Foreign Office says before you book a small car: the roads are sometimes narrow and in poor condition, there are few road signs and it is easy to get lost, car hire should be arranged before you arrive rather than on the day, and the main highway from Douglas-Charles Airport is prone to landslides after bad weather with sheer drops where the road has fallen away. It says not to drive that road at night, which is sound advice whatever you are driving.

How far is Portsmouth from the airport?

Douglas-Charles Airport, the one with the jet services, is about an hour from Portsmouth according to the airport's own Wikipedia entry, and that is the only sourced figure we found, so it is the only one we will print. The runway there is 6,352 feet and it now takes mainline jets: United began flying Boeing 737-700s from Newark in February 2025, and American Airlines is scheduled to add seasonal Airbus A319 flights from Miami from 17 December 2026. Canefield Airport, the small one, is at the other end of the island three miles north of Roseau and handles turboprops in daylight hours only.

Is this a good base for a honeymoon or a quiet week?

It is the best part of the island for it, if quiet is genuinely what you want. The north-west is where Dominica's small, private, high-end places are - villas with plunge pools, cottages in woodland, seven-room hotels - and the coast beyond Cabrits gets emptier the further you go. The trade-off is practical rather than romantic: these places are spread out, some are up steep tracks, and you will either hire a vehicle, hire a driver, or stay put. Decide which of those three you are doing before you book, because it changes which hotel is right.

Is there a beach at Portsmouth?

Portsmouth sits on a broad west-facing bay, and there is coast on both sides of the Cabrits headland - Douglas Bay to the north of it is named on one hotel's own site as part of its view. We are going to stop short of describing the sand, the swimming or the shade on any particular beach, because we could not read a source that describes them and this is not an island where you should assume. If a beach day is the point of the trip, ask your hotel directly which beach they send guests to and how you get there, and bear in mind that Dominica's draw is the mountains and the water rather than the shoreline.

Can I visit Cabrits on a cruise day?

Quite possibly, but we are not going to tell you that your ship docks within reach of it, because we could not confirm Dominica's berth arrangements from the authority that runs them - the Dominica Air and Sea Ports Authority's own website refuses automated readers, so we have no sourced description of where cruise ships tie up on this island or what is walkable from them. Your cruise line's own port information is the version that counts, and it is the one to plan from. What we can tell you is what Cabrits is and why it is worth the trip if your day ashore reaches it.
