Finland

Finland Travel Guide

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Finland is a country of water and forest with two quite different trips inside it, and the first thing to settle is which one you have come for. In the south there is Helsinki, a small, calm Baltic capital of islands, trams, markets and saunas, with a lake district of a hundred and eighty-eight thousand lakes behind it and a thousand-island archipelago off the south-west coast. Then there is Lapland, a third of the country and a long way north of the Arctic Circle, where the sun does not set for weeks in June and barely rises at midwinter, and where people go for snow, reindeer, huskies and the chance — never the promise — of the northern lights. The two are a one-hour flight or an overnight train apart, and plenty of visitors do both. This guide covers where to sleep in each, what the seasons really feel like, and the practical things about Finland that surprise people: how cashless it is, how good the trains are, how seriously the sauna is taken, and how much of the country you are simply allowed to walk into.

Why visit Finland

Two countries for the price of one

A Baltic capital of islands and trams in the south, and Arctic wilderness a thousand kilometres north, joined by good trains and cheap domestic flights. Few countries of five and a half million people offer two holidays this different.

The light is the attraction, both ways

North of the Arctic Circle the sun stays up for weeks around midsummer and the nights never come. In winter it is the opposite, and the dark is what makes the aurora visible. Nowhere else in Europe does either so reliably.

You are allowed to walk into it

Everyman’s right — jokamiehenoikeus — lets anyone walk, ski, swim, camp a night and pick berries and mushrooms on almost any land, private land included, as long as you keep clear of houses and gardens and leave no trace.

It is calm, safe and it works

Trains run, tap water is excellent, English is spoken nearly everywhere, queues form without being asked and nobody hurries you. For a traveller who has had enough of crowds and hustle, that is the whole point of the place.

Finland at a glance

Capital

Helsinki, on a peninsula and a scatter of islands at the bottom of the country, with about a third of all Finns living in its wider region. Small enough to cross on foot and by tram in a day.

Money

The euro, since 2002. Finland is one of the most cashless countries anywhere: cards and phones are taken everywhere, including on buses, at market stalls and in forest cafés. Decline any card machine offering to charge you in dollars.

Language

Finnish and Swedish are both official, and the Sámi languages are recognised in the north. English is spoken fluently by almost everyone a visitor deals with. No visitor needs any Finnish.

Borders

In the European Union since 1995, the Schengen area since 2001 and the euro since 1999, with the notes and coins arriving in 2002. Land borders with Sweden and Norway in the north-west; the crossings on the Russian border have been shut to travellers since 2023.

Getting about

Trains are the way to travel between cities — fast and comfortable to Tampere and Turku, overnight to Lapland with a car carriage. Long-distance buses fill the gaps, domestic flights cover the north, and driving is on the right.

Shape of it

Low, flat, forested and wet: about three-quarters trees, a hundred and eighty-eight thousand lakes, tens of thousands of islands, and no mountains to speak of — the high ground is the bare fells of the far north-west.

The regions

Finland is a long country — Helsinki to the top of Lapland is about the distance from Paris to Rome — so the regions are a day apart rather than an hour.

Helsinki and the south coast

The capital and the old wooden towns along the Gulf of Finland either side of it. This is where most visits start and finish, where the ferries to Tallinn and Stockholm leave, and where the country is at its most urban — which in Finland still means a tram ride from open water.

The Lakeland

The middle of the country is more water than land: Saimaa, the biggest lake in Finland and one of the largest in Europe, broken into thousands of channels and islands, with summer cottages down every track and a medieval castle standing in the water at Savonlinna. This is the Finland Finns themselves go to in July.

The south-west and the archipelago

Turku, the old capital, sits at the mouth of a river that opens into one of the largest archipelagos in the world — tens of thousands of islands and skerries running out towards Sweden, with ferries that are part of the public road network and free to use. Åland, out in the middle of it, is Finnish, Swedish-speaking and runs its own affairs.

Lapland

A third of the country, north of the Arctic Circle: bare fells, reindeer herding, Sámi culture, and the winter trade that brings most foreign visitors — snow from roughly November to April, husky and reindeer sledging, ice hotels and glass cabins, and the aurora when the sky is clear. In summer the same ground is for walking, canoeing and salmon.

The west coast and the north

The Gulf of Bothnia side of the country: Oulu, a tech and university city at the top of the gulf where the sea freezes hard enough to walk on; Vaasa and the flat Kvarken coast, which is rising out of the sea and is a World Heritage site for it; and Kuusamo inland, which is where Finns go for rapids, bears and some of the best river fishing in the country.

Things to do in Finland

Take a sauna properly, with Finns in it

There are said to be around three million saunas in a country of five and a half million people, and the sauna is not a spa treatment here but the place where ordinary life happens. The public saunas on the Helsinki waterfront and the old wood-heated ones in Tampere are the easy way in: hot room, cold sea or lake, repeat, and no need to talk.

Suomenlinna, the sea fortress off Helsinki

A chain of fortified islands fifteen minutes out from the Market Square on a ferry that runs on an ordinary city ticket. Built by the Swedes, finished by the Russians, a UNESCO World Heritage site, and now also a place where a few hundred people live. Walk the ramparts, then the tunnels.

Go north for the dark, and look up

The aurora needs three things: latitude, a clear sky and darkness. Lapland from late August to early April gives you all three often enough that a week there has a real chance, which is the most any honest guide can say. Nobody can promise the lights, and any trip that depends on seeing them will disappoint.

Spend a night in a lakeside cabin

The Finnish summer is a cottage, a lake, a rowing boat and a sauna at the water’s edge, and much of the country disappears into one for July. Renting a mökki for two or three nights in the Lakeland is the closest a visitor gets to how Finns actually take a holiday.

Ride the night train to Lapland

The sleeper leaves Helsinki in the evening and arrives in Rovaniemi for breakfast, and you can put a car on it. It is cheaper than a hotel plus a flight, it saves a day at each end, and waking up to snow forest out of the window is a better arrival than any airport.

Walk into a national park and pick your lunch

About forty national parks, marked trails, free wilderness huts in the north, and the legal right to pick berries and mushrooms wherever you walk. Nuuksio is forty minutes from Helsinki; Koli, Oulanka and Pallas-Yllästunturi are worth a journey of their own.

Best time to visit & getting there

There are two good seasons and they are nothing like each other. Late June to mid-August is the summer: long light, warm enough at around twenty degrees Celsius, lakes swimmable, everything open and the whole country outdoors — with mosquitoes inland and in Lapland, and with the week of Midsummer at the end of June when Finns leave the cities and a surprising number of businesses simply shut. Late January to mid-March is the winter: reliable snow in the north, lengthening days, the aurora season still on, and every sledge, ski and ice-fishing hole working. The in-between months are the ones to be careful with. November is dark and often brown rather than white even in Lapland; April and May can be slush and closed cottages in the south while the north is still skiing. There is a fuller month-by-month account on the best time page.

Almost everyone arrives through Helsinki Airport at Vantaa, which is one of the easiest big airports in Europe to use: the Ring Rail trains, lines I and P, run to the central station in about half an hour on an ordinary commuter ticket. Finnair flies non-stop from several North American cities and the list changes with the season, so check current routes rather than assuming; otherwise you will connect through a European hub. For Lapland there are direct winter flights from a few European cities to Rovaniemi, Kittilä and Ivalo, domestic flights from Helsinki all year, and the overnight train. Ferries cross from Tallinn in about two hours and from Stockholm overnight.

Fishing here

Finland is a fishing country in the most literal sense: a hundred and eighty-eight thousand lakes, a long brackish coast and the big salmon rivers of the north. On the lakes the quarry is perch, pike and zander, and in winter the same water is fished through a hole in the ice, which is a national pastime rather than an adventure sport. In Lapland the rivers are the draw — the Tornio on the Swedish border and the Teno on the Norwegian one are among the great Atlantic salmon rivers of Europe, although the Teno in particular has been under heavy restriction in recent seasons, so check what is actually open before you plan a trip around it. The rules are simple in shape: hook-and-line fishing and ice fishing are free under everyman’s rights, while lure fishing needs the national fisheries management fee that most adults must pay, plus the local permit for the water you are standing in. Our nearest guide on the subject is fly fishing in Iceland and Scandinavia, and the whole shelf is in The Guild Fishing Library.

Travelling in your later years

Flat, level and built for everybody

Finland is the easiest country in Europe for anyone unsteady on their feet. The land is flat, Helsinki has almost no hills to speak of, trams and buses kneel to the kerb, trains have level boarding and lifts, and new buildings are built to be used by people who cannot climb. Accessibility here is ordinary rather than exceptional.

Ice is the real hazard, not distance

From November to March pavements can be glazed, and a thaw followed by a freeze is worse than deep snow. Cities grit and plough well and quickly, but studded rubber slip-ons for your shoes cost very little in any supermarket and are what local people of every age wear. Buy them on the first morning.

The dark asks more of you than the cold

In December and January the south has a few hours of low light and Lapland barely clears twilight. Clothing solves the cold; nothing solves the dark except planning around it. Fill the middle of the day, book somewhere with a view of the sky and a warm room to come back to, and take the long evenings as part of the point.

Heat, cold and the sauna

A Finnish sauna is hotter than most spa saunas and the traditional plunge afterwards is genuinely cold water. Both are a strain on the heart, and the Finns themselves say take it gently, sit on the lower bench and leave the ice hole alone if you have any doubt. Ask your hotel about a cooler family sauna hour.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Finland itself is straightforward. It has been in the European Union since 1995, in the Schengen area since 2001 and on the euro since 2002, so an arrival from another Schengen country has no passport control at all and an arrival from outside it is a single check at Helsinki Airport. The land borders with Sweden and Norway in the north are internal Schengen crossings and are often barely marked. The crossings on the Russian border are a different matter: they have been closed to travellers since 2023, there is no passenger train or scheduled bus to Russia, and nobody should plan a trip that depends on one without checking the Finnish Border Guard first.

Finland — questions

How many days do you need in Finland?

For Helsinki and a taste of the country, four or five nights. For Helsinki plus Lapland — which is what most first visits really want — eight to ten, because Lapland deserves three or four nights of its own and the journey between them takes most of a day either way. If you are coming for the lakes in summer, add two or three nights in a cottage rather than another city. The common mistake is a long weekend in Helsinki in December expecting northern lights: the capital is a thousand kilometres too far south for that to be likely.

Will I see the northern lights?

Nobody can promise them, and any tour that does is selling you something it cannot deliver. What you can do is stack the odds: go north of the Arctic Circle, go between late August and early April, stay at least three or four nights, get away from town lights, and accept that cloud decides the matter more often than the aurora does. On those terms a week in Lapland gives you a good chance. Treat the lights as a bonus on top of a trip that is worth making for the snow, the dogs and the quiet.

Is Finland very expensive?

It is not cheap, and alcohol in particular is expensive because it is heavily taxed and spirits and strong wine are sold only in the state Alko shops. Against that, eating well costs less than people expect if you use the weekday lunch buffets the whole country lives on, museums and public transport are reasonable, tap water is excellent and tipping is not expected anywhere. The genuinely expensive parts of a Finnish trip are the Lapland winter weeks and the glass-cabin kind of accommodation, which is priced for the demand it gets.

Do I need to rent a car?

In the south, no. Trains to Tampere and Turku are fast and frequent, Helsinki is best seen on foot and by tram, and parking in the city is difficult and metered. In Lapland and the Lakeland a car changes the trip entirely, because what you want to reach — trailheads, cottages, a dark field away from the streetlights — is not on a bus route. Winter hire cars come on winter tyres as a matter of course; if you have never driven on snow, take the first hour gently and watch for reindeer, which stand in the road and do not move.

What should I actually pack for a Finnish winter?

Layers rather than one heavy coat: thermal base layer, wool mid-layer, windproof outer shell, hat, gloves and a scarf or neck tube, and boots with room for thick socks. Studded slip-ons for ice are the cheap thing that makes the most difference in a city. For a husky or snowmobile day the outfitter lends you a full oversuit and boots, so there is no need to buy Arctic kit for one afternoon. And bring sunglasses — March snow in bright sun is dazzling.

Is English enough, and what about the sauna etiquette?

English is enough everywhere a visitor goes; Finns are usually better at it than they claim. On the sauna: shower first, go in naked unless the sauna is mixed or a hotel asks for swimwear, sit on your own towel, throw water on the stones gently and ask before you add more, and do not feel obliged to stay longer than is comfortable or to make conversation. Silence in a Finnish sauna is not awkwardness. It is the intended effect.
