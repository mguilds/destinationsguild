Why visit Brazil

Few countries offer this much at once — beaches and rainforest, world cities and wild wetlands, and a culture that runs on music.

Beaches without end

Copacabana and Ipanema in Rio, the dunes of the Northeast, the surf coves of Santa Catarina — thousands of miles of Atlantic sand to choose from.

Wild by the continent

The Amazon rainforest, the wildlife-rich Pantanal wetlands and the roaring cataracts of Iguaçu — some of the greatest nature on earth.

A culture of rhythm

Samba and bossa nova, the drums and capoeira of Bahia, Carnival, feijoada and the churrasco grill — Brazil is a country you feel as much as see.

Cities with soul

Rio's mountains-and-sea drama, São Paulo's restaurants and nightlife, Salvador's colonial gold — each a trip in itself.

How the country divides up

Rio de Janeiro & the Southeast

Almost every first trip begins here, and it should. Rio de Janeiro is one of the most beautiful cities on earth — Christ the Redeemer on Corcovado, the cable car up Sugarloaf, Copacabana and Ipanema curving between granite peaks and the sea. Along the coast, chic Búzios and the colonial port of Paraty make easy escapes, while inland in Minas Gerais the baroque hill town of Ouro Preto keeps the gold-rush past in stone.

São Paulo & the South

São Paulo is the continent's biggest city and its engine — a sprawling metropolis with Brazil's finest restaurants, galleries and nightlife. South of it the country cools and greens: the surf island of Florianópolis, wine country, and, on the Argentine and Paraguayan border, the colossal cataracts of Iguaçu reached from Foz do Iguaçu.

Salvador, Bahia & the Northeast coast

The Northeast is where Brazil's Afro-Brazilian soul beats loudest. Salvador, the first colonial capital, layers gilded churches and pastel façades over a culture of drumming, capoeira and candomblé. North and south stretch some of the country's warmest, dreamiest beaches — the reef pools of Recife and Porto de Galinhas, the dunes and buggies around Fortaleza and Jericoacoara.

The Amazon & the Pantanal

For wilderness, go west and north. Manaus, deep in the Amazon, is the launch point for river lodges, the Meeting of the Waters and canoe trips into flooded forest. Further south, the Pantanal wetlands — reached via Cuiabá — offer the best wildlife-watching in South America, from jaguars to giant otters. Belém, at the river's Atlantic mouth, adds a port city with a famous market and cuisine.

Top things to do in Brazil

The experiences worth building a trip around.

Christ the Redeemer & Sugarloaf

Ride the cog train up Corcovado to the outstretched arms of Christ, then the cable car up Sugarloaf for Rio's bay at golden hour.

Rio's beaches

Copacabana and Ipanema at their liveliest — footvolley, caipirinhas, the sunset applause at Arpoador rock.

Iguaçu Falls

Nearly three hundred cascades on the border with Argentina — walkways to the Devil's Throat and boat rides into the spray.

The Amazon by riverboat

From Manaus into flooded forest and quiet tributaries — pink dolphins, night canoe trips and the Meeting of the Waters.

Wildlife in the Pantanal

The world's largest tropical wetland and the surest place in the Americas to see wild jaguars, caimans, capybaras and macaws.

Bahian culture in Salvador

The Pelourinho old town, drum troupes and capoeira circles, and the acarajé and moqueca of one of Brazil's great cuisines.

Best time to visit & getting there

When to go

December–March is high summer in the south and southeast — hot, festive beach season and the time of Rio's Carnival (February or early March), but also the busiest and most expensive.

May–September is the dry season across much of the country: the best window for the Amazon, the peak Pantanal wildlife months, and clear days on the northeastern beaches.

Iguaçu Falls run year-round, at their most powerful after the summer rains. Because Brazil is so large, always match the season to the specific region you are visiting.

Getting there

São Paulo–Guarulhos (GRU) is the main international hub, with Rio de Janeiro–Galeão (GIG) the other big long-haul gateway; Brasília (BSB) also takes some international flights.

From either, cheap domestic flights link everything else — Foz do Iguaçu (IGU), Salvador (SSA), Recife (REC), Fortaleza (FOR), Manaus (MAO) and beyond. Distances are continental, so plan to fly between regions.

The currency is the Brazilian real. Cards and the Pix system work almost everywhere; carry some cash for small towns and beach kiosks. Always check your current visa and passport-validity requirements before booking.

Brazil travel FAQ

When is the best time to visit Brazil?

It depends where you are going, because Brazil straddles the equator and the tropics. Broadly, the dry, sunny months of May to September are the most reliable for the Amazon, the Pantanal wildlife season and the northeastern beaches. December to March is high summer in the south and southeast — hot, lively and the season of Rio's beach life and Carnival (February or early March), but also the busiest and priciest. Iguaçu Falls run year-round, at their fullest after the summer rains.

What currency is used in Brazil?

The Brazilian real (BRL, symbol R$). Cards are very widely accepted — Brazil is close to cashless in cities, and the instant-payment system Pix is everywhere — but carry some cash for small towns, beach kiosks and markets. ATMs are common; use bank machines in daylight, and tell your bank you are travelling to avoid blocks.

Rio, Salvador or Foz do Iguaçu — which should I choose?

Rio de Janeiro is the classic first trip: Christ the Redeemer, Sugarloaf, Copacabana and Ipanema, all in one dramatic bay-and-mountain city. Salvador is the cultural heart of Afro-Brazilian Bahia, with colonial streets, drumming, capoeira and beaches up and down the coast. Foz do Iguaçu is a shorter, focused trip built around one of the world's great waterfalls. Many travellers pair Rio with one other region rather than trying to see the whole country at once.

How many days do you need in Brazil?

Brazil is continental in scale, so give it time. Ten to fourteen days lets you pair Rio with one other region — the Northeast beaches, Iguaçu, or the Amazon — without living on planes. With only a week, base yourself in Rio and add a single short trip such as Iguaçu or Búzios. Internal distances are long, so budget for domestic flights.

Is Brazil safe for travellers?

Millions visit Brazil every year and the main tourist areas are enjoyed safely, but big cities do have petty and opportunistic crime, so travel with normal urban caution. Keep valuables out of sight, use registered taxis or ride apps at night, avoid displaying phones and jewellery on the street and beach, favour well-reviewed operators for tours, and check your government's current travel advisory before you go.
