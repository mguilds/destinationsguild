Malta

Best Time to Visit Malta

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Malta has a longer usable year than almost anywhere else in Europe and a shorter perfect one than people expect. The sea stays warm into November, the winters are mild enough to walk in, and the sites are open all twelve months - but July and August are genuinely punishing on bare limestone, and from November the north of the island and much of Gozo close a good deal of their seasonal trade. What follows is month by month on the heat, the water, the crowds and the price, with the dates that catch people out: the week around Santa Marija in August, Holy Week, and the fourteen public holidays that shut the museums.

The short answer

Three ways to choose, depending on what you are coming for.

For the sights

May, or October. Warm without the punishment, everything open, and Valletta, Mdina and the temples workable in the middle of the day.

For swimming

Late September and October. The sea holds its heat far later than the air, and October water is usually warmer than June water, with a fraction of the crowd.

For value and quiet

November to March, outside Christmas. Mild, green, genuinely cheap and nearly empty - with the caveat that the north and Gozo close much of their seasonal trade.

Month by month

What each month is actually like, written for a visitor rather than for a weather chart.

January

Mild by European standards - around fifteen degrees in the day - and the wettest stretch of the year, with short hard downpours between bright spells. The cheapest month, the sites nearly empty, and a good deal of seasonal trade in the north and on Gozo shut.

February

Much like January, with Carnival the event: the official one in Valletta with floats and costumed bands, and the older, ruder, unofficial version at Nadur on Gozo, which is worth the crossing. The tenth is the Feast of St Paul’s Shipwreck and a public holiday.

March

The country turns green, which surprises people who only know Malta in August. Wildflowers on the garigue, walking at its best, cool evenings and a sea still too cold for most. Prices are low until Easter approaches. Freedom Day falls on the thirty-first.

April

One of the two or three best months. Warm days around twenty degrees, long light, everything open again and the countryside at its peak. Holy Week is taken seriously here - Good Friday processions in many villages - and the Easter week itself is busy and priced accordingly.

May

Our pick, with October. Reliably warm without heat, the sea just about swimmable by the end of it, the sites comfortable at midday and the summer crowds a month away. The first of May is a public holiday. If you can only come once, come now.

June

Hot and getting hotter, with the longest days of the year and the sea properly warm from about the middle of the month. Sette Giugno on the seventh and the Mnarja festival at the end of June, an old harvest feast in Buskett woodland, are both public holidays. Still short of peak prices.

July

Very hot - regularly in the mid thirties - humid, and the busiest month along with August. Village festas most weekends, fireworks almost nightly somewhere, and the sandy bays and the Blue Lagoon crowded from mid morning. Swim early and late and keep the middle of the day indoors.

August

The hottest and the most expensive, and the month the islands fill. Santa Marija, the Assumption on the fifteenth, is the biggest holiday of the Maltese year and the week around it is effectively a national shutdown - book everything well ahead. The sea is at its warmest.

September

Heat easing from the middle of the month, the sea still very warm, prices falling after the first week and the crowds thinning. The lampuki run starts and appears on every menu. Victory Day on the eighth and Independence Day on the twenty-first are both public holidays.

October

Our other pick, and for swimming the best month of all - the sea is usually warmer than it is in June, the air is in the mid twenties, and the islands are quiet. The rains return towards the end. For most visitors this is the single best compromise Malta offers.

November

Still mild and still swimmable in the first half for the hardy, but the wet season proper begins and the seasonal trade starts closing in the north and on Gozo. Cheap, calm and perfectly good for Valletta, Mdina and the temples. Check your hotel’s winter opening before booking.

December

Mild, wet and cheap, with the shortest days and Valletta lit for Christmas. The eighth and the thirteenth - the Immaculate Conception and Republic Day - are public holidays, and the Christmas and New Year weeks are the one expensive spell of the winter.

Where to be, season by season

The islands do not all have the same season, and the north and Gozo close more of theirs than the harbour towns do.

High summer, July and August

Be where you can swim and where there is shade: Sliema and St Julian’s for the lidos and the promenade, Mellieha for the sand, Gozo for the quiet version of the same. Do the temples and the cities early in the morning. Book the Assumption week months out.

The shoulders, April to June and September to October

Anywhere. This is the only part of the year when all of it works at once - sea warm enough, air cool enough to walk uphill, everything open and prices short of their peak. Valletta and Gozo are both at their best.

Winter, November to March

Be near Valletta. The capital, the Three Cities, Mdina, Rabat and the museums are open, indoors and unaffected by the weather, and the harbour towns keep their restaurants. The north and parts of Gozo shut a good deal of their seasonal trade, and bus services thin.

Whenever you come, give Gozo a night

The second island empties when the last day-trippers take the ferry back, and that is the Gozo people remember. In winter check first that your hotel and somewhere to eat near it are actually open; in summer it is the cooler, calmer half of the country.

Public holidays and the things that close

Malta keeps fourteen public holidays, more than almost any country in Europe, and they matter to a short trip because museums, the smaller sites and a good many shops close on them. The fixed dates are 1 January; 10 February, the Feast of St Paul’s Shipwreck; 19 March, St Joseph; 31 March, Freedom Day; 1 May; 7 June, Sette Giugno; 29 June, St Peter and St Paul, known as Mnarja; 15 August, the Assumption, called Santa Marija; 8 September, Victory Day; 21 September, Independence Day; 8 December, the Immaculate Conception; 13 December, Republic Day; and 25 December. Good Friday moves with Easter. When one falls midweek the whole week runs oddly.

Two of them are worth planning around rather than merely noting. The week around Santa Marija in the middle of August is the busiest and most expensive of the Maltese year, with hotels full and ferries heavy - book months rather than weeks ahead. Holy Week brings processions in most villages and closures on Good Friday. Beyond the official list, every parish holds its own festa on a summer weekend, with band marches and serious fireworks; these do not close anything, but they will keep you awake if one is next door, and they are much the best thing you can stumble into here. Ask your hotel which village has one that week.

One more piece of timing, nothing to do with the calendar: the Hypogeum of Hal Saflieni limits visitor numbers strictly and routinely sells out weeks or months ahead. If you want to see it, book it before you book the flights.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Malta is as straightforward as Europe gets for an American or Canadian visitor. It has been in the European Union since 2004, in the Schengen area since 2007 and on the euro since 2008, so an arrival from another Schengen country has no passport control at all, and an arrival from outside it is a single check at Malta International Airport. There is no land border to cross. The ferry between Malta and Sicily is an internal Schengen crossing; the one to Tunisia is not, and is checked.

Timing a Malta trip — questions

What is the single best month to visit Malta?

October, narrowly, for most visitors. The sea is at or near its warmest - usually warmer than in June - the air sits in the mid twenties, everything is still open, the summer crowds have gone and prices have come off their peak. May is the close second and is better if you want the countryside green and the wildflowers out. If you are coming for the sights rather than the sea, either of those two months beats anything else in the year.

When is the sea warm enough to swim?

Roughly June to November, which is about a month longer at the back end than most of the Mediterranean. The water warms slowly through June, peaks in August and September, and is still comfortable for most people through October and into early November. It is at its coldest in February and March, when only the locals and the determined go in. Malta’s swimming is mostly off rock shelves and ladders rather than sand, so bring something for your feet.

How bad is the heat in July and August?

Harder work than the thermometer suggests. The air sits in the mid thirties Celsius, the humidity is high, and there is very little natural shade on an island built of pale limestone with few trees, so the glare adds to it. It is entirely manageable if you plan around it - early mornings, a long indoor middle of the day, evenings outside - but a full day of sightseeing on foot in August is not a pleasant undertaking, and older travellers in particular should shift the dates if they can.

Is winter worth it?

For the history, yes, and it is much the cheapest time to come. Valletta, the Three Cities, Mdina, Rabat, the museums and the temples are all open and are better without crowds, the weather is mild at around fifteen degrees, and the countryside is green rather than burnt. The honest caveats are that it is the wet season, that the days are short, that swimming is out, and that the north of Malta and much of Gozo close a considerable part of their seasonal trade from November to March. Base yourself near Valletta and check opening hours directly.

When should I avoid Malta?

There is no bad month, but two periods ask for care. The week around 15 August, Santa Marija, is the busiest and dearest of the year, and if you have not booked months ahead it is better avoided. And if a beach holiday is the whole point of the trip, November to March will disappoint you - the sea is cold, the seasonal resorts are shut and the rain arrives in bursts. Come for the stones in winter and the sea in autumn, and both halves of the country will show you their best.
