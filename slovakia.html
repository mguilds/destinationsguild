Slovakia

The Slovakia Travel Guide

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Slovakia is the country people drive through, and that is its great advantage. It sits in the middle of Europe with Austria, Czechia, Poland, Ukraine and Hungary around it, and almost everything that makes it worth a week is squeezed into a long band of mountains running west to east: the High Tatras, the smallest genuinely alpine range in the world, where you can be on bare rock at 2,600 metres half an hour after leaving a valley hotel; the Mala Fatra and the Low Tatras behind them; limestone gorges with ladders bolted into them in the Slovak Paradise; and more castles per square mile than anywhere else in Europe, including Spis, which is one of the biggest castle sites on the continent. At the western end, Bratislava stands on the Danube with Austria on one bank and Hungary twenty minutes away, the only national capital in the world bordering two other countries. At the eastern end, Kosice has the largest church in Slovakia and a main street built to be walked down. It has been in the European Union since 2004, in Schengen since 2007 and in the euro since 2009, so for a visitor from elsewhere in Europe there is no border check and no money to change — and it is still a good deal cheaper than the country on the far bank of its own river.

Why visit Slovakia

The High Tatras are a proper alpine range and they are tiny

This is the thing Slovakia has that nowhere else does. The High Tatras run about twenty-five kilometres end to end — you can see the whole range from one viewpoint — and inside that space they do everything the Alps do: granite walls, glacial tarns, marked ridge routes, mountain huts you walk between, and a highest point at Gerlachovsky stit, 2,655 metres. The compression is the point. A cableway from Tatranska Lomnica puts you on the summit of Lomnicky stit at 2,634 metres in a few minutes, and a funicular from Stary Smokovec takes you to a hut at the tree line, so you can get genuine high-mountain country without a two-day approach. Tatra National Park, declared in 1949, is the oldest in the country. One honest caveat: because it is small, it is busy, and the Strbske Pleso and Hrebienok paths on an August Saturday are nothing like empty.

More castles than anywhere, and Spis is the one to see

Slovakia has well over a hundred castles and castle ruins and it is genuinely hard to drive an hour without passing one on a crag. Spis Castle is the one people photograph: a white limestone sprawl on a ridge above the Hornad valley, among the half-dozen largest castle sites in the country and on the UNESCO list since 1993 together with the cathedral settlement at Spisska Kapitula, the church at Zehra and — added later — the town of Levoca below. Then Orava Castle on its rock above the river in the north, Trencin standing over its town in the west, Bojnice looking like something from a fairy tale and the most visited of the lot, Cachtice in ruins with the Elizabeth Bathory stories attached, and Devin where the Morava runs into the Danube on the Austrian border.

Eight UNESCO entries in a country the size of Denmark

Slovakia carries eight World Heritage properties and they are an unusually varied eight. Levoca, Spissky hrad and the associated monuments. Vlkolinec, a hillside hamlet of forty-five log houses above Ruzomberok, still lived in, and the clearest surviving picture of what a central European mountain village looked like. Banska Stiavnica, a silver-mining town built into a volcanic caldera, which was once one of the richest places in the Habsburg lands and now has a quiet, slightly faded grandeur that is the best half-day in the country. Bardejov in the far north-east, a walled town with a square of burgher houses that survived everything. The wooden churches of the Slovak Carpathians, eight of them, Catholic, Lutheran and Greek Catholic, built without a nail. The caves of the Slovak Karst, shared with Hungary. The primeval beech forests, shared with much of Europe. And the Danube limes, the Roman river frontier, shared with Austria and Germany.

Caves, gorges and ladders bolted into rock

The limestone country in the centre and east is riddled with caves and about a dozen of them are open to visitors, which is a lot for a small country. The Dobsinska Ice Cave holds permanent ice and is cold enough in July to want a coat. Ochtinska is an aragonite cave, one of only a handful anywhere, with white crystal growing in sprays off the walls. Domica runs under the Hungarian border. Demanovska Cave of Liberty in the Low Tatras is the showpiece. Above ground, the Slovak Paradise is the unusual one: a national park of narrow wooded gorges where the route up the stream bed is made passable by iron ladders, rungs and plank bridges, one-way, and genuinely exhilarating. Sucha Bela is the classic. It is not for anyone unsteady on a ladder, and it is not an afternoon stroll.

Spa towns and thermal water, used by locals rather than by tourists

Slovakia sits on a great deal of hot water and has been selling cures since the Habsburgs. Piestany is the grand one, on an island in the Vah, where the mud and the sulphur water have been treating joints since the eighteenth century and the Napoleon Baths and the Thermia Palace are worth seeing whether or not you take a treatment. Rajecke Teplice is prettier and smaller. Besenova and Oravice in the north are thermal water done as big outdoor pools with mountain views, which on a cold bright day is one of the best things you can do in the country. These are working medical spas as much as leisure ones, and the clientele is mostly Slovak, Czech and German rather than international.

It is in the euro, in Schengen, and cheaper than its neighbours

Slovakia joined the European Union and NATO in 2004, the Schengen area in December 2007 and the euro on 1 January 2009 — the first of the central European states to make that last step. For a visitor arriving from Austria, Czechia, Poland or Hungary that means no border check and no currency to change, and for a visitor from further afield it means one less thing to arrange. What has not converged is the price level: Bratislava is markedly cheaper than Vienna an hour down the road, and the Tatras are cheaper than any comparable Alpine week. The gap is narrowing year by year, which is an argument for going sooner.

Slovakia at a glance

Capital

Bratislava, in the far west on the Danube, with about 480,000 people — the only national capital anywhere that has land borders with two other countries, Austria and Hungary. Bratislava Airport (BTS) handles a modest number of routes, mostly low-cost and mostly to Britain, Italy and the eastern Mediterranean. The practical point for most visitors is that Vienna Airport is about an hour away by direct bus and is usually the cheaper and better-connected way in. Kosice Airport serves the east with flights via Vienna, Prague and a handful of others.

Money

The euro, since 1 January 2009. Slovakia was the first of the central European countries to adopt it and the second post-communist state after Slovenia, so the Slovak koruna has been gone for the better part of two decades and any guide quoting one is badly out of date. Cards are taken almost everywhere, contactless is normal on city transport, and ATMs are everywhere. Keep some cash for mountain huts, village pubs, small chairlift offices and church collection boxes.

Language

Slovak, a West Slavic language in the Latin alphabet with a lot of accents, and close enough to Czech that the two are mutually intelligible. It is phonetic, which makes place names easier than they look. English is common among younger people and throughout tourism; German is useful along the Austrian border; Hungarian is a first language for about eight per cent of the population in the southern strip, where signs are often bilingual. In the east, older people may reach for Russian.

Borders

In NATO and the European Union since 2004, in the Schengen area since 21 December 2007 and in the euro area since 1 January 2009. Neighbours are Czechia and Austria to the west, Poland along the whole northern mountain crest, Ukraine in the far east and Hungary along the south. All of those are internal Schengen crossings except Ukraine, which is an external border and where the Entry/Exit System applies.

Getting about

Driving is on the right and a vignette is required for the motorways — electronic, bought online or at filling stations, and usually already on a hire car. The D1 motorway runs the spine of the country from Bratislava towards Kosice but is still not complete end to end, so allow more than the distance suggests. Trains are cheap, reliable and the sensible way between the big towns: Bratislava to Kosice takes between five and six hours on an InterCity. Within the Tatras the narrow-gauge Tatra Electric Railway links Poprad with the resort villages and is a delight in itself. Intercity coaches are often faster than trains on the diagonal routes.

Shape of it

About 49,000 square kilometres, which is a little larger than Estonia or Denmark and a little smaller than West Virginia, with a population of roughly 5.4 million. It is long and thin and ridged: the Carpathians cross the whole north in a series of ranges, the Danube lowland in the south-west around Bratislava is the flat and fertile part, and the far east drops away towards the Hungarian and Ukrainian plains. A parliamentary republic and a member of the single market.

The regions

Five parts of the country that feel genuinely different from one another — the Danube west around the capital, the High Tatras and the Spis, the Mala Fatra and Orava in the north-west, the mining country and the Low Tatras in the middle, and the quiet east.

Bratislava and the west

The Danube lowland: the capital on the river with Austria on the far bank, the Small Carpathian vineyards running north of it, and a line of old towns and castles up the Vah valley.

  • BratislavaGuild guide
  • Devin Castle — a ruin on the cliff where the Morava joins the Danube, right on the Austrian border, and for forty years one of the most heavily guarded stretches of the Iron Curtain; twenty minutes from the city by bus or boat
  • Trnava — the walled “little Rome” of a dozen churches, half an hour from Bratislava and the centre of the Small Carpathian wine country
  • Trencin — a castle standing directly over its town, with a Roman inscription cut into the rock below it recording a victory by the Second Legion at Laugaricio in AD 179 — the northernmost Roman inscription of its kind
  • Piestany — the grand spa town on an island in the Vah, treating joints with sulphur mud since the eighteenth century, with the Napoleon Baths and the Thermia Palace
  • Nitra — the oldest town in Slovakia and the seat of the ninth-century Great Moravian church, under a castle hill in the farming country east of Bratislava

The High Tatras and the Spis

The alpine crest and the old Saxon towns below it: twenty-five kilometres of granite with cableways into it, and a limestone basin full of castles, walled towns and wooden churches.

  • PopradGuild guide
  • Tatranska Lomnica — the eastern resort village and the bottom station of the cableway to the 2,634-metre summit of Lomnicky stit
  • Strbske Pleso — a village at 1,350 metres beside its own glacial lake, the highest of the Tatra resorts and the start of the best short walks
  • Levoca — a walled town on the UNESCO list, with the tallest Gothic wooden altar in the world in the church of St James and a hill of pilgrims above it
  • Spis Castle — a white limestone sprawl on a ridge above the Hornad, among the largest castle sites in the country, UNESCO-listed since 1993 along with the cathedral settlement at Spisska Kapitula and the frescoed church at Zehra
  • Kezmarok — a small Tatra-foothill town with one of the eight UNESCO wooden churches, a Lutheran hall built without a nail that seats a congregation of 1,500

The north-west: the Mala Fatra and Orava

The prettiest valley country in Slovakia and the easiest mountains to walk in: a ridge of rock towers above a river gorge, log villages, and two of the country’s best-known castles.

  • ZilinaGuild guide
  • Terchova — the village at the mouth of the Vratna valley, birthplace of the outlaw Juraj Janosik, and the gateway to the Mala Fatra ridge and the Janosik gorges
  • Ruzomberok — the mill town below Vlkolinec, the UNESCO-listed hillside hamlet of forty-five log houses that is still lived in and still the clearest picture of an old Carpathian mountain village
  • Orava Castle — a tiered fortress on a rock directly above the Orava river in the far north, 154 steps from gate to the top, and the location of a good deal of the 1922 film Nosferatu
  • Cicmany — a village of dark log houses painted all over with white geometric patterns, which began as lime-wash protection for the timber and became the best-known folk motif in the country
  • Rajecke Teplice — a small, well-kept thermal spa in a side valley, the quiet alternative to Piestany

Central Slovakia and the Low Tatras

Mining towns, the biggest ski area in the country, and the caves. This is also the heart of the bear country, so it is the part of Slovakia where the walking advice in this guide matters most.

  • Banska BystricaGuild guide
  • Demanovska Dolina — the valley below Chopok that holds the Jasna ski area, the biggest in Slovakia, and the Demanovska Cave of Liberty
  • Banska Stiavnica — a silver-mining town built into a volcanic caldera, once one of the richest places in the Habsburg empire, UNESCO-listed and the best half-day in the country
  • Besenova — thermal pools in the open air with the Tatras on the skyline, which on a cold bright day is the best value in Slovakia
  • The Dobsinska Ice Cave — permanent ice underground, part of the UNESCO karst inscription, and cold enough in July that you will want a coat

Kosice and the east

The quietest and least visited half of the country: a proper second city, a walled town in the far north-east, gorges with ladders in them, and the wooden churches along the Polish border.

  • KosiceGuild guide
  • Bardejov — a walled town of burgher houses around an intact square in the far north-east, UNESCO-listed, with a Jewish suburb and a spa in the woods above it
  • The Slovak Paradise — a national park of narrow wooded gorges climbed on iron ladders and rungs bolted into the rock, one-way and genuinely exhilarating; Sucha Bela is the classic route and it is not for anyone unsteady on a ladder
  • The wooden churches — eight of them on the UNESCO list, Catholic, Lutheran and Greek Catholic, scattered along the north-eastern border at Hervartov, Tvrdosin, Kezmarok, Lestiny, Hronsek, Ruska Bystra, Bodruzal and Ladomirova
  • Slovak Tokaj — the small Slovak share of the historic Tokaj region, in the far south-east on the Hungarian border, making the same sweet wine on a fraction of the scale and almost unknown outside the country

Things to do in Slovakia

Ride the cableway up Lomnicky stit

From Tatranska Lomnica, changing at Skalnate pleso, up to a summit station at 2,634 metres with an observatory on it and the whole range laid out below. It is the single easiest way anywhere in Europe to stand on a serious alpine summit without climbing, and the drop from the valley floor is about 1,700 metres. Tickets for the top section are timed and limited and sell out in summer and at half-term, so book ahead rather than turning up. It closes in high wind, which is common, and the air at the top is genuinely thin and cold even in August.

Walk a gorge in the Slovak Paradise

The Slovensky raj is the most distinctive walking in the country: you go up the stream bed of a narrow limestone gorge on iron ladders, rungs and plank walkways bolted into the rock, and the popular routes are one-way uphill for that reason. Sucha Bela is the famous one and takes a couple of hours up; Prielom Hornadu is the long flat one along the river for anyone who would rather not climb a ladder with water under it. Go early, wear boots with grip, accept that you will get wet feet, and do not attempt the ladder routes in heavy rain or if you are unsteady.

Give Spis Castle and Levoca a whole day

They are eight kilometres apart and they belong together. The castle is a long white ruin along the top of a ridge, with a steep approach, almost no shade and views over the whole Spis basin; the town below has a complete square of Renaissance houses and, in the church of St James, the tallest Gothic wooden altar in the world, carved by Master Pavol of Levoca in the early sixteenth century. Add the cathedral settlement at Spisska Kapitula and the frescoed village church at Zehra and you have the whole UNESCO inscription in an afternoon.

Eat halusky in a mountain pub

Bryndzove halusky — potato dumplings under melted sheep cheese with bacon on top — is the national dish and it is best where it belongs, in a wooden-benched pub in a valley after a day on the hill. Order it with a Saris or a Zlaty Bazant, or with zincica, the thin whey drink that comes with it in the sheep country. It is a serious plate of food and it is not a light lunch. Add kapustnica, the sauerkraut and sausage soup, if the weather is against you.

See Banska Stiavnica before everyone else does

A silver town built up the inside of a collapsed volcano, with a steep square, two castles, a calvary climbing a cone-shaped hill behind it and the mining academy that was the first of its kind in the world. It was one of the wealthiest towns in the Habsburg empire and then it was nothing for two centuries, which is why it survived. It is UNESCO-listed, it is about two hours from Bratislava, and it still has the feel of a place that visitors have not got around to.

Take the Tatra Electric Railway from end to end

A narrow-gauge electric line running along the foot of the High Tatras from Poprad through Stary Smokovec to Strbske Pleso, with a rack section on the climb at the western end. It takes about an hour, costs very little, runs all year and is the practical way to get between the resort villages without a car — and it is also simply a good ride, with the wall of the range on one side the whole way.

Stand at Devin where two rivers and three countries meet

The ruin on the cliff above the junction of the Morava and the Danube is the oldest story in Slovakia — a Great Moravian stronghold, a Roman outpost before that, blown up by Napoleon’s army in 1809 — and for four decades it was also the edge of the Eastern bloc, with the border fence running under the rock and a memorial now to the people shot trying to cross. Austria is on the far bank. It is a short bus or a boat trip from Bratislava and it is the best single excursion from the city.

Find a wooden church

Eight of them are on the UNESCO list and they are built entirely of wood without a single nail — three Roman Catholic, two Lutheran and three Greek Catholic, which between them tell you most of what you need to know about this corner of Europe. The Greek Catholic ones at Bodruzal and Ladomirova in the far north-east are the ones that look like nothing else: dark shingled towers in threes rising over a graveyard in an empty valley. They are scattered and some keep short hours, so this is a car trip with a phone call ahead.

When to go, and what it costs

Slovakia runs on two calendars and they barely overlap. Late June to mid-September is the mountain summer: the high huts and the chairlifts are open, the Tatra ridge routes are passable, the gorges in the Slovak Paradise are at their best and the valley temperatures are comfortable. January to mid-March is the ski season at Jasna, Tatranska Lomnica, Donovaly and the rest, with the most dependable snow from mid-January. Between them, May and October are lovely in the towns and in the lowland and awkward in the mountains — snow lies on the Tatra passes well into June, and the huts and lifts shut through the second half of October and much of November. July and August are warm rather than hot by southern European standards but they are the busy weeks, with afternoon thunderstorms the normal pattern over the ranges. The cheapest weeks of the year are November and the first half of December.

Fishing here

Slovakia is better fishing country than almost anyone outside it realises, and it comes in two kinds. The mountain rivers — the upper Vah, the Hron, the Orava, the Hnilec, the Dunajec along the Polish border in the Pieniny — hold brown trout and grayling, and the Dunajec in particular is a well-known grayling river fished from both banks by nationals of two countries. The dam lakes and the big lowland water are the other half: Liptovska Mara under the Tatras, Oravska Priehrada on the Polish border, Zemplinska Sirava in the east and the Danube itself below Bratislava, fished for carp, pike, zander, catfish and in the Danube’s case some very large fish indeed. A state fishing licence plus a local permit for the particular stretch is required, both are bought from the Slovak Fishing Union or a local tackle shop rather than online from abroad, the trout season is dated and closed through the winter and spring, and many of the best stretches are club water with their own rules. It is a trip to arrange on the ground with someone who knows which water is open. The Guild has no Slovakia fishing guide on the shelf yet; the whole shelf is in The Guild Fishing Library.

Travelling in your later years

Bratislava is easy; the old town is cobbled

Central Bratislava is small and mostly level, with trams and a short walk covering everything, and the river embankment is a long flat promenade with benches. The old town inside the walls is cobbled throughout, in the original uneven way rather than smoothed setts, and the climb up to the castle is a steady one on steps and ramp. The lift-served alternative is the bus to the castle gate. Kosice is better again: Hlavna is a wide, flat, paved pedestrian street almost a kilometre long with the cathedral in the middle of it and cafes down both sides.

The mountains are unusually reachable without walking

This is the real argument for Slovakia in later years. The cableway from Tatranska Lomnica, the funicular from Stary Smokovec, the chairlifts at Strbske Pleso and the Chopok cableways in the Low Tatras all put you in high mountain country with no climb at all, and the Tatra Electric Railway gets you between the villages without a car. The lakeside path at Strbske Pleso is level and surfaced. You can have a week of serious mountain scenery here on mechanical transport, which is not true of most alpine destinations.

Think about the altitude, and about the thin air at the top

Lomnicky stit is 2,634 metres and the cableway gets you there in minutes from a valley at under 900. That is a fast gain and it is worth knowing if you have a heart or breathing condition; the summit station is cold and windy in any season and there is no gradual acclimatisation. The lower stations at Skalnate pleso and Hrebienok, both around 1,250 to 1,750 metres, give most of the view with much less of the effect, and nobody minds if you stop there.

Where the ground is genuinely difficult

The Slovak Paradise gorge routes are ladders over running water and are not suitable for anyone unsteady, full stop; the river route at Prielom Hornadu is the level alternative. Spis Castle is a long uphill approach on rough ground with no shade and few handrails. The Levoca calvary and the Banska Stiavnica calvary are both steep hill climbs. Several of the show caves involve wet steps and a few hundred of them. Against that: Levoca’s square and church are flat, the Bardejov square is flat, the spa parks at Piestany and Rajecke Teplice are level and planted for walking, and the thermal pools at Besenova and Oravice are built for people who want the water rather than the hill.

Getting in: what applies, and what does not

Slovakia has been a member of the European Union since 1 May 2004 and a full member of the Schengen area since 21 December 2007, so arriving from Austria, Czechia, Poland or Hungary is an internal journey with no routine passport check. The rules below are the ones that apply when you first enter the Schengen area from outside it, which for Slovakia means arriving from Ukraine or from outside Europe.

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen external border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave. United States, Canadian, British and Australian citizens may visit as tourists without a visa for up to ninety days in any one hundred and eighty, counted across the whole Schengen area rather than against Slovakia alone — so a longer European trip uses the same allowance.

The money is simple: Slovakia has used the euro since 1 January 2009, the first of the central European states to adopt it, so there is nothing to change and nothing to work out. The Slovak koruna has not been legal tender for the better part of two decades, and any guidebook or website quoting koruna prices is describing a country that no longer exists in that form.

We could not read the United States travel advisory for Slovakia on the day this page was written — the State Department’s site would not answer us — so we are not printing a level here rather than quoting one from memory. Read the current advisory yourself at travel.state.gov before you go; the level and the wording change, and it is a two-minute check.

Getting here: Bratislava Airport has a modest timetable, mostly low-cost and mostly to Britain, Italy and the eastern Mediterranean, and the honest advice is to price Vienna as well — it is about an hour from central Bratislava by direct bus, it has far more long-haul and European connections, and for many visitors it is both cheaper and quicker. Kosice serves the east through Vienna, Prague and a handful of others, which is worth knowing if your trip is Tatras-and-east rather than Bratislava-and-west. Trains and coaches arrive from Prague, Vienna, Budapest and Krakow, and the Budapest and Vienna runs are both easy. Timetables change by season, so confirm them with the operator for your own dates rather than trusting a figure on any website, including this one — which is why we do not print one.

One genuine safety note that belongs here rather than buried further down: Slovakia has a large brown bear population in the central ranges, there have been serious and occasionally fatal attacks, and the government declared an emergency in several districts in 2025. It is not a reason to stay away — hundreds of thousands of people walk these hills every year without incident — but it is a reason to stay on marked trails, make noise, keep food out of reach at huts and car parks, and walk in company rather than alone at first and last light. There is more on this in the questions below.

Slovakia — questions

Does Slovakia use the euro?

Yes, and it has done for a long time. Slovakia adopted the euro on 1 January 2009 — the first of the central European states to do so and the second post-communist country after Slovenia. The Slovak koruna is long gone. This catches people out in two directions: older guidebooks still print koruna prices, and travellers who have just been to Prague or Budapest arrive expecting another currency to change and find they do not need one. Cards are taken almost everywhere and contactless is normal; carry a little cash for mountain huts, village pubs and the smaller chairlift ticket offices.

How many days do I need in Slovakia?

Four days does Bratislava properly with a day out to Devin or across to Vienna. A week is the right trip for most first visitors: two nights in Bratislava, then three or four in the mountains at Poprad or in the Tatra villages, with Spis Castle and Levoca on the way. Ten days adds Kosice and the east, which is where the wooden churches, the caves and the quietest country are. The country is small — about 49,000 square kilometres — but it is long and ridged west to east, so the drive from Bratislava to Kosice is a real day rather than a hop, and the mountain roads are slower than the map suggests.

Is Slovakia cheaper than Austria or Czechia?

Cheaper than Austria by a wide margin and broadly similar to Czechia, though Prague has pulled ahead of Bratislava on price and the Slovak mountains are notably better value than the Alps an hour to the west. Eating and drinking are the standout: a proper plate of halusky and a local beer in a Bratislava pub costs a fraction of the same meal across the Danube in Austria. The two places you will feel a squeeze are the Tatra resort villages in the February and August peaks and Bratislava old town in the Christmas market weeks. We do not print figures because they move.

Is Bratislava just a day trip from Vienna?

It is sold that way, and that is a mistake. The two capitals are about 50 to 60 kilometres apart — closer than any other pair in Europe — and the bus or the boat makes a day trip easy, which is why a great many people see the old town in four hours and leave. Give it two nights instead. The old town is small but the city around it is not: Devin at the confluence, the Slavin memorial on its hill, the Danube embankments, the castle, the Blue Church, the Petrzalka housing estates that are a piece of twentieth-century history in their own right, and an eating and drinking scene that is much better than its reputation. Also worth knowing in reverse: Vienna airport is often the cheapest way into Slovakia, with a direct bus to Bratislava in about an hour.

Are the bears a real problem for walkers?

They are a real consideration, and we would rather say so plainly than leave it out. Slovakia has a substantial brown bear population — the government put it above 1,300 animals in 2025 — concentrated in the central ranges, the Low Tatras, the Velka Fatra and the hills around Banska Bystrica rather than on the high bare rock of the Tatra peaks. There have been serious attacks: a man was killed in central Slovakia in April 2025, after which the government declared an emergency in several districts and approved culling 350 bears, a decision the European Commission objected to; a hunter near Kremnica was attacked and shot a female bear with a cub in June 2026. Set against that, hundreds of thousands of people walk these hills every year without seeing one. The practical rules are the ordinary ones: stay on marked trails, make noise on blind corners and in berry scrub, walk in company rather than alone at dawn or dusk, never leave food or rubbish at a hut or a car park, never go near a cub, and call 112 if you see one. Guided walking is a sensible choice in the central ranges if this worries you.

Do I need a car?

Not for Bratislava, not for Kosice, and not for a week based in the Tatras, where the Tatra Electric Railway links Poprad, Stary Smokovec and Strbske Pleso and the funiculars and cableways do the climbing. For everywhere else a car changes the trip: the castles, the wooden churches, the painted village at Cicmany, the caves and the spa towns all sit along roads rather than rails. Driving is on the right and a motorway vignette is required — it is electronic, bought online or at a filling station, and a hire car usually has one already, which is worth confirming at the desk. Winter tyres are compulsory in winter conditions, and the mountain passes close in snow.

Is Slovak the same as Czech?

Close enough that Slovaks and Czechs understand each other without effort, and the two languages were taught side by side for seventy years of a shared state, but they are separate languages. Slovak is West Slavic, written in the Latin alphabet with a generous supply of accents, and it is phonetic once you know the letters. English is widely spoken by younger people, in Bratislava and in anything to do with tourism; German is useful near the Austrian border; Hungarian is the first language of about eight per cent of the population along the southern strip, where town signs are often in both languages. Older people in the east may offer you Russian before English.

What should I eat?

Bryndzove halusky, and do not leave without it: small potato dumplings under melted bryndza, the soft sheep cheese, with fried bacon on top. It is the national dish and it is heavy enough to be a day’s walking in itself. Then kapustnica, the sauerkraut and sausage soup that is Christmas Eve and also a good lunch in February; zemiakove placky, potato pancakes; lokse, the thin potato flatbreads sold at Christmas markets with goose fat or poppyseed; and the smoked sheep cheeses, parenica wound in a coil and ostiepok shaped like a little barrel. Drink the beer — Saris and Zlaty Bazant are the big names and the small breweries are better — and look for the wine, which nobody expects: the Small Carpathian vineyards north of Bratislava, and Slovak Tokaj in the far south-east, which is the same historic region as the Hungarian one and makes the same sweet wine on a much smaller scale. Borovicka is the juniper spirit; Tatratea is the tourist one and it is stronger than it tastes.
