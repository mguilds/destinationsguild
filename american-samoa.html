A short history

Samoans have lived across these volcanic islands for roughly 3,000 years, developing fa'a Samoa, a communal way of life built around extended families (aiga) and matai chiefly titles that still governs daily life today. European contact began in the 1700s, and by the late 1800s Germany, Britain and the United States were competing for influence in the Samoan archipelago.

An 1899 treaty split the islands, with the United States taking the eastern group, formalized through deeds of cession signed by local chiefs between 1900 and 1904. Pago Pago's deep natural harbour made it valuable as a naval coaling station, and American Samoa remains an unincorporated U.S. territory administered locally, with residents holding U.S. national, though not automatic citizen, status.

The economy has long depended on tuna canning, government employment and remittances from Samoans living abroad. The National Park of American Samoa, established in 1988, protects rare Pacific rainforest and reef ecosystems, and today the territory remains one of the least-visited corners of the United States, prized for its authenticity.

When to go

The drier months (June–September) are most comfortable.

Winter (Dec–Feb)

The wet season, warm and humid with frequent tropical downpours.

Spring (Mar–May)

Rain gradually eases; lush green landscapes and fewer crowds.

Summer (Jun–Aug)

The drier, cooler season and the most comfortable time to hike and dive.

Autumn (Sep–Nov)

Still relatively dry, transitioning back toward the wetter months by November.
