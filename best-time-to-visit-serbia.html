Serbia

Best Time to Visit Serbia

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Serbia has a moderately continental climate and very little to soften it: no coast, no maritime breeze, and air masses that arrive either from Siberia or from Africa depending on the week. That gives it cold foggy winters, hot still summers, a short unstable spring and a long, dry, golden autumn that is the most under-rated thing about the country. The plain in the north and the mountains in the south-west keep different calendars, and the gap between them in the same week can be twenty degrees. This page sets out the year month by month, says what each one does to a trip, and gives the Belgrade averages because Belgrade is where most visitors spend most of their time.

The short answer

Go in September, May or June. September is the pick: the summer heat has gone, the days are still long, the vineyards and orchards are picking, the crowds have gone home with the school year and prices have come off their peak. May is almost as good and greener. June is the month to choose if you want the cities and the mountains in one trip, and it is also the wettest — afternoon thunderstorms rather than grey days. July and August are hot, sometimes fiercely so on the plain, and are the months to spend at altitude or on the rivers. October is the country’s own favourite and the right month for the monasteries, the Danube gorge and the wine country. December to February is cold, grey and cheap, excellent for Belgrade kafanas and museums and for skiing at Zlatibor or Kopaonik, and poor for everything outdoors in between. If you are coming for one week and do not ski, come in the second half of September.

Serbia month by month

January

The coldest month and the foggiest. Belgrade averages about 1 degree, with daytime highs near 4 or 5 and nights below freezing; a cold snap off Russia can take it to minus 15 or lower for a few days, and when cold air settles in the Danube basin the fog can sit for a week. Sunshine is at its worst — about two and a half hours a day. This is the cheapest time to be in the cities and a genuinely good one: the kafanas are full, the museums are empty, and Serbian Orthodox Christmas on 7 January is the warmest public occasion in the calendar. Zlatibor and Kopaonik are skiing, and New Year week is the single dearest week of the mountain year.

February

Much like January but turning at the end of it: about 3 degrees on average in Belgrade, highs around 7, still foggy and grey in spells, still cheap. Statehood Day on 15 February is a two-day holiday. The ski season is at its most reliable this month. Nothing in the cities is closed and nothing is crowded, which for the fortresses and the galleries is a real advantage.

March

The thaw, and an unreliable month. Averages climb to about 7 or 8 degrees in Belgrade with highs near 13, but late snow is perfectly possible and the weather swings week to week. The plain is bare and the mountain roads can still be winter roads. Prices are at their lowest. Good for cities and museums, too early for the countryside, and the last of the skiing goes in the second half of the month.

April

Spring properly, and a pleasant time to be here: about 12 or 13 degrees on the plain, highs around 18, the Fruska Gora and Sumadija woods coming out, and the monasteries at their best for a visit without heat or crowds. Expect showers. Orthodox Easter falls in this part of the year on its own reckoning and moves, so check the date before you fix a trip around it — it is the biggest family holiday in the country and rooms near a famous monastery go early.

May

One of the three best months. Belgrade averages about 17 degrees with highs of 23, the days are long, everything is open and nothing is full. The plateau at Zlatibor is at its greenest, the Uvac canyon and Tara are walking country again, and the Danube is high. Rain comes in afternoon thunderstorms that clear. If you want one month for a first trip and can choose freely, this or September.

June

The wettest month in Belgrade — about 75 millimetres — and almost all of it afternoon thunderstorms rather than grey days; sunshine still runs about eight and a half hours a day. Averages near 21 degrees with highs of 27. It is the best all-round month for combining the cities with the mountains: warm without the August weight, the river terraces open, the high country comfortable. The second half starts to fill up.

July

Hot. Belgrade averages about 23 degrees with highs of 29 and spells when air off Africa pushes it past 35; the plain holds the heat and Nis in its valley is hotter still. The cities thin out towards the rivers and the mountains, which is their own recommendation: Zlatibor at a thousand metres is several degrees cooler and this is its best month. The EXIT festival fills Novi Sad and every bed in it for a week, on dates that move from year to year, so check before you book anything there in July.

August

The hottest and stillest month, with the same averages as July and less rain — about 55 millimetres and only six rainy days, the driest-feeling stretch of the year. The sunshine is excellent. Serbian families are on holiday, so the mountain resorts and the river beaches are at their fullest while Belgrade and Novi Sad are quieter than usual. The Guca trumpet festival falls in this month, on dates that move, and fills western Serbia completely for a few days.

September

The other best month, and the one we would pick. Averages drop to about 18 degrees with highs of 24, the heat has gone out of the day, the grill tables are outside, the Fruska Gora and southern vineyards are picking, and the crowds have gone home with the school year. The Danube is at its most agreeable. Prices come off their summer level. Everything that is open in summer is still open.

October

The quiet triumph of the Serbian year, and badly under-rated. About 13 degrees on average with highs around 18, dry by the standards of the spring, and the woods of Sumadija and Fruska Gora turning. It is the month for the monasteries, the Iron Gates and the wine country, and the cities are at their most comfortable for walking. Rooms are cheap again. The light on the Danube in the second half of the month is the best of the year.

November

The month the year closes down. Averages near 7 or 8 degrees, highs around 12, grey and damp with fog returning to the river basins and sunshine down to about three hours a day. Mountain places begin to shut between seasons and the ski lifts are not yet running. It is very cheap and it is fine for a city trip — Belgrade and Novi Sad do most of their living indoors anyway — but it is the least rewarding month for the countryside.

December

Cold, dark and often foggy, with averages near 2 degrees and the worst sunshine of the year. In the cities it is Advent, lights, markets and full kafanas, and it is inexpensive until the last week. In the mountains the ski season opens as the snow arrives, which varies from winter to winter, and the week between Western Christmas and the Orthodox New Year is the busiest and dearest in the resorts. Serbian Orthodox Christmas is not until 7 January, so 25 December is an ordinary working day for most of the country.

The weather, and what it does to a trip

Serbia is landlocked and the climate shows it. There is no sea to even out the year, so the country swings between cold air of Siberian origin in winter and warm air from the Mediterranean or North Africa in summer, and the changes can be sudden. Rainfall is modest — under 600 millimetres a year in northern Vojvodina, between 600 and 700 in central Serbia and Belgrade, over 700 in the west and above 1,000 in the mountains — and the wettest months are May and June, mostly in afternoon thunderstorms. August and September usually have the fewest rainy days of the year.

Belgrade’s averages, over the thirty years to 2020, give the shape of it. Mean temperatures by month in Celsius: January 1, February 3, March 7.5, April 12.6, May 17.3, June 21, July 22.8, August 23, September 18.2, October 13, November 7.6, December 2.2. Daytime highs run from about 4.5 in January to 29 in July and August; nights are below freezing from December to February. Rain by month in millimetres: 40, 35, 45, 50, 65, 75, 65, 55, 55, 50, 45, 50 — about 630 millimetres a year across 88 rainy days. Sunshine is the figure that surprises people: about two and a half hours a day in January against nine and a half in July, which is why the winter feels darker than the temperature suggests.

Three practical consequences. First, the mountains are not the plain: Sjenica in the Zlatibor district, at a thousand metres, averages about minus 3 in January against Belgrade’s 1, and about 17 in July against 23 — so a July trip that takes in both wants layers, and a January drive into the south-west is a winter drive. Second, summer heat on the plain is a planning constraint rather than a detail: Belgrade’s fortress, Novi Sad’s fortress and the Nis grill lanes are all early-morning and late-afternoon pleasures in August. Third, winter fog is the real winter problem, not cold: when cold air settles in the Danube and Morava basins it can sit for days, which matters more for a drive or a view than for a city break.

For your own dates, use a forecast. The figures above are thirty-year averages for one city and are here to show you the shape of the year, not to tell you what Thursday will be like.

Dates worth planning around

Serbian Orthodox Christmas, 7 January

The Serbian Orthodox Church keeps the Julian calendar, so Christmas falls on 7 January and 25 December is an ordinary working day for most of the country. Christmas Eve on the 6th, with the badnjak oak branch and the family meal, is the heart of it. Cities quiet down, intercity buses fill, and some businesses close for several days. A warm time to be in Serbia if you have anyone to spend it with, and a flat one if you do not.

Statehood Day, 15 February

A two-day national holiday marking the 1804 uprising and the 1835 constitution, centred on Orasac and Kragujevac. It is the one winter weekend that fills the mountain and spa resorts, so book ahead if you want Zlatibor, Kopaonik or Vrnjacka Banja around it.

Orthodox Easter, which moves

Calculated on the Julian reckoning, so it often falls a week or more after Western Easter and the date changes every year. It is the largest family holiday of the Serbian year: cities empty, the monasteries are busy, and rooms near a famous one go early. Check the year’s date before fixing a spring trip around it.

EXIT, Novi Sad, July

Several days of music inside Petrovaradin Fortress, and the largest festival in this part of Europe. It fills Novi Sad and everything within reach of it, at several times the usual room price, and the dates move from year to year. If you want it, book months ahead; if you do not, check the dates before booking anything in Novi Sad in July.

The Guca trumpet festival, August

Four days of Balkan brass in a small town in western Serbia, and one of the great unruly folk festivals of Europe — brass bands in competition, roast lamb, rakija and very little sleep. It fills Guca and every bed for a long way around, and the dates move. It is not a gentle event and it is unforgettable.

The ski weeks, and the slava

The mountain season runs from roughly late December into March, with New Year and the February school holidays the dearest and fullest weeks at Zlatibor and Kopaonik. Through the year you may also find a household closed or a host distracted by a slava — a family’s own patron saint’s day, kept more seriously than a birthday. If you are invited to one, go.

What it costs, and when

Serbia is one of the best-value countries in Europe for a visitor and the cheapest in its immediate neighbourhood. We do not print figures, because a number a reader plans around ought to come from the booking page rather than from us. The pattern, though, is stable and worth knowing.

The cities barely have a season. Belgrade, Novi Sad, Nis and Subotica are working places rather than resorts, and their rooms cost much the same in February as in July — which makes a Serbian winter city break unusually good value, and makes the cities the right answer in the expensive mountain weeks. The mountains invert that completely. Zlatibor and Kopaonik are dearest at New Year and in the February half-terms, dear again through August, and cheap in May, late October and November, when some places shut.

Two weeks spike sharply. Novi Sad during EXIT in July, and western Serbia around Guca in August, are the only times in the Serbian year when a bed is genuinely hard to find at any price. Both move from year to year, which is reason enough to check the dates before booking in those months.

Two things that are not room rates but shape a budget. The first is transport: intercity coaches and the Soko trains are very cheap, so a trip built on Belgrade, Novi Sad and Subotica costs almost nothing to move around, while the monasteries, the Iron Gates and the south-west effectively require a hire car and fuel. The second is that the dinar is the only legal tender whatever currency a listing quotes, and card networks have been disrupted during demonstrations — so keep some cash on you, which is the UK government’s own advice as well as ours.

Fishing here

The Serbian fishing year follows the same calendar as everything else. Spring high water on the Danube, the Sava and the Tisa is the big-fish season and June is the month the river anglers wait for; the summer low water is harder work and better early and late in the day; and autumn, with the water cooling and clearing, is the best stretch of the year on the reservoir lakes of the south-west — Uvac, Zlatar and Perucac. Winter fishing happens but the rivers can ice at the edges. Licences come from the angling association that manages each water, closed seasons and size limits differ between them, and national parks add their own rules, so arrange it locally rather than from abroad. Getting in: what applies, and what does not

Serbia is not in the European Union and not in the Schengen area, so it runs its own border. British passport holders may visit without a visa for up to 90 days in any 6-month period, with a passport valid for at least 90 days beyond the day they leave — that is what the UK government’s Serbia travel advice said when we read it on 26 November 2026. Many other nationalities including the United States are admitted visa-free for comparable periods; we could not open the American source on the day we wrote this, so check your own. Make sure your passport is actually stamped on entry — leaving without an entry stamp can mean charges of illegal immigration, a heavy fine and a prison sentence — and note that you must register with the local police within 24 hours, which a hotel does for you. If Kosovo is also in your plans, read the Kosovo paragraph in The Serbia Travel Guide first.

If your trip also crosses into Hungary, Croatia, Bulgaria, Greece or anywhere else in the Schengen area — and most flights from North America reach Serbia through a Schengen hub — the following applies to that crossing and not to Serbia:

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Serbia’s seasons — questions

What is the single best month to visit Serbia?

September, narrowly ahead of May and June. The heat has gone, the days are still long, the vineyards and orchards are picking, the summer crowds have gone home and prices have come off their peak. May is almost as good and greener; June is the best month if you want to combine the cities with the mountains, at the cost of being the wettest. If you are coming to ski, February is the most reliable month at Zlatibor and Kopaonik.

Is Serbia too hot in summer?

July and August are hot and sometimes very hot. Belgrade averages highs around 29 degrees and spells of air from Africa can push it past 35 or even 40, and Nis in its valley runs hotter again. Nothing closes and the evenings on the rivers are the best thing about the season, but the middle of the day is for shade. If you are travelling in those months, think about basing yourself at altitude — Zlatibor at a thousand metres is several degrees cooler and that is exactly why Serbs go there.

When is the cheapest time to come?

November to March in the cities, with January and February the cheapest of all, and March the cheapest month that also has decent daylight. The mountains invert this: their expensive weeks are New Year and the February half-terms, and their cheap ones are May, late October and November, when some places close altogether. Novi Sad in EXIT week and western Serbia around Guca are the two sharpest price spikes in the Serbian year.

When are EXIT and the Guca trumpet festival?

EXIT runs for several days at Petrovaradin Fortress in Novi Sad in July, and the Guca trumpet festival in western Serbia in August. Both move from year to year, so check the year's dates rather than relying on any website, including this one — which is why we print no dates. Both fill every bed for miles and both are worth planning either for or around, depending on what you want from the trip.

Does Serbia get snow?

Yes, regularly in the cities and reliably in the mountains. Belgrade sees frequent light snowfalls through the winter rather than deep lying snow, and the January average of about 1 degree sits either side of freezing. The mountains are a different matter: Zlatibor and Kopaonik have a ski season from roughly late December into March, with cover that varies considerably from winter to winter. Mountain roads in the south-west can be snowy from December, so a winter drive wants winter tyres and some caution.

What is the autumn really like?

It is the best-kept secret of the Serbian calendar. October is dry by the standards of the spring, mild, golden, and quiet, and it is the right month for the monasteries, the Iron Gates gorge and the wine country on Fruska Gora and in the south. Serbia's rainfall is not heavy — Belgrade gets about 630 millimetres a year, and autumn is not the wet season here that it is on the Adriatic coast. November is the one to avoid outside the cities.

Are the public holidays worth planning around?

A few are. Serbian Orthodox Christmas on 7 January and Orthodox Easter, which moves, are the big family occasions, when cities empty, intercity buses fill and some businesses shut for several days. Statehood Day on 15 February and the 1 May holidays are both two-day affairs that fill the mountain and spa resorts. Western Christmas on 25 December is an ordinary working day for most of the country.
