Luxembourg

The Luxembourg Travel Guide

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Luxembourg is a small country built on a dramatic piece of ground. The capital stands on a sandstone promontory with two river gorges cut around three sides of it, and what was once one of the strongest fortresses in Europe is now a city you walk through on ledges, bridges and lifts, with whole quarters sitting in the valley a hundred feet below the one you started in. Beyond it the Grand Duchy divides neatly into four: wooded Ardennes hills and castles in the north, a maze of sandstone ravines and waterfalls in the east, vineyards along the Moselle in the south-east, and the old iron country in the south, where the blast furnaces have been turned into a university. The whole of it is about eighty kilometres from end to end, all the public transport is free, and three languages are in use before you count the English you will hear in the capital.

Why visit Luxembourg

The capital is built on a gorge, and that is the whole point

Almost every European city on a hill flattens out once you are in it. Luxembourg City does not: the Alzette and the Pétrusse have cut two deep wooded valleys around the old town, so the city works on several levels at once, joined by bridges, cliff paths, tunnels and lifts. Walking the Chemin de la Corniche along the top of the old ramparts and then going down into Grund is the best hour in the country, and it costs nothing.

All the public transport is free

Luxembourg made buses, trams and trains free across the whole country in March 2020, for residents and visitors alike, with first class on the trains the only exception. There is no ticket, no app and nothing to validate. For a traveller this changes the shape of a trip: you take the tram three stops because you feel like it, you change your mind at a junction, and a day out to a castle costs you only the time.

Four quite different landscapes in eighty kilometres

The Ardennes in the north are high, wooded and dotted with castles. The Mullerthal in the east is a sandstone labyrinth of mossy ravines, waterfalls and squeezes between rock walls. The Moselle in the south-east is terraced vineyard country on a wide river. The Minett in the south is red iron ground, now a biosphere reserve with a university inside the old steelworks. You can see all four in three days.

The fortress is a UNESCO site you can get inside

The old quarters and fortifications of Luxembourg City have been on the UNESCO World Heritage list since 1994, and this is not a view-it-from-outside kind of monument. The Bock casemates are kilometres of gun galleries cut into the rock under the old town, open to walk through, with windows looking straight down into the valley. It is the clearest explanation of why anybody fought over this rock for four hundred years.

It is genuinely trilingual, and it shows

Luxembourgish, French and German are all official, and the country moves between them without apparent effort — the menu in French, the newspaper in German, the conversation at the next table in Luxembourgish, and the office upstairs working in English. Nearly half the population holds another country’s passport and some two hundred thousand people cross a border to work here each morning. For a country this small it is a remarkably cosmopolitan place.

It is where a good deal of Europe was signed

The Schengen Agreement that abolished Europe’s internal borders was signed in 1985 on a boat moored at Schengen, a village of a few hundred people on the Luxembourg Moselle where three countries meet; there is a small museum there now. Luxembourg was in at the founding of everything that became the European Union, and the Court of Justice, the Court of Auditors and the European Investment Bank sit on the Kirchberg plateau above the capital.

Luxembourg at a glance

Capital

Luxembourg City, on its promontory above the Alzette and the Pétrusse, with a little under a fifth of the country’s people in it. The old quarters and fortifications are a UNESCO World Heritage site; the European institutions and the modern office city are on the Kirchberg plateau across the Red Bridge; and the airport at Findel is about six kilometres out.

Money

The euro, from the beginning, having kept the Luxembourg franc in step with the Belgian one before that. Cards are taken everywhere a visitor goes and contactless is universal. Prices for food, drink and hotels sit at the higher end of the European range, and the one thing that costs nothing at all is getting about.

Language

Three official languages. Luxembourgish is the national language and a Germanic one in its own right; French is the language of administration, menus and shop signs; German is what most of the press is printed in. English is very widely spoken in the capital and generally understood elsewhere. Nobody expects a visitor to attempt Luxembourgish.

Borders

A founding member of what became the European Union, in the Schengen area from the start — the agreement was signed on its own Moselle bank — and in the euro from the beginning. The land borders with Belgium, Germany and France are internal Schengen crossings with no checks, and in several places you cross without noticing. Arrivals from outside Schengen are checked once, at the airport.

Getting about

Driving is on the right. All public transport within the country — train, tram and bus — has been free since March 2020, with first class on trains the only exception. The rail network runs out from the capital like spokes, so some cross-country journeys mean changing in the city; a car is unnecessary in Luxembourg City and useful for the Mullerthal and the wine road.

Shape of it

About 2,600 square kilometres and roughly two-thirds of a million people, which makes it one of Europe’s smallest sovereign states and one of its most densely employed. Close to half the residents are foreign nationals. It is a constitutional monarchy, a Grand Duchy, and the only one left in the world; Grand Duke Guillaume came to the throne in October 2025.

The regions

Four of them, and the furthest two are about an hour and a half apart by road.

The capital and its valleys

The old town on the rock, the casemates beneath it, the valley quarters of Grund, Clausen and Pfaffenthal in the gorge below, the station district across the Pétrusse, and the European institutions on the Kirchberg plateau. Nearly every visitor stays here, and with free transport reaching the whole country it is a perfectly sensible base for the entire trip.

The Mullerthal: Echternach and Little Switzerland

The eastern corner, where a sandstone plateau has been cut into ravines, overhangs and narrow squeezes, with beech woods on top and moss on everything. The marked Mullerthal Trail runs through the best of it and the Schiessentümpel waterfall under its little wooden bridge is the picture everyone takes. Echternach, at the German border, is the base.

  • Trier, over the German border — Roman gates and baths, under an hour away

The Ardennes and the north

Locally the Oesling: higher, colder, emptier and more wooded than the rest of the country, cut by the deep valleys of the Sure, the Our and the Clerve, and studded with castles. Vianden’s is the great one. Clervaux holds The Family of Man, Edward Steichen’s photographic exhibition, in its castle. This is also the ground the Battle of the Bulge was fought over, and the war is close to the surface here.

The Moselle and the Minett

Two quite different souths. Along the Moselle the slopes are terraced in vines, the river is wide and slow, and a string of small wine towns runs down to Schengen, where Luxembourg, Germany and France meet and the border agreement was signed. West of them the Minett is the old red iron country: pit heads, slag heaps grown over into a biosphere reserve, and the blast furnaces at Belval with a university campus built around them.

Things to do in Luxembourg

Walk the Corniche and drop into Grund

The Chemin de la Corniche runs along the top of the old ramparts with the Alzette gorge opening below it, and a nineteenth-century writer’s description of it as Europe’s most beautiful balcony has stuck. Follow it to the end, then take the steps or the lift down into Grund, the village-in-a-valley directly beneath the city, and walk back along the river. An hour, no ticket, and the best thing in the country.

Go into the Bock casemates

Under the old town are kilometres of galleries, stairs and gun chambers cut into the rock over three centuries of siege warfare, part of the UNESCO listing and open to walk through. They are cool, damp, low in places and not for anybody unhappy on stairs, and they explain the city better than any museum above ground. Check the season before you plan a day around them.

Climb to Vianden castle

The great restored castle of the Ardennes stands on a spur above a single steep street of slate-roofed houses, with a chairlift up the opposite hillside for the view back at it. Victor Hugo spent part of his exile in the house by the bridge, now a small museum. Come late in the day: the coaches leave in the afternoon and the town is a different place in the evening.

Walk a stretch of the Mullerthal Trail

The sandstone country around Echternach is cut into ravines narrow enough to turn sideways in, with waterfalls, moss, ferns and staircases bolted to the rock. The full trail runs well over a hundred kilometres in three loops, but the point is that any half-day section of it is worth the trip. Boots with grip: the rock is slippery even in dry weather, and reliably so after rain.

See the blast furnaces at Belval

Two of the old Esch-Belval blast furnaces have been left standing in the middle of the university quarter that replaced the steelworks, with the lecture halls, the national archive and a concert hall built around and under them. One of the furnaces can be climbed. It is the most interesting piece of industrial reuse in this part of Europe and almost no visitor to the capital knows it is twenty minutes away by free train.

Follow the wine road down to Schengen

The Luxembourg Moselle is Riesling, Pinot and crémant country, and the road from Grevenmacher through Remich to Schengen runs between terraced slopes and small family cellars that mostly welcome visitors. At the end of it the village of Schengen marks where the border agreement was signed on a boat in 1985, with a museum and three countries meeting at a point you can stand on.

When to go, and what it costs

Luxembourg has a mild, wet, thoroughly temperate climate with no extreme season and no month that is unusable. The walking country is at its best from May to early July, when the beech woods of the Mullerthal are in new leaf and the days are long, and again in September and early October, when the Moselle harvest is on and the Ardennes turn. July and August are the warmest and busiest and the castles fill with coach parties by late morning. Winter is grey and damp more often than it is crisp, the Ardennes get some snow, and the capital puts on Christmas markets across its squares. The dates to know are National Day on 23 June, when the capital celebrates the evening before and takes the day itself off, and the Schueberfouer, the big funfair that fills the Glacis ground from late August into early September and takes most of the city’s hotel rooms with it.

Fishing here

Fishing in Luxembourg means the Sure, the Our and the Moselle and the reservoir at the Upper Sure lake — trout and grayling in the Ardennes rivers, coarse fish and pike in the slower water. A permit is required, the rules differ between the state licence and the local lease on a given stretch, and the frontier rivers have arrangements of their own, so it is best arranged locally rather than puzzled out from abroad. Travelling in your later years

Free transport takes the friction out of a trip

No tickets, no machines, no working out zones in a language you do not read, and no reason not to take the bus the last half mile. For anybody who finds the logistics of European travel more tiring than the travel itself, this is a country that removes most of them, and it applies to trains and trams as much as to the local buses.

The capital is vertical, and there are lifts

The gap between the old town and the valley quarters is a hundred feet of cliff, and the old ways down are long flights of steps. There are two public lifts — one from the Plateau du Saint-Esprit down to Grund, one at Pfaffenthal with a glass cabin and a view — and they are free like everything else. Knowing they exist is the difference between enjoying the gorge and avoiding it.

The distances are very short

Nowhere in the Grand Duchy is more than about an hour and a half from the capital, and most of the places in this guide are well under an hour. You can see the country from one hotel, unpack once and still be back each evening, which is not true of most European itineraries and matters more the less you enjoy a long day in a vehicle.

Where the ground is difficult

The casemates are stairs, low ceilings and uneven rock; the Mullerthal paths involve ladders, steps and slippery sandstone; Vianden’s street up to the castle is genuinely steep, though a shuttle runs up it in season and the chairlift gives you the view without the climb. None of that rules the country out — the old town, Kirchberg, the Moselle promenades and Esch are all easy going — but it is worth choosing deliberately.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Luxembourg itself is about as straightforward as Europe gets: a founding member of what became the European Union, in the Schengen area from the beginning — the agreement that created it was signed on the Luxembourg bank of the Moselle in 1985 — and in the euro from the start. The land borders with Belgium, Germany and France are internal Schengen crossings with no controls at all, and on the Moselle bridges and the Ardennes back roads you can cross without noticing. If you fly in, the single check is at Findel. There is no separate permission of any kind to visit the Grand Duchy.

One practical point that catches people out: because all three neighbours are a short ride away, many visitors base themselves here and spend days in Trier, Metz or the Belgian Ardennes. That is perfectly easy, but the free transport covers the Luxembourg side of such a journey rather than the foreign end of it, so check what your ticket needs to cover before you set off.

Luxembourg — questions

How many days do I need in Luxembourg?

Two days covers Luxembourg City properly, which is what most visitors come for, and it is a city that rewards a slow walk rather than a checklist. Four gets you the capital plus the Mullerthal or the Ardennes with a car or a bus. A week is enough to see the whole country without hurrying, because the Grand Duchy is about eighty kilometres top to bottom and nothing in it is more than about an hour and a half from anything else. It also works very well as three or four days bolted on to a trip through Belgium, France or the Rhineland.

Is public transport really free?

Yes, within the country. Since March 2020 Luxembourg has made buses, trams and trains free across the national territory for everybody, residents and visitors alike, with no ticket and nothing to show. First class on the trains is the exception and still has to be paid for. The free travel applies inside Luxembourg rather than to the foreign end of a cross-border journey, so if your train starts in Trier, Metz or Brussels, check with the operator what the fare covers. It is the single thing that most changes how a visit here works: you simply get on.

Is Luxembourg expensive?

The hotels are, and that is the honest headline. Luxembourg City is a banking and European-institutions town with a small amount of hotel stock and a weekday business trade that sets the price, so midweek rooms in the centre cost more than the same room would in most of Belgium or eastern France. The compensations are real: transport costs nothing, many of the museums are modestly priced or free on certain evenings, and weekend rates in the city are often far below the weekday ones because the business travellers have gone home. We do not print figures because they move, but that weekday and weekend split is reliable and worth planning around.

What language should I speak?

Luxembourgish is the national language and what you will hear between locals. French and German are both official alongside it, and in practice French is the language of shops, restaurants and menus while German dominates the newspapers. English is very widely spoken in the capital, where a large share of the working population is foreign, and well enough understood in the towns. A visitor needs no Luxembourgish at all, though moien for hello and merci are always well received.

Do I need a car?

Not for Luxembourg City, which is compact, walkable and has a tram and buses that cost nothing. For the rest of the country it depends on your patience: trains and buses reach Echternach, Vianden, Clervaux, Esch-sur-Alzette and the Moselle towns perfectly well and cost nothing, but they run to a timetable and the connections through the valleys can be slow. If you want to walk the Mullerthal trails or follow the wine road at your own pace, hire a car for those days. Driving is on the right.

Where exactly is Luxembourg, and what is it next to?

The Grand Duchy sits between Belgium, Germany and France, at the point where the Ardennes run down into the Moselle valley. Brussels, Cologne, Strasbourg and Paris are all within a few hours by train or road, and Trier, the oldest city in Germany, is under an hour from Echternach. The country is landlocked, roughly 2,600 square kilometres, and small enough that a visitor based in the capital can reach any corner of it and be back for dinner.

Is it a good trip for a first visit to Europe?

It is one of the gentlest. The distances are tiny, the public transport is free and punctual, the standard of hotels and restaurants is high, English goes a long way in the capital, and the country is safe and easy to move around alone. What it is not is a week-long holiday on its own for most people; pair it with Belgium, the Rhineland or Alsace and it becomes an unusually rewarding few days rather than a thin one.
