Kuressaare, Saaremaa

Where to Stay in Kuressaare

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Kuressaare is the one town on Saaremaa, Estonia’s largest island, and it is built around a castle that has no real rival in this part of Europe — a squat grey dolomite keep inside a water-filled moat, still roofed and still whole. The town around it is small, low and arcaded, with a square, a park, a scatter of good restaurants and a run of spa hotels that keep the place working through a winter in which the ferries thin out and the island goes quiet. Almost everything a visitor needs is within a ten-minute walk. Almost everything else on Saaremaa — the cliff at Panga, the meteorite crater at Kaali, the windmills, the juniper heaths — needs a car.

The centre, between the square and the castle

Most centralWalk to the castleWhere the restaurants are

Kuressaare is small enough that “central” means almost all of it. The town square with its arcaded town hall sits a few minutes’ walk from the castle gate, and the streets between the two hold most of the restaurants, the shops and a good share of the beds. This is the obvious place to stay: you can walk to the castle, the park and the harbour, and you are not dependent on a car for dinner. In July the town is busy with Estonian and Finnish holidaymakers and rooms go early; for the rest of the year it is very quiet indeed.

The spa hotels by the park

Pools and saunasOpen in winterA few minutes from the square

Kuressaare has made itself Estonia’s spa town, and the larger hotels with pools, saunas and treatment wings stand around the castle park and the approach to the sea. They are what keeps the place open and staffed through a winter in which very little else is, and they are used as much by Estonians and Finns on a few days’ break as by foreign visitors. If you are coming between October and April, this is effectively where you will stay, and that is no hardship.

Towards the harbour and the water

Sea airQuietShort walk or a bike ride

South of the castle the land runs out through reeds and low shore to the small harbour at Roomassaare, where the yachts and the island boats tie up. There is little formal accommodation along here and a scatter of guesthouses and apartments, and what you get is quiet, flat walking and the particular light that comes off a shallow Baltic bay. It is a pleasant twenty minutes or so into town on foot, longer if you dawdle, and most people who stay out this way have a car or a bicycle.

Out on the island

Manor houses and farm staysCliffs, craters and windmillsA car is essential

Saaremaa is a large island and most of it is not Kuressaare: there are juniper heaths, a limestone cliff at Panga, the preserved windmills at Angla, the meteorite crater at Kaali and a long scatter of small churches and manor houses. Accommodation outside the town is farm stays, manor hotels and summer cottages, and it is very good indeed if you want quiet. Getting around Kuressaare

On foot and by bicycle

The town is flat, small and quiet, and you will walk all of it without thinking about it. Bicycles are the right way to see the near parts of the island — the shore road, the harbour and the heath behind the town — and are easy to hire in summer.

Getting to Saaremaa by ferry

The usual route is the ferry from Virtsu on the mainland to Kuivastu on the island of Muhu, which takes under half an hour, and then a causeway across to Saaremaa and a drive of roughly an hour to Kuressaare. Sailings are frequent in summer and thinner in winter, and in high season vehicle places are worth reserving in advance; in a hard winter the crossing can be disrupted by ice, and an official ice road across the strait is sometimes opened at short notice and closed just as quickly.

By bus and by air

Direct coaches run from Tallinn and from Pärnu and drive straight onto the ferry, which makes arriving without a car entirely practical if you intend to stay in the town. There is also a small airport outside Kuressaare with flights from Tallinn; it is a short hop and the service is limited, so check what is running for your dates rather than assuming.

Driving

Within Kuressaare a car is unnecessary. For the rest of Saaremaa it is close to essential: the distances are real, the bus network outside the town is sparse, and the windmills, the crater and the cliffs are not walkable from anywhere. Driving is on the right, dipped headlights are required day and night all year, and the island roads carry deer and elk, which is a genuine hazard at dusk.

When to come, and what it costs

Kuressaare has the sharpest season of any town in this book. From late June to the middle of August the island fills with Estonian and Finnish holidaymakers, the castle park has concerts and the opera days, the restaurants are open and the rooms are booked out well ahead. May, early June and September are mild, light and much quieter, and they are the Guild’s own choice for the island — the juniper heaths and the coast are at their best without anybody on them. From November to March the town is very quiet, several restaurants close, the ferries run less often and the weather can genuinely cut the island off for a day; what stays open, and stays busy, are the spa hotels.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Estonia itself is straightforward: in the European Union since 2004, in the Schengen area since the end of 2007 and on the euro since the beginning of 2011. An arrival from another Schengen country has no passport control at all, and an arrival from outside it is a single check at the airport you land in. The land borders with Latvia and the sea crossing from Finland are internal Schengen movements. The eastern border with Russia is an external EU frontier and the arrangements at its crossings have changed more than once in recent years, so if any part of your plan depends on one, check with the Estonian Police and Border Guard Board before you book rather than trusting anything written earlier, including this page.

One point of confusion worth clearing up: Estonian e-Residency is a digital identity for running a business online. It is not residency, it is not a visa and it gives you no right to enter or stay. If you are coming as a visitor, the Schengen rules above are the ones that apply to you.

Kuressaare — questions

How do I get to Saaremaa?

By ferry from Virtsu on the mainland to Kuivastu on Muhu, then over the causeway and on by road to Kuressaare, which is about an hour’s drive from the ferry. The crossing itself is under half an hour. Direct coaches from Tallinn and Pärnu drive onto the ferry, so you do not need a car to arrive. There is also a small airport outside Kuressaare with flights from Tallinn. In summer, reserve a vehicle place on the ferry rather than turning up and hoping.

Is the castle worth the trip?

It is the reason most people come, and it earns it. Kuressaare’s episcopal castle is the best-preserved medieval castle in the Baltic states — a square keep of grey dolomite inside a later bastioned earthwork, with its moat still full of water and its interiors largely intact rather than reconstructed. You can walk the vaulted halls, the chapel and the defensive passages, and the island museum inside it is good. Allow a couple of hours, more if the weather sends you indoors.

Do I need a car on the island?

In Kuressaare, no. Anywhere else on Saaremaa, effectively yes. The island is large, the bus network outside the town is thin, and the things people come to see — the Kaali meteorite crater, the windmills at Angla, the cliff at Panga, the small churches — are spread across it with nothing walkable in between. If you are staying two nights in the town and visiting the castle, you can manage without. If you want the island itself, hire a car or bring one on the ferry.

What is there to do in winter?

Spas, mostly, and that is not a small answer — it is why Kuressaare has a winter economy at all. The castle stays open, the town is handsome under snow and almost empty, and the quiet is the attraction. Be realistic about the limits: several restaurants close for the season, ferry sailings are less frequent and can be disrupted by ice or weather, and a short January day does not leave much time for driving around the island. Check what is open before you book, and leave slack around your ferry.

Is Saaremaa better than Hiiumaa?

They are different in scale rather than in kind. Saaremaa is much the larger and has the one real town, the castle, the spa hotels and most of the sights, so it suits a first visit and anyone who wants somewhere to eat in the evening. Hiiumaa, to the north, is smaller, emptier and more thinly served, and people who already know the islands often prefer it for exactly that reason. If you have one trip and are coming for the castle, come here.
