Subotica

Where to Stay in Subotica

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Subotica sits ten kilometres from the Hungarian border at the top of the Vojvodina plain, and it is the most surprising city in Serbia. It grew rich at the turn of the twentieth century as a Hungarian provincial capital and spent the money on architecture: the City Hall, the Synagogue, the Raichle Palace and a long list of lesser buildings, all in the Hungarian Secession version of Art Nouveau, with coloured tile, floral ironwork and Zsolnay ceramics. Nothing else in the country looks remotely like it. It is a bilingual city — Hungarian is spoken as much as Serbian — it is flat, it is quiet, and Lake Palic with its own spa park is eight kilometres east. A night or two, easily reached on the Soko train from Belgrade or Novi Sad.

The main square and the City Hall

Most centralFlatThe architecture

Everything in Subotica happens around the square the City Hall stands on, a vast 1910 pile of brick, tile and stained glass that is the reason most people come. The Korzo pedestrian street runs off it, with the cafes, the theatre and the Raichle Palace a few minutes away. Staying here is level walking to all of it, and the hotels on and around the square are the city's main ones. Quiet by the standards of any Serbian city; the square itself is a daytime place.

The Synagogue quarter

QuieterResidentialShort walk in

A few streets north-west of the square, around the 1902 Synagogue — one of the finest Art Nouveau synagogues anywhere, restored over many years and now open again. The streets here are low, leafy and residential, with villas in the same Secession manner and apartments and small guesthouses to rent. It is five to ten minutes' walk to the square and noticeably cheaper. A good choice for a second night.

The station end

Early trainsCheapestFunctional

The railway station is on the eastern side of the centre, and the Soko trains to Novi Sad and Belgrade go from it. The quarter around it is plain and a little trafficky, with the cheapest rooms in the city, and the square is about ten minutes on foot. Worth it only if you are travelling early or counting every dinar.

Lake Palic

Spa parkLakesideLevel walking

Eight kilometres east, a shallow soda lake with an Art Nouveau spa complex around it: the water tower, the Grand Terrace, the music pavilion and a long park promenade along the shore, all from the same era as the City Hall. There are lakeside hotels and guesthouses, a zoo, and flat walking and cycling all the way round. It is calmer and greener than the city and a bus or a short taxi gets you in. Good for several nights, and particularly good if the city's pavements are more walking than you want.

Getting around Subotica

On foot

Subotica is flat and the centre is small: the square, the Korzo, the Synagogue and the theatre are all within ten minutes of each other on level pavement. It is one of the easiest Serbian towns to walk, and almost everything worth seeing in the city is in that compass.

Buses to Palic and the border

City and suburban buses run out to Lake Palic regularly and take about fifteen minutes, and there are frequent services to the Hungarian border and on to Szeged. The bus station is near the railway station on the eastern side of the centre.

The Soko train

The rebuilt main line puts Subotica on the fast service through Novi Sad to Belgrade, which has made the city far easier to reach than it used to be. Trains are modern and cheap; buy at the station or online. The through service across the border to Budapest has been delayed more than once, so check it with the operator rather than relying on a published plan.

Taxis and cycling

Taxis are cheap and the simple way out to Palic in the evening. The ground is flat enough that cycling is a genuine option, and the lake circuit and the road out to it are popular with local riders.

Crossing to Hungary

Szeged is about forty-five minutes away including the border, and the crossing is a real one — Serbia is outside the Schengen area, so expect a passport check in both directions, and expect queues at holiday periods. If you are driving a hire car, confirm with the hire company before you book that it may be taken out of Serbia.

When to come

Subotica is on the open plain and gets the sharper end of the continental climate: hot summers, cold winters, and more fog than the hills. May, June and September are the best of it, with the cafes on the Korzo out and the lake park at Palic in full leaf. July and August are hot and the lake is the place to be. October is mild, golden and very quiet. November to February is cold with grey, foggy spells, and Vojvodina is the driest part of Serbia, with under 600 millimetres of rain a year on this border; the architecture looks well in winter light and the rooms are cheap. The local date is the Palic European Film Festival in July, which moves from year to year, and the city keeps Hungarian as well as Serbian holidays in practice.

Getting in: what applies, and what does not

Serbia is not in the European Union and not in the Schengen area, so it runs its own border. British passport holders may visit without a visa for up to 90 days in any 6-month period, with a passport valid for at least 90 days beyond the day they leave — that is what the UK government’s Serbia travel advice said when we read it on 26 November 2026. Many other nationalities including the United States are admitted visa-free for comparable periods; we could not open the American source on the day we wrote this, so check your own. Two Serbian rules catch people out: make sure your passport is actually stamped on entry, because leaving without an entry stamp can mean charges of illegal immigration, a heavy fine and a prison sentence; and you must register with the local police within 24 hours, which a hotel or registered apartment does for you automatically. If Kosovo is also in your plans, read the Kosovo paragraph in The Serbia Travel Guide before you book anything — entering Serbia from Kosovo without an existing Serbian entry stamp usually means being refused.

If your trip also crosses into Hungary, Croatia, Bulgaria, Greece or anywhere else in the Schengen area — and most flights from North America reach Serbia through a Schengen hub — the following applies to that crossing and not to Serbia:

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Subotica — questions

Why come to Subotica?

For the architecture, which is unlike anything else in Serbia: the 1910 City Hall, the 1902 Synagogue and the Raichle Palace are three of the best Hungarian Secession buildings anywhere, and there is a long tail of smaller ones. Add a bilingual Serbian-Hungarian border city that almost no foreign visitor stops in, the Art Nouveau spa park at Lake Palic eight kilometres out, and flat easy walking, and it earns a night or two.

Is the Synagogue open?

It reopened after a long restoration and is one of the few surviving great Art Nouveau synagogues in Europe. Opening arrangements for a building like this change, and it is also used for concerts, so check locally on the day rather than relying on any website's hours, including ours — which is why we do not print them.

What is there at Lake Palic?

A shallow soda lake eight kilometres east with an Art Nouveau spa complex laid out around its western end — the water tower, the Grand Terrace, the music pavilion — and a long level promenade through the park along the shore. There is a zoo, lakeside hotels and guesthouses, and flat walking and cycling right round. Buses from the city take about fifteen minutes.

Can I get to Hungary from here?

Easily. Szeged is about forty-five minutes by road including the border crossing, and there are frequent buses. Remember that this is a real international border: Serbia is outside the Schengen area and the European Union, so passports are checked both ways and the queues at holiday periods can be long. Check with your hire company before taking a car across.

How do I get here from Belgrade?

The Soko fast train on the rebuilt Belgrade–Budapest main line, via Novi Sad, which is much the easiest way and inexpensive. Intercity coaches also run. By car it is the E75 motorway most of the way. The through train on to Budapest has been repeatedly delayed, so do not build a plan on it without checking with the operator first.

Is Hungarian or Serbian spoken here?

Both, genuinely. Subotica has one of the largest Hungarian communities in Serbia and Hungarian is an official language of Vojvodina alongside Serbian, Croatian, Slovak, Romanian and Rusyn. Signs are often bilingual, the Serbian is written in both Cyrillic and Latin, and English is spoken by younger people and in the hotels.
