Uruguay — the river, the ocean and the cattle country

Uruguay Travel Guide — Where to Stay, River and Coast

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Uruguay is the smallest country in South America after Suriname, and the one that asks the least of a traveller. Montevideo holds half the population and all of the city life. Colonia del Sacramento, two and a half hours west, is a Portuguese smuggling town with a World Heritage quarter and an hour’s ferry to Buenos Aires. Punta del Este, two hours east, is where the brown river finally becomes green Atlantic. Behind it all is cattle country, Tannat wine within an hour of the capital, and a coast that fills in January and empties in May. Below is each region, where to sleep in it, and the two things that actually shape a trip here — the upside-down seasons, and reading the crime advice for Montevideo properly rather than from a headline.

And the honest word on the rest of the country. Uruguay is small — you can drive the whole Atlantic coast in a day — and a great deal of it has no hotel inventory worth linking. The thermal spa towns on the Río Uruguay in the north-west, Salto and Paysandú, are a real domestic holiday and almost unknown to foreign visitors. Cabo Polonio, out on the dunes in Rocha, has no mains electricity and is reached by four-wheel-drive over sand; that is the point of it, and it is not a place we will pretend has a bookable hotel list. José Ignacio east of Punta del Este is a fishing village that became expensive. We have written books for the three places where a visitor actually needs help choosing a bed, and we would rather do three properly than ten thinly.

Why visit Uruguay

It is the calm one

Uruguay sits between Argentina and Brazil and behaves like neither. It is small, stable, largely middle-class and quiet, and travellers who have found the rest of the continent hard work tend to exhale here.

A coast with two moods

The water changes from brown river to green ocean somewhere around Punta del Este. West of that you get warm, flat, shallow bathing; east of it, surf, dunes and lagoons all the way to Brazil.

Beef and Tannat

The asado is a national institution rather than a tourist show, and the wine country in Canelones just outside Montevideo has made Tannat its own grape. Both are everyday, not luxury.

The longest carnival anywhere

Uruguay claims the longest carnival season in the world, about forty days from late January, with drumming troupes in the Montevideo streets and a parade season that belongs to residents more than visitors.

Uruguay at a glance

Where it is. The south-east corner of South America, on the Atlantic and the north bank of the Río de la Plata, with Argentina across the water to the west and Brazil overland to the north.

Size and people. About three and a half million people, and close to half of them live in Montevideo and its suburbs. After the capital the next towns are small, which is why the road network matters more than the domestic flight network.

Language and money. Spanish, and the Uruguayan peso. Cards work in cities and on the coast; carry notes for small towns, buses and markets. Tourists paying by foreign card have in recent years received a value-added tax rebate on hotel and restaurant bills — it is applied automatically at the till where it applies, the terms are set by the tax authority and they change, so treat it as a pleasant surprise rather than a budget line.

Seasons are upside down. This is the southern hemisphere. Summer is December to February, winter is June to August, and the Atlantic coast empties completely out of season.

Getting around. Long-distance buses are good, frequent and cheap, and they run from the Tres Cruces terminal in Montevideo. The coast road east to Punta del Este takes a little over two hours. Colonia is about two and a half hours west, or a ferry hop from Buenos Aires.

The three Uruguays

Small country, three quite different trips.

The river — Montevideo, Colonia and the Plata shore

The west and south, facing Argentina across water so wide and so brown that it reads as sea. This is where the cities are, where the history is, and where the wine country sits. The bathing here is warm, shallow and unglamorous, and the interest is urban.

The ocean — Punta del Este east to the Brazilian border

From Punta del Este the coast turns to real Atlantic: surf beaches, pine and eucalyptus, sand dunes, sea lions and a string of lagoons behind the shore. It runs through La Barra, José Ignacio, La Paloma, Cabo Polonio and Punta del Diablo to Chuy on the frontier. In January it is the holiday coast of two large neighbouring countries. In May it is almost shut.

The interior — cattle country and the thermal north

Inland is rolling grass, cattle, estancias that take guests, and in the far north-west a line of hot-spring resorts along the Río Uruguay at Salto and Paysandú. Almost no foreign visitors go, which is either a reason to or a reason not to.

Things to do in Uruguay

  • Walk the Rambla in Montevideo. The waterfront promenade runs for miles along the south of the city, past beach after city beach. Sunday afternoon, with a thermos and a gourd of mate under every arm, is the city at its most itself.
  • Eat at the Mercado del Puerto. A nineteenth-century iron market hall by the port in Ciudad Vieja, now a room full of wood-fired grills. Busy at lunch, touristy, and still worth it.
  • Spend a night inside the old quarter at Colonia. The cobbled Barrio Histórico is small enough to cross in ten minutes and it is a different place once the day-trip ferries have gone home.
  • Drive the coast road east. Punta del Este to Punta del Diablo, stopping where the car wants to. The lighthouse at José Ignacio, the dunes at Cabo Polonio, the sea lion colony off the rocks.
  • Taste Tannat in Canelones. The wine country starts within an hour of Montevideo and most of the bodegas are family-sized. Book ahead; several open by appointment only.
  • Sit in the hot springs at Salto. Thermal water, a long way from anywhere, and a holiday Uruguayans take and foreigners do not.

Best time to visit & getting there

The short version: late October to mid December, and March, are the best weeks of the year — warm enough to swim by the end of them, and without the crowds and the prices of January. January and February are hot, lively and full. June to August is cold, grey and wet on the coast and perfectly pleasant in Montevideo if a city is what you came for.

Montevideo’s airport at Carrasco is the main way in, with connections through São Paulo, Panama City, Buenos Aires, Santiago and Lima; there is also a seasonal airport at Punta del Este. Many visitors arrive by water instead: the fast ferries from Buenos Aires to Colonia and direct to Montevideo are a genuine alternative to flying, and Montevideo is a scheduled call for cruise ships through the southern summer.

The full month-by-month picture, including sea temperatures and the dates to avoid, is in Best Time to Visit Uruguay.

Fishing here

Uruguay is a fishing country in a quiet way, and the Guild has no book of its own on it yet. The Atlantic shore from Punta del Este east to the Brazilian border is surfcasting water — corvina and bluefish from the beaches and the rock points, best in the cooler months when the holiday crowds have gone. The coastal lagoons behind that shore, Laguna de Rocha and Laguna Garzón among them, are shallow, birdy and fished from small boats. Inland and north, the Río Uruguay above Salto is the dorado river, which is the fish most anglers come for; the dams and the rapids there concentrate it. Sport fishing is administered nationally by DINARA, the fisheries authority under the agriculture ministry, and the rules on seasons and size change, so take a local operator’s word on what may be kept rather than ours. The rest of the shelf is in The Guild Fishing Library.

Travelling in your later years

Uruguay is one of the kinder South American countries for an older traveller, and the reasons are structural rather than sentimental. There is no altitude anywhere — the highest point in the country is barely over five hundred metres, so nothing here asks of your heart and lungs what Cusco or Quito do. There are no tropical disease worries of the kind that shape an Amazon trip. Distances are short and the roads are good. Medical care in Montevideo is of a decent standard and concentrated in one city, which is exactly what you want if something goes wrong.

What to watch instead. The old quarters at Colonia and in Ciudad Vieja are cobbled, uneven and hard on a stick or a wheel; the Rambla and the Punta del Este beachfronts are flat and smooth and make a better daily walk. Summer heat on the Atlantic coast is real and the shade is thin. Street crime in central Montevideo is a pickpocket and bag-snatching problem rather than a violent one, and the sensible answer — a ride app rather than a hailed taxi, nothing in a back pocket, no phone in the hand — is the same answer as in any city. And the shoulder months we recommend for value are also the months with the gentlest temperatures, which is a happy coincidence for anyone who does not want to walk a city in thirty degrees.

Getting in: what to expect

British and United States tourist passport holders do not need a visa for a stay of up to ninety days, and the passport needs to be valid at the time you enter. Neither the UK Foreign Office nor the US State Department lists a mandatory vaccination for entry. Those are the rules as we read them on the official pages, and entry rules are exactly the sort of thing that changes without warning: check your own government’s current page for your own passport before you book, not a guide.

On safety, the honest position is that no part of Uruguay carries advice against travel, and that this is not the same as nothing to think about. The UK Foreign Office describes street crime in Montevideo — bag-snatching, pickpocketing and muggings that are sometimes armed — names break-ins and smash-and-grab thefts from cars stopped at junctions, and advises against walking alone in the downtown and port areas, particularly at night and at weekends, and around the Tres Cruces bus terminal. The US State Department asks visitors to exercise increased caution because of crime. Read both for yourself: they are written for your passport and updated far more often than any guidebook.

Uruguay — questions

How many days do I need in Uruguay?

Five to seven nights is the honest answer for a first visit, and it does not need to be more. Two nights in Montevideo, two in Colonia del Sacramento and two or three in Punta del Este covers the country that most visitors come for, with short road or ferry hops between them. Add two or three nights if you want to drive the coast east of Punta del Este properly, which is the part of Uruguay that rewards an unhurried car. Many people see Uruguay as a few days attached to Buenos Aires, and that works well: the ferry crossing to Colonia takes about an hour on the fast boats.

Do I need a visa for Uruguay?

British and United States tourist passport holders can visit without a visa for up to ninety days, and the passport must be valid when you arrive. No vaccination is listed as mandatory for entry by either the UK Foreign Office or the US State Department. Entry rules change, so read your own government's current page for your own passport rather than relying on any guide, including this one.

Is Uruguay safe to visit?

No part of Uruguay is under advice against travel, which makes it one of the easier countries on the continent, and that is not the same as nothing to think about. The UK Foreign Office describes street crime in Montevideo including bag-snatching, pickpocketing and muggings that are sometimes armed; it names break-ins and smash-and-grab thefts from cars stopped at junctions; and it advises against walking alone in the downtown and port areas, especially after dark and at weekends, and around the Tres Cruces bus terminal. The US State Department asks visitors to exercise increased caution because of crime. The practical answer is ordinary city sense: a ride app or a hotel taxi rather than a hailed one, nothing valuable in sight in a parked car, and the Rambla rather than the back streets after dark.

When is the cheapest time to go?

The Atlantic coast runs on a short, fierce season, so the price swing between January and May on the same room is larger here than almost anywhere else we write about. The cheapest months are the winter ones, June to August, when much of Punta del Este simply closes. The best value for an actual holiday is the shoulder: late October to mid December, and March. Those weeks are warm, the sea is swimmable by the end of them, and the beds cost a fraction of what they do in the second half of January.

Should I base myself in Montevideo or Punta del Este?

Montevideo if you want a city, a working port, museums, markets, the wine country within an hour and something open in every month of the year. Punta del Este if you want a beach holiday and you are coming in the southern summer. In winter the choice makes itself, because Montevideo carries on and the resort does not. If you have a week, do not choose: they are a little over two hours apart by road and the drive is easy.

Can I use US dollars, and what about the tax rebate?

The currency is the Uruguayan peso and you should expect to pay in it. Dollars are sometimes accepted on the coast and in the resort trade, usually at a rate that does not favour you. Cards are widely taken in Montevideo, Colonia and Punta del Este, and cash is what you want for small towns, buses, markets and tips. Tourists paying hotel and restaurant bills with a foreign card have in recent years had value-added tax rebated automatically, which is a real saving where it applies, but the terms are set by the tax authority and have changed more than once, so do not build a budget on a figure printed here.
