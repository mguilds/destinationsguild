Where to Stay in Cartagena

Where to Stay in Cartagena — Best Hotels & Areas

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated August 2026

Cartagena de Indias is the finest surviving colonial city on the Caribbean, and the choice of where to sleep in it matters more than in almost any other place we write about. Get it right and you step out of your door into a lit, flat, walkable seventeenth-century town where everything is five minutes away. Get it wrong and you spend the week in a taxi.

Four areas matter. The Centro — the walled city proper — and the quieter San Diego quarter inside the same walls hold the loveliest hotels in Colombia, almost all of them converted colonial houses round courtyards. Getsemaní, immediately outside the clock-tower gate, was the working quarter and is now the restaurant and music district: cheaper, younger, livelier, and noisier after midnight. Bocagrande is a peninsula of modern towers on a grey-sand beach across the bay — good value, sea views, cold air conditioning, and a ten-minute taxi from the charm. And Barú, an hour south, is where the white sand and the all-inclusive resorts are.

The one thing to know before you choose a hotel. Cartagena is hot — around 88°F with heavy humidity, every month of the year, and it does not cool off at night. That single fact should shape your booking more than the photographs do. Prize cold, quiet air conditioning and a pool or a rooftop over a fourth courtyard fountain; ask about lifts if stairs are difficult, because many of the beautiful colonial hotels have none; and plan to be out early and late and indoors in the middle of the day. Cartagena also sits south of the main Atlantic hurricane track and is very rarely affected, which is a genuine advantage over most of the Caribbean in September and October.

Luxury

Cartagena’s grandest addresses are converted convents and mansions inside the walls — and both of these have the lifts and the cold air that the prettier small hotels sometimes lack.

Sofitel Legend Santa Clara Cartagena

A Poor Clares convent of 1621 turned into the city’s grand hotel, wrapped round a vast palm courtyard with a proper swimming pool in it — a rarity inside the walls, where most hotels manage a plunge pool at best. It is the address other hotels measure themselves against, and for older travellers it has the practical virtues to match the beauty: lifts, level corridors, genuinely cold rooms and a spa. The modern wing rooms are less romantic than the convent ones but larger and quieter; ask which you are being sold.

What You Need to Know Before You Book

The four things that decide whether a week in Cartagena is a delight or a slog.

The heat is the whole problem, and it is solvable

88°F all yearPlan the day

There is no cool season in Cartagena. Highs sit around 88°F with humidity to match in every month, and the nights stay in the high seventies. Visitors who treat it as a Mediterranean city and set out to walk at one in the afternoon have a miserable time; visitors who adopt the local rhythm have a wonderful one.

The rhythm: out early — the walls and the old streets are at their best between six and nine, and almost empty. Back to the hotel from about eleven to four for the pool, a long lunch and a rest. Out again from five for the ramparts, the sunset and dinner, which Colombians eat late. Carry water, keep to the shaded side of the street, and do not plan two outdoor sights in one afternoon.

What to look for in a room: air conditioning that is cold and quiet, not decorative; a pool or a rooftop you can actually use; and windows that are not directly over a bar. February and August are the breeziest, most comfortable months.

Which district, honestly

Centro walkableBocagrande is not

Centro and San Diego (inside the walls): the reason to come. Flat, lit, patrolled, and everything within a fifteen-minute walk. The most beautiful hotels and the highest prices, and horse-drawn carriages and music under the window at night in the busier streets. Ask for a courtyard-facing room.

Getsemaní: five minutes further out, half the price, and now the best eating in the city. It is a real neighbourhood with a lively square, and it is noisy late; the back streets are best avoided after dark. Excellent for a traveller who wants character over polish.

Bocagrande and El Laguito: modern towers, sea views, cold air, lifts, family pools, and a ten- to fifteen-minute taxi to the old city. If step-free access or a big pool matters more than colonial charm, stay here without apology.

Barú and the Rosario Islands: an hour or more away, and where the white sand is. A stay in its own right, not a base for the city.

Taxis, apps and what a fair fare is

No metersAgree first

Cartagena taxis do not use meters. Agree the fare before you get in or use a ride app that quotes the price, and have small notes: drivers rarely have change for a 50,000-peso bill. As a rough guide, a trip within the old city or from the walls to Bocagrande is in the region of 12,000 to 20,000 pesos, and the airport to the walled city is generally under 30,000. Hotels will call a trusted driver for a few thousand more, which is money well spent at night.

Do not rent a car. There is nowhere useful to park inside the walls, the one-way system is fierce, and everything you want is either walkable or a cheap ride away. For Barú or the Rosario Islands, book a car and driver or a boat through your hotel rather than negotiating on the beach.

The beach question, answered before you book

City sand is greyBarú is the postcard

This catches more visitors out than anything else. The beaches at Bocagrande and Marbella are grey-brown sand with murky water and a steady procession of vendors; they are pleasant enough for a walk and disappointing as a holiday beach. The Caribbean of the photographs is on Barú at Playa Blanca and out in the Rosario Islands, an hour away by road or boat.

So decide which holiday you are taking. For the city, stay inside the walls and take a day trip to the islands — go with a hotel-booked boat and leave early, because the day-tour crowds arrive at eleven. For the beach, book a Barú or islands resort for three or four nights and give the walled city two nights at the start or the end. Trying to do both from a Bocagrande tower satisfies neither.

Frequently Asked Questions

The questions Guild readers ask most about staying in Cartagena.

Which part of Cartagena should I stay in?

For a first visit, inside the walls in the Centro or San Diego. Everything you came to see is within a fifteen-minute walk on flat ground, the streets are lit and patrolled well into the night, and the hotels are the loveliest in the country. Getsemaní, just outside the Puerta del Reloj, is livelier and cheaper and has become the city’s restaurant quarter, but it is noisier at night. Bocagrande is a wall of modern towers on a grey-sand beach across the bay: good value, sea views, ordinary charm, and a taxi ride from the old city. Barú and the Rosario Islands, an hour away, are where the white sand actually is.

Is the beach in Cartagena any good?

Not in the city itself, and it is better to know that before you book. Bocagrande and Marbella are grey-brown sand with cloudy water and busy with vendors. The Caribbean postcard is an hour away on the Barú peninsula at Playa Blanca, or out at the Rosario Islands, reached by road or boat as a day trip or an overnight. If a white-sand beach is the point of the holiday, stay at a Barú or islands resort and visit the old city for a day or two rather than the other way round.

How hot is Cartagena, and when is it most bearable?

Hot all year: about 88°F by day with heavy humidity, and it barely cools at night. There is no cool season. What changes is the breeze and the rain: December to March and again June to August are dry and breezy, and February is usually the windiest and most comfortable month. September and October are wet and still. The practical answer is to walk the walled city before nine and after five, keep the middle of the day for a pool or an air-conditioned museum, and book a hotel with genuinely cold air conditioning rather than a beautiful courtyard alone.

Is Cartagena safe for older travellers?

The walled city, San Diego and Bocagrande are among the most heavily policed districts in Colombia and are comfortable to walk by day and in the evening. The ordinary risks are pickpocketing in crowds, aggressive beach and street vendors, taxi overcharging, and drink-spiking in bars. The precautions are simple: use a hotel taxi or a ride app rather than hailing on the street, agree a fare before you set off, keep the phone in a pocket, do not walk on the city beaches or in Getsemaní’s back streets after dark, and never leave a drink unattended. The U.S. advisory for Colombia as a whole is Level 3; the areas it names as Level 4 are nowhere near Cartagena.

Do I need a car in Cartagena?

No. Inside the walls a car is a nuisance — narrow one-way streets and nowhere to park. Everything in the old city is walkable, and for anything further a taxi is inexpensive: a few dollars across town. Taxis have no meters, so agree the fare first or use a ride app that quotes it. Have your hotel book a car and driver for the Barú or Rosario Islands day trip rather than arranging it on the beach.

Are the walled city hotels accessible if stairs are difficult?

Ask before you book, and ask specifically. Many of the loveliest hotels in the Centro are converted seventeenth- and eighteenth-century houses, which means beautiful courtyards, uneven colonial floors, and sometimes no lift to the upper rooms. The streets outside are cobbled and the kerbs are high. Where step-free access matters, the modern hotels in Bocagrande and the larger resorts on Barú are more reliable, and the Sofitel Legend Santa Clara and Hyatt Regency both handle mobility needs well. Email the hotel and ask about lifts, ground-floor rooms and roll-in showers rather than trusting a booking-site tick box.
