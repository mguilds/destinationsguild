Best Time to Visit the Maldives

The Best Time to Visit the Maldives

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

The Maldives has no winter and no storm season. It has two monsoons, and choosing between them is really a choice between weather and money. December to April is iruvai, the dry north-east monsoon: calm seas, clear water, reliable sun and the highest prices of the year. May to November is hulhangu, the south-west monsoon — windier, wetter, far cheaper, and the season the manta rays come for. Here is what each month actually gives you.

Two Monsoons, No Winter

The Maldives has no cold season and no real storm season. It has two monsoons, and they decide everything about what a trip costs and what you see underwater.

Iruvai — the north-east monsoon, December to April

The dry half of the year. Winds are lighter, the sea between the atolls lies flatter, rain is occasional and underwater visibility is at its best. Air and sea temperatures hardly vary from one month to another here; what varies is the wind and the water.

This is the season the brochures were photographed in, and it is priced as such. Expect the highest rates of the year, minimum stays over the Christmas and New Year fortnight, and the popular islands booked months ahead.

Hulhangu — the south-west monsoon, May to November

The wet half. Wind from the south-west, squalls that blow through quickly, more cloud, and a rougher crossing to your island. It rarely rains all day, and the temperature does not drop, so a wet-season week is a long way from a washout.

It is also much cheaper, much quieter, and better for one particular thing: the plankton that the current brings in draws manta rays, and the famous gatherings in Baa atoll belong to these months.

What does not change

Warmth. The Maldives sits on the equator and the temperature is close to the same all year, in the air and in the water. There is no month when it is cold, no month when you need more than a light layer after dark, and nowhere in the country more than a few feet above sea level.

There is also no cyclone season worth planning around, which is rarer than travellers realise in this part of the world.

Month by Month

What to expect, and what it costs you, in each month of the year.

January

Peak season at its most reliable. Dry, sunny, light winds, flat water and the clearest visibility of the year. It is also among the two or three most expensive months, and the popular islands fill early. If you want the postcard conditions and the budget allows it, this is the month.

February

The same weather as January and, by most reckonings, the single best month in the water — calm, clear and bright. Prices stay high. Book a long way ahead for anything well known.

March

Still firmly in the dry season and the hottest stretch of it. Sun, calm seas and good visibility, with humidity beginning to build toward the end. Prices start to soften in the second half of the month.

April

The turn. Hot, often still, with the first heavy showers arriving as the monsoon changes over. Underwater conditions are usually very good early in the month. Rates ease through April, which makes it one of the better-value months in the dry half of the year.

May

The south-west monsoon sets in: wind, squalls and a choppier sea. It is also where the prices fall off a cliff, and the manta season is beginning in Baa. A good month if you can take weather in exchange for value.

June

Wet and windy, quiet and cheap. Rain comes in bursts rather than all day and the temperature barely moves. Western-side visibility suffers; eastern channels can be excellent. Mantas are around in the Baa atoll.

July

Still the wet season, but prices harden with the European holidays. Expect wind, grey stretches and good manta diving. Not the month to come if you want a bargain or a guarantee of sun.

August

Much like July: busy with holidaymakers, wet, windy and well into manta season. Hanifaru Bay is at its most reliable in this part of the monsoon.

September

One of the best-value months of the year. The holiday crowds have gone, the islands are quiet, mantas are still about and the rates are at or near their lowest. The weather is unsettled, which is exactly what you are being paid to accept.

October

Quiet, green and cheap, with the monsoon starting to ease toward the end of the month. Seas are still lively. For travellers who want space and value more than a guaranteed tan, September and October are the Guild’s pick.

November

The changeover month. The wind drops, the rain thins out and the water begins to clear, while prices have not yet climbed to their December level. A gamble that often comes off.

December

Dry season proper returns and the island fills. The first three weeks are excellent and merely expensive; the last ten days are the costliest of the year, with minimum stays, compulsory dinners and premiums on every transfer. Book it early or avoid it deliberately.

The Cheapest Time to Go

Where the money is, and the fortnight to avoid if you are watching it.

May, June, September and October

These four months are where the value is, and the gap is not small — the same room on the same island can cost less than half the February price. September and October are the pick of them: the European school holidays are over, the islands are quiet, the mantas are still around and the monsoon is beginning to tire.

What you are buying is weather risk. Some days will be grey and squally, some crossings will be bouncy, and some dives will be called off. On a two-week trip you will almost certainly still get sun.

The fortnight to avoid, unless you mean it

Roughly 20 December to 5 January is the most expensive stretch of the Maldivian year by a wide margin. Rates multiply, minimum stays appear, compulsory gala dinners are added to the bill and transfer prices rise with everything else.

If those dates are the only ones you have, book very early and read the fine print for the compulsory extras. If they are not, moving a fortnight either way can halve the cost of the same holiday.

What the headline price leaves out

Two things catch people out. The first is the transfer: a seaplane or a domestic flight and boat to a far island is a substantial, separate cost, charged per person each way, and a near island reached by speedboat costs a fraction of it. The second is tax — a green tax per guest per night and a tourism goods and services tax are added to the bill, at different rates for resorts and for guesthouses.

Both have been raised recently and we will not print a rate we cannot stand behind today. Ask for the full total, including transfers and taxes, before you pay a deposit.

Wind, Rain & Crowds

The four things that actually shape a Maldives trip.

Rain, and what it really does

Wet-season rain in the Maldives is mostly squalls: the sky goes dark, it empties, and twenty minutes later the sand is steaming. Whole washed-out days happen but are not the rule. Because nothing is cold, getting rained on is a minor event rather than a ruined afternoon.

Wind, which matters more

The wind is the thing to plan around, not the rain. In the south-west monsoon it builds a short, steep sea between the atolls, which makes a speedboat transfer wet and bumpy, can delay a seaplane and sometimes closes a dive site. If anybody in your party is unhappy on boats, favour the dry season or an island with a short transfer.

Visibility, which has sides

Underwater visibility is best in the dry season, but it is not uniform. In the south-west monsoon the plankton-rich water sits on the western side of the atolls, so visibility drops there while the eastern channels stay clear — and the plankton is exactly what brings the big animals in. Divers, unlike sunbathers, often choose the wet months deliberately.

Crowds, and where they are

A resort island is never crowded in the ordinary sense; it is simply full or not. The crowding you can feel is on the guesthouse islands and the popular excursions in February and over the holidays, and at Hanifaru Bay, where numbers are capped by permit. Travelling in the shoulder months is the whole answer.

Best Time by Area

The country is 500 miles long. The right month is not quite the same at the top and the bottom.

Greater Malé and the near atolls

The easiest part of the country to visit in any season, because the transfers are short. Even in the windy months a speedboat from the airport to Maafushi or Guraidhoo is a manageable crossing, and if the weather turns you are close enough to the capital to change plans.

Ari atoll

Good all year, and the one place where the headline animal is not seasonal: whale sharks are seen along the southern rim in every month. Dry season gives the better visibility; wet season gives the better price.

Baa atoll

A wet-season destination if you are coming for the mantas. Hanifaru Bay works on the south-west monsoon current, broadly May to November, and is managed by permit with snorkelling only. In the dry season Baa is a pleasant atoll like any other.

The far south — Fuvahmulah and Addu

Close enough to the equator that the seasons blur further still. Fuvahmulah’s tiger sharks are a year-round draw rather than a seasonal one, and the far south often keeps its weather when the middle of the country is under a squall. Getting there is a domestic flight whatever the month.

Maldives Timing FAQ

The timing questions travellers ask most before booking the Maldives.

What is the best time to visit the Maldives?

December to April, if clear water and reliable sun are what you are paying for. This is iruvai, the north-east monsoon: less rain, lighter winds, flatter seas and the best underwater visibility of the year. It is also the dearest season, and the fortnight around Christmas and New Year is dearer still. If you care more about value than about guaranteed sun, the second half of the south-west monsoon in September and October is quiet, green and a great deal cheaper.

When is the cheapest time to go to the Maldives?

May, June, September and October are the cheapest stretches of the year, and the saving is large rather than marginal — the same island can cost less than half what it does in February. These are south-west monsoon months, so expect wind and squally rain, usually in bursts rather than all day. July and August are wet months too but prices firm up again with the European school holidays, so the bargain sits either side of them.

When is the rainy season in the Maldives?

Roughly May to November, the south-west monsoon the Maldivians call hulhangu. It does not rain constantly. The pattern is squalls that come over fast, empty themselves and move on, with the heaviest and most persistent weather usually in the middle of the season. The sea gets choppier and the wind picks up, which matters more than the rain itself: it makes speedboat transfers bouncier and can cut underwater visibility on the western side of the atolls.

When can you see manta rays and whale sharks?

They are two different answers. Whale sharks are seen along the southern rim of Ari atoll throughout the year, which is unusual and is why that stretch of water is protected. Manta rays are seasonal and move with the current: the famous feeding aggregations at Hanifaru Bay in Baa atoll happen during the south-west monsoon, broadly May to November, with the biggest numbers in the middle of that window. If mantas are the reason you are going, book the wet season and go to Baa.

Does Ramadan affect a trip to the Maldives?

Barely at a resort, noticeably on an inhabited island. Ramadan moves about eleven days earlier each year, so check where it falls before you fix dates. On a resort island, which is private, your meals and bar run as normal. On a guesthouse island, cafes may close during daylight or keep shorter hours, boat schedules can shift, and eating or drinking in public during the fasting hours is not done. None of it spoils a trip; it simply needs knowing in advance.

Is there a cyclone or hurricane season in the Maldives?

No, not in the way the Caribbean or the Bay of Bengal have one. The Maldives sits close enough to the equator that tropical cyclones very rarely form over it, and that is one of the quiet advantages of the destination. What you do get in the south-west monsoon is strong wind, heavy squalls and rough water between the atolls — enough to make a crossing unpleasant or to postpone a dive, but not a storm season in the ordinary sense.
