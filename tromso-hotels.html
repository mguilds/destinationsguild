Tromsø

Where to Stay in Tromsø

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Tromsø is a town of about 70,000 people roughly 200 miles above the Arctic Circle, and it is the easiest place in Europe to have a genuinely Arctic holiday without hardship: there are restaurants, museums, a university and a brewery, and the aurora tours and whale boats leave from the middle of town. Because the dark half of the year is the busy half, and because the pavements are icy, where you stay here is mostly a question of how far you want to walk in the dark.

Where to stay in Tromsø, area by area

Three choices, and for a winter trip the first one is the answer for almost everybody.

The Guild does not name a hotel until an Explorer has confirmed it on the hotel’s own website, so this guide sets out the areas and sends you to the full, current list for each one rather than printing a shortlist we cannot stand behind yet. Sentrum - the island centre and harbour

Most convenientWalk to everythingTours depart here

The centre of Tromso occupies a few blocks on the eastern shore of Tromsoya island, between Storgata and the quays, and this is where essentially every visitor should stay. The restaurants, the Polar Museum, the cathedral, the harbour and the pick-up points for aurora chases, whale watching and dog sledding are all within a few minutes' walk, which matters a great deal when it is dark at two in the afternoon and icy underfoot. It is small - fifteen minutes end to end - and the ground along the harbour is level, though the streets climb gently as you go inland.

The university and museum end, south of the centre

QuieterBus to centreOften better value

South along the island from the centre, around the university and the Arctic science institutions, the town becomes residential and the accommodation turns to apartments and smaller guesthouses, often at a lower rate than the harbour hotels. The buses are frequent and the ride into the centre is ten minutes or so. This end also holds the botanic garden and the Polaria aquarium. Worth considering for a longer stay or if you want a kitchen; less good if you will be coming back from a late aurora tour and want your bed within sight.

Tromsdalen - the mainland side, under the Arctic Cathedral

Views back at the townCable carAcross the bridge

Across the sound on the mainland, Tromsdalen sits beneath the mountain and holds two of the town's landmarks: the white triangular Arctic Cathedral and the Fjellheisen cable car, which lifts you to a viewpoint over the island and the fjord. Staying here gives you the view back at Tromso, and the cable car on your doorstep for aurora watching above the town lights. The cost is the bridge: it is a twenty-minute walk or a short bus ride into the centre, exposed and windy, and in winter that is a real consideration rather than a quibble.

The light, and why it decides your trip

Tromsø sits roughly 200 miles above the Arctic Circle, and that single fact shapes everything. From about the third week of May to the third week of July the sun never sets — the midnight sun — and the town behaves accordingly, with boats out at two in the morning and hiking at midnight. From about the last week of November to the middle of January comes the polar night, when the sun does not clear the horizon at all, leaving several hours of blue twilight in the middle of the day and no sunrise for weeks.

Between those, the shoulder months do something useful: late September to late November and mid-January to late March give you both a normal day and a dark night, which is the combination that makes an aurora trip work. If seeing the northern lights is the point, come in that window rather than at midwinter, and give it several nights.

Getting around, and dressing for it

The centre is walkable and the buses cover the rest of the island and the mainland side frequently; the airport is only a few miles away, with a bus and inexpensive taxis. You do not need a car unless you intend to drive inland yourself in search of clear sky, which the tour operators do on your behalf.

What you do need is footwear. Tromsø is milder than its latitude thanks to the Gulf Stream, which sounds like good news and in practice means the temperature hovers around freezing and the pavements thaw and refreeze. They are genuinely slippery. Every local wears strap-on ice spikes over their boots in winter and every shop in town sells them for very little; buy a pair on the first morning. Add a proper windproof layer — it is the wind off the water, not the air temperature, that makes an Arctic evening hard work.

Norway uses the Norwegian krone (NOK, written kr), not the euro. Norway is not a member of the European Union, and euros are not accepted in shops. It is also one of the most nearly cashless countries in the world: cards and phones are taken everywhere, including on buses, in mountain huts and at market stalls, and many places no longer handle notes at all. You do not need to carry krone in cash. Wine and spirits are sold only through the state shops, Vinmonopolet, which close early and are shut on Sundays; beer is in supermarkets but stops being sold at a set hour in the evening. Tipping is not expected and never chased — rounding up a restaurant bill is plenty. Decline any card machine that offers to charge you in dollars; pay in krone and let your own bank convert.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS — the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years — is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today — nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Norway is the case readers get wrong most often. It is not in the European Union, but it is in the Schengen area, so everything above applies here in full — the border checks, the Entry/Exit System, and ETIAS when it comes. Flying Oslo to Copenhagen or Stockholm is an internal Schengen hop with no passport control. What Norway’s position outside the EU does change is customs: there are duty limits on what you may bring in, and the allowance for alcohol and tobacco is modest and genuinely enforced.

Staying in Tromsø — questions

Which part of Tromso should I stay in?

The island centre, almost without exception. Tromso's downtown sits on the eastern side of Tromsoya island around Storgata and the harbour, and it holds nearly all the hotels, the restaurants, the museums and the departure points for aurora tours, whale trips and dog sledding. It is compact and walkable. The mainland side across the bridge, under the Arctic Cathedral, is residential and has the cable car, but staying there means crossing the bridge every time you want dinner.

What is the polar night like?

In Tromso the sun does not rise above the horizon from about the last week of November to roughly the middle of January. That does not mean total darkness: there are several hours of deep blue twilight around the middle of the day, and the snow and the sea reflect a surprising amount of it, so you can walk about and take photographs. But there is no sunrise and no sunset for weeks, street lights stay on, and the town leans into it with candles and lamps everywhere. Most visitors find it extraordinary; a few find it oppressive. It is worth deciding which you are likely to be before booking.

When will I see the northern lights from Tromso?

The season runs from roughly late September to late March, when the nights are dark enough. Within that, nothing can be promised: the aurora needs solar activity and a clear sky as well as darkness, and Tromso is a coastal town with a good deal of cloud. The sensible approach is to allow at least three or four nights, take a tour that drives inland or towards the Finnish border to get out from under the local cloud, and book daytime activities you would enjoy anyway so a cloudy run is not a wasted trip.

And the midnight sun?

From about the third week of May to the third week of July the sun does not set in Tromso at all. It is a genuinely strange and lovely thing - hiking at midnight, boats out on a bright sea at two in the morning - and the town stays up accordingly. Two practical notes: bring a sleep mask, because hotel curtains vary, and do not assume summer is warm. Tromso in July is mild rather than hot, and the sea wind is cold.

Is Tromso very cold?

Less than its latitude suggests, because the Gulf Stream runs past it. Midwinter days typically hover around or a little below freezing rather than plunging, which is milder than inland Finnmark or much of central Sweden. What it is instead is wet, windy and icy underfoot: the thaw-freeze cycle makes pavements treacherous, and the wind off the water cuts. Bring boots with real grip, or buy the strap-on ice spikes that every local wears and every shop sells.

How many days should I spend there?

For a winter trip, four nights is the sensible minimum - one for the town, three for the chance of clear sky. For a summer trip, two or three days covers the town, the cable car, a boat trip and a walk. Tromso is also the natural base for a wider Arctic trip; the Lofoten islands and Alta are both reachable, though not as day trips.
