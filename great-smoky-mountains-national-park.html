Before you go

The Smokies cost nothing to enter, but they reward a little planning more than almost any park in the country.

Great Smoky Mountains National Park straddles the Tennessee and North Carolina line, and about 12 million people a year come through it, more than any other national park. Most of them arrive by car from the Tennessee side, through Gatlinburg, and most of them arrive between 9 a.m. and 2 p.m. That single fact shapes a good visit: start early, or come late in the afternoon, and you will see a very different park.

There is no entrance fee, which surprises first-timers. Instead, any vehicle parked in the park for more than 15 minutes needs a parking tag. Driving straight through on Newfound Gap Road needs no tag. Your Senior Pass or other America the Beautiful pass does not cover the parking tag, but a state disability placard or license plate does exempt you.

The park has two personalities. The Tennessee side, reached through Gatlinburg, Pigeon Forge and Townsend, holds Cades Cove, the Roaring Fork Motor Nature Trail and the busiest trailheads. The North Carolina side, reached through Cherokee, is quieter, with the Oconaluftee Visitor Center, the Mountain Farm Museum and elk grazing in the fields. Newfound Gap Road (US 441) ties the two together across the crest of the mountains.

Hurricane Helene hit the North Carolina side hard in September 2024. Most of the park has long since reopened, but in late 2026 several trails in the Cataloochee and Big Creek areas are still closed or damaged, and a few back roads have no opening date yet. If your heart is set on the eastern end of the park, check the park's closures page before you go.

Parking tags: tags are sold at visitor centers, at parking-tag machines (credit card only, and most trailheads have none), and on recreation.gov to print at home. Each tag covers one vehicle and must match its license plate. Hang an annual tag on the lower passenger side of the windshield; set a daily or weekly tag face-up on the passenger side of the dashboard. Digital tags on a phone are not accepted, and a tag does not guarantee you a parking space. You do not need a tag while parked at your own campsite.

Current closures (park update of November 16, 2026): the Laurel Falls Trail is closed for rehabilitation, with reopening expected sometime in 2027. Part of the Sugarlands Mountain Trail is closed, the Carlos Campbell Overlook on Newfound Gap Road is temporarily closed, and Mingus Mill is temporarily closed for repairs. On the North Carolina side, Helene damage still closes Gunter Fork and Swallow Fork trails, much of Caldwell Fork Trail, and Big Creek Trail beyond Mouse Creek Falls (the first two miles are open). Straight Fork Road and Heintooga Round Bottom Road have no 2026 opening date.

Seasonal roads: Kuwohi Road (to Clingmans Dome) and the Roaring Fork Motor Nature Trail are open until November 29, 2026, then close for winter. In 2026 Kuwohi Road opened April 1 and Roaring Fork April 17. The Cades Cove Loop Road is open sunrise to sunset and is typically closed to motor vehicles on Wednesdays from May through September, which makes those days good for bicycles.

Sources: the National Park Service website (nps.gov/grsm): fees, current cautions and closures, seasonal road schedule, frontcountry camping, accessibility, directions and FAQ pages, checked November 2026.

Best things to do in Great Smoky Mountains National Park

Six experiences to build your visit around, most of them easy to enjoy from the car or a short walk.

Scenic drive

Cades Cove Loop

An 11-mile, one-way loop through a broad valley of old farm fields, log cabins, churches and the Cable Mill. Deer and black bears are common. Go at sunrise; by late morning the loop can crawl.

Across the mountains

Newfound Gap Road

US 441 climbs from Gatlinburg over Newfound Gap at 5,046 feet and drops to Cherokee. The overlook at the gap sits right on the state line and the Appalachian Trail.

Highest point

Kuwohi

The park's summit at 6,643 feet, formerly called Clingmans Dome. A steep, paved half-mile path climbs to the observation tower. The road is open April through November.

Short drive

Roaring Fork Motor Nature Trail

A narrow, winding one-way road just outside Gatlinburg, past rushing streams and old homesites. Motor homes, buses and trailers are not allowed.

North Carolina side

Oconaluftee & the Mountain Farm Museum

Next to the Oconaluftee Visitor Center near Cherokee, a collection of log farm buildings sits along the river, and elk often graze in the nearby fields early and late in the day.

Early summer

The synchronous fireflies

For about a week in late May or early June, fireflies near Elkmont flash in unison. Viewing is by a lottery for vehicle passes on recreation.gov each spring; in 2026 entries opened in late April.

From the Guild

Buy your parking tag online at home before you leave, and if you are staying a few days, the $15 weekly tag pays for itself on the fourth day. Then enter the park by 8 a.m. The park itself advises it: the rush builds from about 9 to 2, and a 3 p.m. start works too if you carry a flashlight for the walk back.

When to visit Great Smoky Mountains

Spring (April and May) brings wildflowers on the lower trails and mild days in the valleys, though the high country can still be cold, and the seasonal roads reopen through April and May. Summer is warm, humid and busy, with afternoon thunderstorms; it is also the season for ranger programs and the synchronous fireflies.

Fall is the park's most popular season. The park says the last three weeks of October are usually best for color, starting at the high elevations and working down. Expect slow traffic in Cades Cove and on Newfound Gap Road on October weekends, and book rooms in Gatlinburg and Pigeon Forge early.

Winter is quiet and often mild in the valleys, but snow and ice can close Newfound Gap Road for a day or more at a time, and Kuwohi Road and Roaring Fork close at the end of November. Cades Cove and Smokemont campgrounds stay open all year. A winter weekday in Cades Cove can be one of the most peaceful days you will spend in any national park.

How to get to Great Smoky Mountains National Park

By car

To reach the Gatlinburg entrance, leave I-40 at Exit 407 (Sevierville), take TN-66 south, and follow US 441 through Sevierville, Pigeon Forge and Gatlinburg into the park. For the quieter Townsend entrance and Cades Cove, come in from Maryville on US 321 and TN-73.

From the east, leave I-40 at Exit 27 and follow US 74 and US 19 through Maggie Valley to Cherokee, North Carolina, the gateway on the North Carolina side, then US 441 north into the park. The park warns that GPS units in the mountains sometimes send drivers the wrong way on one-way roads or onto dead-end routes, so pick up a free paper map at a visitor center.

By air

McGhee Tyson Airport (TYS), just south of Knoxville, is about 45 miles from the Gatlinburg entrance and the most convenient airport for the Tennessee side. Asheville Regional Airport (AVL) is about 60 miles from the Cherokee entrance.

There is no public transit into the park from either city. Rent a car; you will want one to get around the park in any case.

Where to stay near Great Smoky Mountains National Park

Most visitors base themselves on the Tennessee side. Gatlinburg puts you at the park's front door, Pigeon Forge has the most rooms, and Townsend is the quiet choice for Cades Cove.

Lodges inside the park

There is only one place to stay inside the park, and you have to walk to it. LeConte Lodge sits near the top of Mount LeConte, the park's third-highest peak, and no road reaches it. Five hiking trails lead to it, from about 5 to 9 miles one way, and the lodge is generally open from late March to mid-November.

Guests sleep in rustic, hand-built log cabins with propane heat, kerosene lanterns and wool blankets, and meals are served family style. Advance reservations are required, and the lodge books up well ahead; contact LeConte Lodge directly, by phone at (865) 429-5704 or through its own website. The lodge is run under contract with the National Park Service by LeConte Lodge L.P. Remember that trailhead parking still needs a parking tag.

For everyone else, the beds are in the gateway towns. On the North Carolina side, Cherokee has hotels and the casino resort of the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians, and Bryson City and Maggie Valley are close by.

RV travel & camping at Great Smoky Mountains

Ten developed campgrounds, all by advance reservation, none with showers, and almost no hookups.

The park has ten developed campgrounds, and advance reservations are required at every one of them, all year, through recreation.gov or 1-877-444-6777. Cades Cove and Smokemont campgrounds are open year-round; Elkmont, Cosby, Deep Creek, Look Rock and the others open seasonally. Check-in is 1 p.m. and check-out is noon.

Plan on dry camping. No park campground has showers, and the park says Look Rock is the only one with electric and water hookups, at some sites (check that it is open for your dates). Cades Cove, Smokemont and Elkmont have small 5-amp outlets at some sites, for medical equipment only. Dump stations are at Cades Cove, Cosby, Deep Creek, Look Rock and Smokemont, and across from Sugarlands Visitor Center, which is the closest one for Elkmont campers.

RV length limits are set site by site and listed on each site's recreation.gov page; many sites in these older campgrounds are short, so check before you book a big rig. Each RV site takes one RV or trailer, and no more than two vehicles or one vehicle with a trailer. Stays are limited to 14 nights in a row and 60 nights a year. Generators must be off from 8 p.m. to 8 a.m.

Some park roads are not for RVs. Motor homes, buses and trailers are not allowed on the Roaring Fork Motor Nature Trail. Recreation.gov warns Elkmont campers that RVs and trailers cannot cross the narrow bridge at Metcalf Bottoms, even if your GPS routes you there. The Cataloochee road is narrow, winding gravel, and the park does not recommend the Route 32 approach for RVs.

If you want full hookups and a hot shower, the private RV parks and campgrounds in Pigeon Forge, Gatlinburg and Townsend have plenty of full-hookup sites, many on the rivers. They fill in October, so book ahead.

Fishing in Great Smoky Mountains

Every stream in the park is open to fishing, and the Smokies hold native brook trout along with rainbow and brown trout and smallmouth bass. Anglers 16 and older need a valid Tennessee or North Carolina fishing license; either state's license is good anywhere in the park, no trout stamp is needed, and licenses are not sold inside the park. Fishing inside the towns of Gatlinburg and Cherokee requires their own special permits.

The park's rules are stricter than most: one hand-held rod, artificial flies or lures with a single hook only, and no bait of any kind. Brook, rainbow and brown trout and smallmouth bass must be at least 7 inches, with a daily limit of five in any combination. Fishing is allowed year-round from 30 minutes before sunrise to 30 minutes after sunset. Confirm the current rules on the park's fishing page before you cast.

For bigger water, the TVA lakes around the park are close by. See our guides to lake fishing in Tennessee and Kentucky and lake fishing in the Carolinas.

Accessible travel at Great Smoky Mountains

What is available for visitors with limited mobility, and the passes that help.

All of the park's visitor centers are accessible. Sugarlands (near Gatlinburg), Oconaluftee (near Cherokee) and Cades Cove visitor centers have accessible parking and restrooms and are open year-round except Christmas Day. Vehicles with a valid state disability placard or license plate do not need a parking tag.

The Sugarlands Valley Nature Trail, a paved, level half-mile loop near Sugarlands Visitor Center, is the park's one fully accessible trail. Short walks behind the Sugarlands and Oconaluftee visitor centers and the marked quiet walkways along Newfound Gap Road are other easy options. The Mountain Farm Museum's gravel paths can be managed with help.

In Cades Cove, most of the historic buildings can be seen from your car. The John Oliver Cabin has a level, paved path of about 0.3 miles, though there is a step up into the cabin, and the Becky Cable House has a ramp. At Kuwohi, the path to the tower is paved but steep.

Off-road wheelchairs can be borrowed free at Sugarlands and Oconaluftee visitor centers, first come, first served, and can be reserved by email in the summer season; they work only on certain trails. Accessible campsites are available at Cades Cove, Smokemont and Elkmont, and adaptive programs such as adaptive kayaking run in summer.

Park passes that save you money. U.S. citizens and permanent residents with a permanent disability can get the free lifetime Access Pass. Visitors 62 and over can buy the Senior Pass ($80 for life, or $20 a year). Veterans and Gold Star families get a free Military Lifetime Pass, and current service members a free annual pass. Each covers entrance fees at every national park.

Traveling on dialysis? The nearest dialysis clinics are in Sevierville, about 15 miles north of the Gatlinburg entrance, with more in Knoxville. Book your treatment at a clinic there several weeks before you go; the large dialysis companies run travel desks that will arrange it with your home unit.

Great Smoky Mountains National Park FAQ

Does Great Smoky Mountains National Park have an entrance fee?

No. There is no entrance fee, but any vehicle parked in the park for more than 15 minutes needs a parking tag: $5 for a day, $15 for a week or $40 for a year.

Does my Senior Pass cover the Smokies parking tag?

No. America the Beautiful passes, including the Senior, Access and Military passes, do not cover the parking tag. Vehicles with a state disability placard or license plate are exempt.

Where do I buy a Smoky Mountains parking tag?

Daily and weekly tags are sold at visitor centers, at parking-tag machines in the park and on recreation.gov. Annual tags are sold online. Digital tags on a phone are not accepted, so print it or pick one up.

Is there a hotel inside the Smokies?

Only LeConte Lodge, a rustic lodge near the top of Mount LeConte that you can reach only on foot, by trails of about 5 to 9 miles. It is open from late March to mid-November and requires advance reservations.

Is the Laurel Falls Trail open?

Not in 2026. The park closed Laurel Falls Trail for rehabilitation and expects it to reopen sometime in 2027.

When is Cades Cove closed to cars?

The Cades Cove Loop Road is open sunrise to sunset every day, but it is typically closed to motor vehicles on Wednesdays from May through September so walkers and bicyclists can use it.

Can I bring an RV into the Smokies?

Yes, on the main roads and in the campgrounds, though most campgrounds have no hookups and length limits vary by site. RVs, buses and trailers are not allowed on the Roaring Fork Motor Nature Trail.

When is the best time to see fall colors in the Smokies?

The park says the last three weeks of October are usually best, with color starting on the high ridges and moving down into the valleys.

Plan your Great Smoky Mountains trip

Book your room in Gatlinburg, Pigeon Forge or Townsend, buy your parking tag before you leave home, and plan an early start for Cades Cove.
