Where to Stay in Cluj-Napoca

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Cluj-Napoca is the largest city in Transylvania and the one with the least interest in being picturesque for visitors. It is a university city of long standing — Romanian, Hungarian and German higher education all have roots here — and today it is the country’s technology centre, which gives it a young population, a serious coffee and restaurant culture, and more going on in the evening than anywhere outside Bucharest. The historic centre is handsome rather than postcard-pretty: a broad square with a very tall Gothic church in the middle of it, an Austro-Hungarian grid around it, and a Hungarian and Saxon history that is written into the street names. Travellers often arrive expecting a stop and stay an extra night.

Piata Unirii and the centre

Most centralBig squareWalk to everything

Union Square is the middle of the city: St Michael’s Church standing alone in it with one of the tallest Gothic towers in the region, the Banffy Palace art museum along one side, and cafes around the edges. The streets off it hold the museums, the better restaurants and most of the city’s central hotels. Level, compact and the obvious choice for a short stay — you can reach everything a visitor wants on foot from here in fifteen minutes.

Piata Muzeului and the old lanes

AtmosphericCafesQuieter evenings

A few minutes north of Union Square, the small Museum Square sits in the oldest pocket of the city with the Franciscan church, the history museum and a cluster of terraces that are the pleasantest place in Cluj to sit outside. The lanes around it are narrow and largely free of traffic. Rooms are mostly apartments and small guesthouses. It has more character than the main square and is marginally quieter at night, which makes it our pick for two or three nights.

The station end: Horea and the river

Best valueTransportMixed streets

North across the Somes river, along Strada Horea toward the railway station, is where the mid-range hotels and the better prices sit. It is an ordinary city street rather than a handsome one, with a ten- to fifteen-minute walk over the bridge into Union Square. Convenient if you are arriving or leaving by train, and good value for a longer stay, but choose the specific address: the stretch nearest the station is noisier than the stretch nearest the bridge.

Zorilor and Gheorgheni

QuietestResidentialShort bus in

South and east of the centre, up the slope in Zorilor and out past the lakes in Gheorgheni, is residential Cluj: apartment streets, parks, the botanical garden on the near side and the Iulius park development on the far one. Quiet, green and noticeably cheaper, with a ten-minute bus or a fifteen- to twenty-five-minute walk into the middle. Sensible for a week, and for anyone who would rather sleep than be in the middle of a student city on a Friday.

Grigorescu and the Hoia edge

GreenFamily-friendlyWalking from the door

West along the river, Grigorescu runs out toward the Hoia woods and the open-air ethnographic museum on the hill, which makes it the greenest central-ish part of the city and the one you would choose to walk from. It is a calm residential district with a bus or a twenty-minute riverside walk into town. Rooms are mostly apartments. A good choice in summer, and for anyone who wants trees rather than nightlife at the end of the street.

Getting around Cluj-Napoca

On foot

The centre is flat, compact and easy: Union Square, Museum Square, the two main churches, the museums and the better restaurants all sit inside a fifteen-minute walk. The ground rises as soon as you go south toward Zorilor or up to the Cetatuia hill, where there is a good view back over the roofs. Pavements are in reasonable condition by Romanian standards and the centre has been steadily pedestrianised.

Trams, buses and trolleybuses

A proper city network with trams on the main east-west axis and buses and trolleybuses filling in, paid for with a card from a kiosk or contactless on board. It is cheap, it is frequent on the main lines, and it is the easy way out to Gheorgheni, Grigorescu or the Iulius end. Check the route on a phone rather than at the stop.

Taxis and ride-hailing

Both work well and both are inexpensive. Ride-hailing is the simpler option; street taxis are metered and honest in Cluj by reputation. Fares within the city are small enough that there is no reason to walk a leg in the rain.

To and from the airport

Avram Iancu International is about nine kilometres east of the centre and is the busiest airport in Romania after Bucharest, with a useful spread of European connections. A city bus runs in, and taxis and apps wait at the terminal. The drive is short outside rush hour. For most visitors flying into Cluj is a far better idea than the long train from Bucharest.

Trains, and getting out of town

Cluj is well placed for the north and west and badly placed for Bucharest: the railway south takes most of a day and almost everyone flies instead. Trains and buses run to Oradea, Sibiu, Brasov and Baia Mare, and Maramures with its wooden churches is within reach to the north. The Turda salt mine, a vast worked-out chamber with a boating lake in it, is about forty minutes by road and is the standard half-day trip.

When to come

Cluj follows its university term as much as its weather. May, June and September to October are the best months: mild, long evenings, the terraces full and the students in town, which is when the city feels most like itself. July and August are warm and the students are gone, so the centre is quieter and cheaper except around the big summer music festivals, when rooms sell out months ahead and prices rise sharply — check the festival dates before you book anything in late July or August. November to March is cold and often grey, with snow likely in January and February; the city works perfectly well in winter and is at its cheapest, and the Christmas market in Union Square is a good one.

Getting in: what applies, and what does not

Romania is in the European Union and, since 1 January 2025, a full member of the Schengen area — air and sea borders opened in March 2024 and the land borders followed on New Year’s Day 2025. Arriving from another Schengen country is now an internal journey with no routine passport check, which is a real change from older guidebooks. Romania is not in the euro area: the currency is the Romanian leu.

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen external border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave. Ninety days in any one hundred and eighty is the visa-free tourist allowance for American, Canadian, British and Australian visitors, and it is counted across the whole Schengen area rather than against Romania alone.

Cluj-Napoca — questions

Which part of Cluj-Napoca should I stay in?

Around Piata Unirii for a short first visit, or in the lanes by Piata Muzeului a few minutes north if you would rather have character and terraces than the main square. For better prices, the Horea street end toward the station, chosen address by address. For quiet and a longer stay, Zorilor or Gheorgheni. For trees and river walks, Grigorescu.

How long do I need in Cluj-Napoca?

One full day covers the sights: St Michael’s Church, the two squares, the Banffy Palace art museum, the botanical garden and the walk up Cetatuia for the view. Two nights is the comfortable version and gives you an evening in a city that is genuinely good at evenings. A third night is worth it if you want the Turda salt mine or a run north toward Maramures.

Is Cluj worth a stop at all?

Yes, though for different reasons from Brasov or Sibiu. It is not a preserved medieval town — it is a living university city with good food, good coffee, a real music scene and a handsome Austro-Hungarian centre. If your trip is only about Saxon squares and castles you could skip it; if you want to see where Romania is going rather than only where it has been, it is the most interesting city in the country after the capital.

What is the easiest way to get here?

Fly. Avram Iancu airport has the widest range of European connections outside Bucharest, and it is twenty minutes from the centre. The train from Bucharest takes most of a day for a distance you would drive in six hours, and the road is slow. From Brasov or Sibiu, driving or the bus is better than the railway.

Is the Turda salt mine worth the trip?

It is one of the more memorable things in Transylvania: an enormous worked-out salt chamber a hundred metres down with a boating lake, a Ferris wheel and a mini-golf course improbably installed in the bottom of it, reached by stairs and a lift. It is about forty minutes by road and takes a half day. Dress for a steady twelve degrees or so whatever the season, and check opening arrangements before setting out.

Do I need a car in Cluj?

Not in the city — the centre is walkable, the trams work and parking in the middle is difficult. A car earns its keep for the day trips: Turda, the Apuseni mountains, the painted and wooden churches to the north. Hiring for one or two days from the city and returning it is usually better than keeping a car for the whole stay.

Is there much left of the Hungarian and Saxon city?

A good deal, once you look. St Michael’s is a Hungarian Gothic foundation, the statue of King Matthias Corvinus stands in front of it and the house said to be his birthplace is a short walk away; there are Hungarian-language schools, theatres and newspapers, and about a sixth of the city speaks Hungarian. The city has three names for a reason, and the museums handle the overlapping history better than they once did.
