Where to Stay in La Romana

Where to Stay in La Romana — Best Hotels & Areas

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated August 2026

La Romana sits on the Dominican Republic’s southern shore, an hour west of Punta Cana on a modern toll road, and it is the quiet answer to the east coast’s noise. The water here is the country’s clearest — these beaches face south, sheltered by reef, and are the most reliably free of sargassum seaweed anywhere in the Dominican Republic. For readers who have been disappointed by a weedy week in Bávaro, that alone is reason enough.

There are three quite different places to stay. Casa de Campo is a seven-thousand-acre private estate with three Pete Dye golf courses, its own marina, its own beach and the hilltop village of Altos de Chavón — magnificent, and so large that a golf cart is not a novelty but a necessity. Bayahíbe is a small, flat fishing village where you can genuinely walk to dinner, and the departure point for the Saona Island boats. Playa Dominicus, just along the coast, is the resort strip: a wide, gently shelving beach with the easiest sea entry in the country.

This is also the gateway to Cotubanamá National Park and the reefs off Catalina Island, and one of the calmest corners of the country for anyone who wants the Caribbean without the crowds.

Luxury

Two very different kinds of grand: a private estate the size of a small town, and a polished adults-only resort on the sand.

Casa de Campo Resort & Villas

$$$$ Luxury7,000-acre estateThree Pete Dye coursesPrivate marinaMinitas Beach

One of the Caribbean’s legendary estates: seven thousand acres with its own beach, marina, three Pete Dye golf courses including the famous Teeth of the Dog, and the hilltop village of Altos de Chavón. Hotel rooms are surprisingly attainable; the villas are another matter entirely. Pet-friendly

We could not verify a single mainstream resort here that accepts pets, and we will not invent one. Casa de Campo publishes an explicit no-pets policy — service dogs case by case, never unattended, and barred from the beach, pools, restaurants and gym. A handful of small guesthouses appear on booking-site pet filters, but none publishes its own terms, so get any policy confirmed in writing before you travel. Note too that the Dominican Republic asks for a recent veterinary health certificate and proof of rabies vaccination; it does not require an import permit for a traveller’s own pets. Getting There, Getting Around, Getting In the Water

This coast is the easiest in the country to travel — if you know a few things first.

Where you can walk, and where you cannot

Bayahíbe village: yesCasa de Campo: cart

Bayahíbe village is genuinely walkable, and that makes it unusual on this coast. It is small, flat and compact — restaurants, dive shops and the boat dock are all within five or ten minutes. The lanes are uneven and partly unpaved and you do have to cross the main road, but for a traveller who wants to walk out for dinner rather than be driven to it, this is the place.

Playa Dominicus is half walkable: there is a small commercial plaza and a beach path serving the resort strip, but hopping between resorts needs a taxi and there is little shade at midday. La Romana city is a working Dominican town — the Parque Central area is flat and pleasant, the pavements are uneven, and there is not a great deal for a visitor on foot.

Casa de Campo is a resort bubble, and an enormous one. Seven thousand acres means a golf cart is not optional. That is part of the charm for some guests and a genuine consideration for others.

Airports, transfers and whether to drive

LRM is closestPUJ 50–60 min

La Romana International (LRM) is the closest and by far the easiest arrival — ten to fifteen minutes to Casa de Campo, about half an hour to Bayahíbe and Dominicus. Punta Cana (PUJ) is fifty to sixty minutes away on the modern Autovía del Coral toll road, with tolls of about RD$200 — roughly three and a half dollars — each way, and has far more flights. Santo Domingo (SDQ) is roughly two hours.

Reckon on about a hundred dollars for a private transfer for a car of four or five people one way from Punta Cana, or twenty-five to forty per person on a shared shuttle. Uber will bring you from Punta Cana airport for around sixty dollars, but the return leg out of Bayahíbe is unreliable — do not build a plan on it.

Should you drive? For a single-resort holiday, pre-booked transfers plus excursion pickups is the low-stress answer, and the one we would choose. The highways here are genuinely good, so a rental is workable if you intend to roam — but avoid night driving, when unlit secondary roads, motorbikes and assertive overtaking make it a different proposition. At Casa de Campo, rent a golf cart rather than a car.

What to do — and how hard each one is

Saona is a long dayChavón is cobbles

Altos de Chavón is a stone replica of a sixteenth-century Mediterranean village built in the 1970s above the Chavón River gorge, with a five-thousand-seat Grecian amphitheatre that Frank Sinatra opened in 1982, artisan workshops and a small museum of Taíno artefacts. It is beautiful and it is the hardest walking on this page — cobblestones, steps and some steep sections. Go early, before the heat.

Saona Island, inside Cotubanamá National Park, is the classic day out: roughly ten hours door to door, two hours each way by catamaran and speedboat, about four hours on the beach, plus the famous starfish ‘natural pool’ stop. From around eighty dollars a head in a group. Be realistic about it: wet landings, a rocky dock at Bayahíbe and a very long day in the sun. Water shoes are essential, and it is the weakest fit on this page for anyone with limited mobility.

Diving and snorkelling off Bayahíbe are the best in the country, on the Cotubanamá reefs and the wall at Catalina Island, with several long-established dive centres in the village. A snorkelling trip is the accessible version and a genuine pleasure. Golf at Casa de Campo, with carts and caddies as standard, is among the more accessible activities here — Teeth of the Dog is one of the great courses of the Caribbean.

Weather, and why the water is clearer here

Best: Dec–AprLeast sargassum in the DR

December to April is the sweet spot — highs around 82–86°F, lower humidity and the least rain. Hurricane season runs 1 June to 30 November with the sharp end from mid-August to early October, which is also the wettest stretch and the one most likely to disrupt a trip.

The sargassum advantage is real. The weed drifts onto east-facing Caribbean shores, which is why Bávaro and Punta Cana take the brunt from roughly June to August. Bayahíbe and Dominicus face south and are sheltered by reef, and together with Saona and Catalina they are the most dependably clear water in the Dominican Republic. Not immune — nowhere is — but markedly better, and worth knowing if a previous Caribbean holiday disappointed you on that score.

Frequently Asked Questions

The questions Guild readers ask most about staying in La Romana.

Is La Romana or Punta Cana better?

It depends on what disappoints you most easily. Punta Cana has more resorts, more adults-only choice and more direct flights, but its east-facing beaches collect sargassum seaweed from roughly June to August. La Romana and Bayahíbe face south behind reef and have the clearest, most dependably weed-free water in the country, plus a village you can actually walk around — but fewer resorts and fewer flights. For a quiet, clear-water week we would choose La Romana.

Which airport should I fly into for Bayahíbe?

La Romana (LRM) is closest and easiest — about thirty minutes to Bayahíbe and Dominicus, ten to fifteen to Casa de Campo — but it has few flights. Punta Cana (PUJ) is fifty to sixty minutes away on the modern Autovía del Coral toll road and has far more service from the United States; a private transfer runs about a hundred dollars per car. Santo Domingo (SDQ) is roughly two hours. Most American travellers fly PUJ and transfer.

Is the Saona Island trip worth it?

For most people yes, but go in with clear eyes: it is a full ten-hour day, about two hours each way by catamaran and speedboat, four hours on a genuinely beautiful beach, and a stop at the shallow starfish ‘natural pool’. From around eighty dollars a head in a group. There are wet landings and a rocky dock at Bayahíbe, so water shoes are essential, and it is a long time in the sun. If mobility or stamina is a concern, a half-day Catalina Island snorkelling trip is the kinder alternative.

Can I stay somewhere here with a pet?

Not easily, and we would rather say so than pretend otherwise. We could not verify a single mainstream La Romana or Bayahíbe resort that accepts pets, and Casa de Campo publishes an explicit no-pets policy — service dogs case by case only, never unattended, and barred from the beach, pools, restaurants and gym. A few small guesthouses appear on booking-site pet filters but none publishes its own terms; get any policy confirmed in writing. The Dominican Republic asks for a recent veterinary health certificate and proof of rabies vaccination; it does not require an import permit for a traveller’s own pets.

Do I need a car at Casa de Campo?

You need a golf cart, not a car. The estate covers seven thousand acres, and the beach, marina, golf courses, restaurants and Altos de Chavón are genuinely far apart — walking between them is not practical. Cart rental is standard practice and every guest does it. For getting to and from the resort, use a pre-booked private transfer rather than a hire car; the airport is only ten to fifteen minutes away.

Where is the best beach for swimming near La Romana?

Playa Dominicus. It is wide, calm and gently shelving, with the easiest sea entry of any resort beach in the Dominican Republic — which matters a great deal if you are less steady in surf than you once were. It is also reef-sheltered and south-facing, so it stays remarkably clear of sargassum. Bayahíbe’s village beach is smaller and more local in feel, and Casa de Campo has its own private Minitas Beach for estate guests.
