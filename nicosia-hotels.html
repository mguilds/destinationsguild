Nicosia

Where to Stay in Nicosia

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Nicosia is the capital, the biggest city on the island, the only one with no coastline, and the last divided capital in Europe. Most visitors to Cyprus never come here at all, which is their loss: inside the star-shaped Venetian walls there is a dense, shaded old city of lanes, courtyards, a Gothic cathedral that has been a mosque for five centuries, two first-rate museums and a restored craft quarter, and running through the middle of it the buffer zone, with a pedestrian crossing at the end of Ledra Street where you show your passport and walk into the other half of the same city. Outside the walls, modern Nicosia is a prosperous, ordinary business and university town. Come for a night or two rather than a week, and come in spring or autumn: inland and ringed by hills, this is the hottest place in Cyprus in summer.

Inside the Venetian walls

The reason to comeWalk to everythingLimited choice of rooms

The old city is where you want to be. Within the walls you have the Ledra Street crossing, the Selimiye mosque and the Gothic cathedral it was built as, the Cyprus Museum just outside the Paphos Gate, the restored Chrysaliniotissa craft workshops, and a growing number of small bars and restaurants in the lanes around Faneromeni. Accommodation inside the walls is boutique hotels and restored townhouses rather than chains, so there is not a great deal of it and it books up; it is worth planning ahead. Some streets near the buffer zone are run-down and a few are dead ends at the line, which is part of the character of the place.

The modern centre: Makarios Avenue and Stasikratous

Business hotelsShops and restaurantsTen minutes from the walls

Immediately south of the walls, the new centre around Makarios Avenue is where the larger hotels, the shopping streets and a good share of the better restaurants are. It is an easy ten- or fifteen-minute walk into the old city, it is well served by taxis, and the hotels here are built for business travellers — which means good rooms, reliable air conditioning and, usefully for a visitor, rates that often fall at weekends rather than rising. If the old city is full, this is the sensible second choice.

Engomi and the university district

QuieterResidentialNeed a bus or taxi

West of the centre, Engomi is a leafy residential district with the University of Nicosia, a cluster of embassies and a number of mid-range hotels, and it is where the city feels most like a comfortable provincial capital. Rooms are cheaper than in the centre and there is parking, which matters here. You are a ten-minute drive or a bus ride from the walls, so this suits people with a car who want space rather than atmosphere.

Agios Dometios and the western edge

CheapestNear the vehicle crossingLittle character

Out towards the western edge of the city, Agios Dometios is the district around the main crossing point for cars through the buffer zone, and is a practical place to stay if you are driving across for a day or arriving from elsewhere on the island. It is an ordinary suburb — there is nothing here for a visitor beyond convenience and price — and the old city is a short drive or bus ride away.

Strovolos and the southern suburbs

Everyday NicosiaSpace and parkingEntirely residential

South of the centre, Strovolos is the largest of the suburbs and is where a great many Nicosians actually live: wide streets, shopping centres, the athletic clubs and almost nothing for a tourist. There are a few hotels here and they are good value, particularly if you have a car and are using Nicosia as a stop between coasts rather than as a destination. Do not stay here if you came to walk the old city.

Getting around Nicosia

On foot

The old city is flat and compact and walking is the only sensible way round it; the lanes are narrow and many are one-way or closed to cars. From the walls into the modern centre along Makarios Avenue is ten or fifteen minutes on pavement. Summer is the limiting factor rather than distance — Nicosia in July regularly passes forty degrees, and the old city holds the heat.

Crossing to the north

The Ledra Street crossing is in the middle of the old city at the end of the main pedestrian shopping street, and it is a simple matter of showing your passport at the Republic’s booth and again at the Turkish Cypriot one. Agios Dometios, on the western edge, is the crossing for vehicles. Take your passport, remember that time spent across the line counts towards your ninety days in the Republic, and check the limits on goods before you buy anything to bring back. If you are driving a hire car, confirm your rental agreement allows it.

Buses and taxis

City buses radiate from the Solomos Square station just outside the walls and cover the suburbs adequately; intercity coaches run to Larnaca, Limassol, Paphos and the south-east coast and are fast on the motorways. Taxis are plentiful and are the simplest way to move after dark. There is no railway anywhere in Cyprus.

Driving and parking

Nicosia is the one Cypriot city where a car is more nuisance than help. The old city is largely impenetrable, the ring road around the walls is busy, and parking inside is limited to a few car parks near the gates. If you are touring the island by car, the practical approach is to park outside the walls and walk in. Driving is on the left, and the motorways to Larnaca and Limassol are quick and well signed.

When to come, and what it costs

Nicosia is the hottest place on the island and the only major town with no sea breeze to take the edge off it. From late June to early September temperatures in the forties are routine and sightseeing on foot is genuinely unpleasant in the afternoon; if you must come in summer, do the old city early and the museums at midday. Spring and autumn are excellent — warm, clear and comfortable for walking — and winter is mild, with the occasional cold snap and some rain, and is a perfectly good time to visit a city whose attractions are mostly indoors or in the shade. Rooms are cheapest at weekends, when the business trade goes home.

Getting in: Cyprus is in the EU but outside Schengen

The Republic of Cyprus belongs to the European Union and uses the euro, but it is not in the Schengen area. Days you spend in Cyprus do not count against the ninety days in any hundred and eighty that Schengen allows, and days spent in Schengen countries do not count against your Cyprus allowance; the two are tracked separately. Cyprus is also outside the EU’s Entry/Exit System, so there is no biometric registration on arrival. Citizens of the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, Australia and the European Union need no visa for a holiday of up to ninety days. British advice is a passport issued within the last ten years and valid three months beyond departure; United States advice recommends six months of validity. Confirm your own nationality’s terms with your own foreign ministry.

One rule matters more than any other here. Enter the Republic only through its own points of entry — the airports at Larnaca and Paphos and the southern seaports. Arriving through the north and crossing south is treated as illegal entry and can mean a fine or a refusal. Crossing north for a day from the south is the opposite: legal, routine and well worth doing, with your passport in your pocket. Time spent in the north still counts towards your ninety days, there are limits on goods brought back, and many hire companies restrict taking their cars across — check your rental agreement first.

Nicosia — questions

Is Nicosia worth a night, or is it a day trip?

A night is better, and two is plenty. A day trip from the coast works, but it means arriving in the middle of the day, which in summer is the worst possible time to walk the old city, and leaving before the evening, which is when the lanes around Faneromeni come alive. Staying over lets you do the walls and the crossing early, the museums in the heat of the afternoon and dinner in the old city, which is how the place is best seen.

What do I need to cross at Ledra Street?

Your passport, and a few minutes. You walk to the end of the pedestrian street, show it at the Republic's booth, walk a few metres through the buffer zone and show it again on the Turkish Cypriot side. There is no charge and no visa. Three things to keep in mind: the time you spend across counts towards your ninety days in the Republic; there are limits on goods you bring back, tobacco especially; and you must not use the north as your way into the Republic in the first place, which is treated as illegal entry.

Is it safe to walk around the old city?

Yes. Nicosia is a low-crime city and the old quarter is ordinary and lived-in rather than threatening. The buffer zone itself is the one thing to respect: it is a military line, not a curiosity, and several lanes simply stop at a wall of sandbags and oil drums. Do not climb, photograph the sentry posts or try to enter it anywhere other than a designated crossing point, and you will have no difficulty at all.

Is there anywhere to swim?

Not in Nicosia, which is the one Cypriot city with no coast. The nearest sea is at Larnaca, about forty minutes away by motorway, and Limassol is under an hour. Most hotels of any size have a pool, which in July and August is less of a luxury than a necessity.

Why do so few visitors come here?

Because Cyprus sells itself as a beach destination and the capital has no beach, sits inland, and is at its most forbidding in exactly the months most people visit. That is a reason to come in April or October rather than a reason to stay away. The old city, the museums and the walk across the line are the most interesting few hours on the island, and you will share them with very few other visitors.
