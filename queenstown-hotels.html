Queenstown

Where to Stay in Queenstown

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Queenstown is a small town with a very large reputation, wedged between Lake Wakatipu and the mountains with not much flat ground to build on. That shortage is the thing to understand before you book: the town is expensive in both summer and ski season, almost everything that is not on the lakefront is up a hill, and the places that look close together on a map are separated by a climb. Below are the four sensible bases, including the one most visitors have not considered.

Where to stay in Queenstown, area by area

Four places to sleep around the lake, what each is like, and who each one suits.

The town centre and the lakefront

Walk everywhereDearestBest for a short stay

The few blocks between the Mall, the gardens and the Steamer Wharf, and the right choice for a first visit or a trip of two or three nights. Everything is here: the lake, the restaurants, the activity desks, the gondola station and the bus hub, all within a flat few minutes of each other. It is also the most expensive part of town by a wide margin, and in high summer and midwinter a central room goes for a price that surprises people. Rooms are often small, and the nightlife along the Mall carries on late, so ask for a quiet side if you are a light sleeper.

Fernhill and Sunshine Bay

Lake viewsBetter valueSteep

The hillside immediately west of town, a kilometre or two along the lake road. This is where a good deal of the larger hotel and apartment stock sits, and it buys you a proper view down the lake for noticeably less than the same room in the centre. It is a fifteen to twenty-five minute walk into town and a few minutes on the frequent bus. The catch is in the name: it is a hill, the walk home at the end of an evening is all uphill, and in winter the side streets can be icy. Worth it for the view and the price if you are happy to use the bus.

Frankton and the airport side

Good valueCar-friendlyNeed the bus or a car

Eight kilometres east, where the lake narrows and the airport and the supermarkets are. Motels, apartments and a few hotels, at the most reasonable rates near Queenstown, with easy parking and the Kelvin Heights golf course and the Frankton Arm walking track nearby. It makes good sense if you have a hire car, an early flight, or a family needing space and a kitchen, and the bus into town is regular. It makes less sense if you came for the lakefront evenings, because you will be making a twenty-minute journey to get to them.

Arrowtown

QuietHistoricTwenty minutes by car

Our own preference, and the one people forget. Twenty minutes north-east, Arrowtown is a preserved gold-rush village of stone and timber cottages under an avenue of sycamores, with good restaurants, a fine small museum and the Arrow River at the bottom of the street. It is calmer and cheaper than Queenstown, it is spectacular in April when the trees turn, and it is well placed for the Gibbston wineries and the drive to Wanaka. The trade-off is that you need a car or the bus for anything in Queenstown itself, and the last bus back is earlier than the last drink.

Getting around

The town centre is small and walkable and everything beyond it is not, so the local bus service is the thing to learn. Buses run from the central hub to Fernhill, Frankton, the airport and Arrowtown at a flat fare that is one of the cheapest in the country, paid by card or on a transport card, and they are frequent through the day. The airport is eight kilometres out and the bus into town takes about half an hour; a taxi is quick and far dearer.

A hire car is useful here rather than essential, and in the centre it is a liability: central parking is limited, metered and fought over in season. If you are touring the South Island, the usual pattern is to keep the car for the drives - the Crown Range to Wanaka, the Gibbston wineries, Glenorchy at the head of the lake - and leave it parked while you are in town. One thing to check in winter: the roads to the ski fields and over the Crown Range may require chains to be carried, and a hire agreement may restrict where you can take the car.

When to come, and what it costs

Queenstown has two high seasons and they are nothing like each other. December to February is summer: long warm evenings on the lakefront, every walk and boat running, and the town at its fullest and dearest. July and August are the ski months, with the Remarkables and Coronet Peak above the town and Cardrona and Treble Cone over the range, and prices to match. April is the loveliest time to be here, when the willows and poplars turn gold and the crowds have gone, and May and November are the two genuinely cheap months.

If the budget matters, those shoulder weeks are where the difference is - the same room can cost half what it does in January. Book accommodation for a summer or ski trip months ahead; this is the one town in New Zealand where leaving it late is reliably expensive. Getting in: what you need, and what you pay

Most visitors from visa-waiver countries need a New Zealand Electronic Travel Authority, the NZeTA, requested before you fly, and most also pay the International Visitor Conservation and Tourism Levy, which is normally collected at the same time. A separate New Zealand Traveller Declaration is completed shortly before you arrive and has replaced the old paper arrival card.

Both the NZeTA fee and the levy have changed more than once in recent years, so we do not print the figures here — read them off the official page at immigration.govt.nz when you book, and request the NZeTA through Immigration New Zealand’s own app or website rather than any agent offering to do it for you. Australian citizens need neither; Australian permanent residents who are not citizens do need the NZeTA. The airline checks it before you board, so do it when you book rather than at the airport.

Take the biosecurity questions on arrival seriously: declare all food, and clean the soil off boots, tents and fishing tackle before you pack them. Declaring something costs nothing; not declaring it can cost you an instant fine. The full picture is in our New Zealand travel guide.

Staying in Queenstown — questions

Is it cheaper to stay in Arrowtown or Frankton than Queenstown?

Usually yes, and sometimes by a good deal. Frankton, near the airport, has the most reasonable everyday rates within easy reach of town, with easy parking and supermarkets. Arrowtown is cheaper than central Queenstown in most seasons and much nicer to walk around in the evening, but it is twenty minutes away and the last bus back is early. Fernhill sits in between: cheaper than the centre, a view down the lake, and a hill between you and dinner.

How many days do you need in Queenstown?

Three nights is a comfortable minimum and four or five is better. One day goes on the town, the gondola and the lake; a second on a day trip, most often to Milford Sound or Glenorchy; and a third on whatever you came to do, which may be a walk, a winery or something strapped to a harness. Milford Sound as a day trip from Queenstown is a long one - roughly twelve hours door to door - and many visitors prefer to move to Te Anau for a night instead.

Do I need a car in Queenstown?

Not in the town itself, where the buses are frequent and cheap and parking is the opposite. A car earns its keep for the drives out: Glenorchy at the head of the lake, the Crown Range to Wanaka, the Gibbston wineries, and Arrowtown. If you are staying in the centre and taking organised trips to Milford and the ski fields, you can do the whole visit without one. In winter, check whether your hire agreement allows the ski-field access roads and whether you must carry chains.

Is Queenstown worth visiting in winter if I do not ski?

Yes, and it is one of the better value times to go. The lake and the Remarkables under snow are the best view in the country, the gondola and the restaurants run as usual, the Winter Festival fills a week in June, and there are hot pools, wineries and cellar-door lunches that are pleasanter in cold weather than in heat. You will be sharing the town with skiers, so book ahead for July and August, but the non-skiing half of the day is yours.
