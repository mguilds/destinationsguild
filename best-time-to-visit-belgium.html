Best Time to Visit Belgium

Best Time to Visit Belgium

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Belgium has a mild Atlantic climate, which is a polite way of saying it is grey and damp more often than it is not, and that no month is either reliably dry or a write-off. What changes through the year is the light, the crowds and the price. This is a month-by-month account of what you would actually find, written by Explorers who would rather tell you that November is dark than sell you a shoulder season that does not exist.

The short answer

Three ways to choose, depending on what you are coming for.

For the cities

June, or September. Long light, warm but not hot, terraces open, and the Bruges day-trip crush short of its peak.

For value & quiet

Late January to March, or November. Grey and dark, genuinely cheap, museums nearly empty and restaurants that will seat you on the night.

For the Ardennes

June, or late September into October. Walking weather and then the autumn colour through the forests and river valleys of the south.

Month by month

What each month is actually like, written for a visitor rather than for a weather chart.

January

Cold, grey and dark by half past four, with the markets gone by the second week. The cheapest month of the year and the emptiest museums. Carnival preparations begin in the south.

February

Much like January but lengthening. Carnival is the event, and Binche — whose carnival is on the UNESCO intangible heritage list — is the one people travel for, on the Sunday to Tuesday before Lent.

March

Unreliable. Cold mornings, the first real light in the evenings, occasional fine spells and plenty of rain. Prices are still low and the cities are still quiet, which is the argument for it.

April

Improving and busy at Easter, when Bruges in particular fills. Blossom in the parks, long enough days to be out properly, and weather that can give you four seasons before lunch.

May

One of the two best months. Warm without heat, long light, terraces out, gardens at their best. Three public holidays fall in or near it, so check dates before booking a quiet weekend.

June

Our pick. The longest days of the year, bright until about ten in the evening, warm, and the summer crowds not yet at full strength. The best single month for the Flemish cities and for the Ardennes.

July

Warm and the busiest month. The Gentse Feesten take over Ghent for ten days mid-month, the Ommegang pageant runs in Brussels, and 21 July is the National Day. Bruges is at its most crowded.

August

Warm, and a month of two halves: Bruges and the coast are full, while Brussels is noticeably quieter because its own residents are away. The flower carpet is laid on the Grand-Place every other August.

September

Our other pick. Most of the warmth, a fraction of the crowds, students back in Ghent, Leuven and Louvain-la-Neuve, and rooms easier to find. The best month for travelling without planning far ahead.

October

Cooling and wetter, and the best month in the Ardennes, where the forests turn. The cities are quiet, the museums are comfortable, and the evenings start to want an indoor plan.

November

The low point: damp, grey and dark, with All Saints on the 1st and Armistice Day on the 11th, which is marked seriously here. The Christmas markets open in the last days of the month and change the mood completely.

December

Cold, wet, rarely snowy, and full of markets and lights in every city. Weekends are crowded and expensive, midweek is manageable, and the week between Christmas and New Year is busy throughout.

Which part of Belgium, and when

The country is small, but it does not all have the same season.

Brussels

Works all year, because so much of it is indoors. The quirk worth knowing is that its hotels are built for officials and the European institutions, so weekday nights cost more than weekends — the opposite of most capitals. August is its quietest, most liveable month.

Bruges

May, June, September and midweek December. The city is lovely in any month and overwhelmed between about ten and five from July to late August. Whenever you come, the trick is the same: be out early and again after five, when the coaches have gone.

Ghent and Antwerp

Best from May to early October, and both carry winter better than the small towns because they are working cities with plenty indoors. Avoid mid-July in Ghent unless you want the Gentse Feesten, which is a ten-day street festival and not a quiet break.

The Ardennes

June for walking, late September and October for the colour. Winter is cold, wet and quiet, with some places closed; spring can be muddy. This is the one region where you want a car, and the one where the weather genuinely dictates the trip.

The coast

Really only July and August, when the Belgian and Dutch families arrive and the trams run the length of the shore. Out of season the resorts are bracing, cheap and largely shut. De Haan keeps its belle epoque looks better than most.

Flanders Fields

Any month, and November most of all. The Last Post has been sounded under the Menin Gate at Ypres every evening since 1928, barring the occupation years, and the Armistice Day commemorations on 11 November are large and deeply felt.

Public holidays worth planning around

Banks, offices and many shops close on 1 January, Easter Monday, 1 May, Ascension Day, Whit Monday, 21 July, 15 August, 1 November, 11 November and 25 December. Most museums and the main sights stay open, and restaurants generally do too, but a long weekend built around one of the spring holidays will be busier and dearer than you expect, because the whole country travels at once. The separate thing to watch is not a holiday at all: a great many Belgian museums close every Monday, year round. Check the day before you build a plan around a collection.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS — the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years — is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today — nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Belgium is about as straightforward as Europe gets. It is a founding member of both the European Union and the Schengen area, it uses the euro, and its land borders with the Netherlands, Germany, Luxembourg and France are internal Schengen crossings that you will cross on a train or a motorway without noticing. Arriving by air from another Schengen country means no passport control at all. The one border that is still a proper frontier is the Channel: the Eurostar to and from London is checked at the station before you board, so allow the time the operator asks for rather than turning up as you would for a domestic train.

When to visit Belgium — questions

What is the best month to visit Belgium?

June, with September a very close second. June gives you the longest light of the year — it is still bright at ten in the evening this far north — warm rather than hot days, and terraces open everywhere, without the full weight of the July and August crowds in Bruges. September keeps most of the warmth, loses most of the visitors, and puts the students back in Ghent and Leuven, which is when those cities are at their best. May is the third choice and slightly less reliable for weather.

When is the worst time to go?

November and the first half of January are the hardest months: short days, steady grey, dark by half past four, and little compensating festivity once the markets have packed up. They are also the cheapest weeks of the year, and if you are coming for museums, restaurants and beer rather than for walking around squares, they can be a shrewd trade. The other thing to avoid, unless you want it specifically, is Bruges in the second half of July and all of August, when the day-trip traffic peaks.

How much does it actually rain?

Often, but usually not hard. Belgium has an Atlantic maritime climate and rain is spread fairly evenly through the year rather than concentrated in a wet season, so no month is reliably dry and no month is a washout either. The practical consequence is that a packable waterproof is worth more than a weather forecast, and that you should plan each day with one indoor option in it. Expect the heaviest, longest rain in late autumn and winter, and short sharp showers in summer.

Are the Christmas markets worth planning a trip around?

They are genuinely good, and Brussels, Bruges, Ghent and Antwerp all run one from late November to the first days of January. Brussels is the biggest, with the market spread between the Grand-Place, the Bourse and the Marche aux Poissons, and a light show on the square. Bruges is the prettiest and by some distance the most crowded at weekends. If you go, go midweek, book accommodation early, and dress for cold damp rather than crisp cold — it is rarely snowy and often around freezing with a wind.

Is there anything that closes on a particular day?

Yes, and it catches people out: a great many Belgian museums close on Mondays, including some of the big ones. Check opening days before you plan a Monday around a collection. Public holidays close banks, offices and many shops, though restaurants and the main attractions usually stay open; the ones to know are 1 January, 1 May, 21 July for the National Day, 15 August, 1 and 11 November and 25 December. Sunday is quieter for shopping than in some countries but the markets, including the big Sunday one at Brussels-Midi, are a reason to be out.

When should I go for the Ardennes rather than the cities?

Late September and October, or June. The Ardennes in the south are forest, river valleys and small stone towns, and they work on a different calendar from the cities — autumn colour through October is the best of it, and early summer is good for walking and paddling before the Belgian and Dutch holiday traffic arrives. Winter there is genuinely cold and wet and a number of smaller places shut. It is the one part of the country where a car earns its keep.
