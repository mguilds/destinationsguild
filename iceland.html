Iceland

Iceland Travel Guide

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated August 2026

Iceland is a country of about four hundred thousand people living on a volcanic island the size of Kentucky, and roughly two-thirds of them live within half an hour of the capital. Everything else — the waterfalls, the glaciers, the black beaches, the fishing villages of two hundred souls — is spread thinly around one two-lane road that circles the island. That single fact shapes every good Iceland trip. This is not a country you walk or take a train through; there is no railway anywhere on the island. It is a country you drive through slowly, or are driven through, stopping often, in weather that changes its mind four times before lunch. Do that, and it is one of the most astonishing places on earth. Rush it, and you will remember mostly the inside of a car.

A 2,400 kr bowl of soup is about twenty dollars. Iceland is the most nearly cashless country most Americans will ever visit — cards work at petrol pumps, in mountain huts and at roadside coffee stops, and a great many visitors never touch a banknote at all. Decline the machine’s offer to charge you in dollars and pay in krónur; the built-in rate is always worse.

Why visit Iceland

Geology happening at conversational distance, in a country that is safe, orderly and entirely used to visitors.

The earth is still being made here

Iceland sits astride the ridge where the North American and Eurasian plates pull apart, which is why it has some thirty active volcanic systems, why the ground steams by the roadside, and why almost every building on the island is heated by water that came out of the rock hot. You do not have to hunt for this. You can stand in the rift valley at Þingvellir, look at the cliff on either side, and understand exactly what you are looking at.

Waterfalls you can walk up to

Gullfoss, Skógafoss, Seljalandsfoss, Dettifoss, Goðafoss — the great ones are minutes from a car park, not hours up a trail. Seljalandsfoss can be walked behind, on a wet and slippery path that is worth doing once. In a country where so much scenery is famously hard-won, Iceland’s best is unusually generous to those who cannot walk far.

Hot water as a national habit

The Blue Lagoon is the famous one and the Sky Lagoon is the handsome new one, but the real Icelandic bathing culture is the municipal swimming pool: outdoor, geothermal, open all year, in every town of any size, and costing a few dollars. Locals go daily, sit in the hot pots and talk. It is the single cheapest and most authentic pleasure in the country.

Room to breathe

Iceland receives well over two million visitors a year, and in July nearly all of them are on the same 200 miles of the south coast. Drive four hours further — to the east fjords, the Westfjords, the north — and you can spend a morning without meeting a car. Almost nowhere else in Europe still offers that.

Iceland at a glance

Money

The Icelandic króna (ISK), roughly 120 to the US dollar. Cards are accepted absolutely everywhere, including unmanned petrol pumps — bring a card with a PIN. Tipping is not expected and service is included. Iceland is expensive: budget as you would for Norway or Switzerland, not for continental Europe.

Language

Icelandic, with English spoken fluently by nearly everyone under seventy. You will never need a phrase book, though takk (thanks) is always welcome.

Getting there

Keflavík International (KEF), 30 miles from Reykjavík, with direct flights from a dozen North American cities and a flight time of five to seven hours from the US east coast. The Flybus and Airport Direct coaches meet every arrival; a taxi is very expensive.

Getting around

No railways at all. Route 1, the Ring Road, is 828 miles of mostly two-lane paved highway. Rental car, coach tour or scheduled bus — and in winter, seriously consider letting somebody else drive.

When to go

Late May to early September for daylight and open roads; late September to March for the northern lights. July and August are busiest and dearest. See our month-by-month guide.

Two websites that matter

vedur.is for the weather and road.is for road conditions, updated constantly and used by everyone who lives there. safetravel.is carries the official alerts. Check all three each morning; that is simply how Icelanders travel their own country.

Top things to do in Iceland

The short list, honestly ranked, with what each one actually asks of you.

The Golden Circle

Þingvellir, the Geysir hot spring area and Gullfoss, in a loop of about 150 miles from Reykjavík. It is the most visited thing in Iceland and it deserves to be. All three have level paths and proper facilities. Go early or late; the tour coaches arrive between ten and two.

The south coast to Vík

Seljalandsfoss, Skógafoss, the Skógar folk museum, the black sand at Reynisfjara. One warning we will repeat: the waves at Reynisfjara are genuinely dangerous and have killed visitors. Stay well up the beach and never turn your back on the sea.

Jökulsárlón and Diamond Beach

A lagoon of floating icebergs calved from Vatnajökull, and the beach opposite where they wash up on black sand. Five hours from Reykjavík, which is why staying at Höfn or Skaftafell rather than driving it in a day is the better plan.

Whale watching from Húsavík

The north coast has the highest sighting rates in the country — humpbacks reliably, blue whales occasionally, from a small harbour town with a good museum. April to October. Dress far warmer than you think; they lend overalls for a reason.

A geothermal soak

Blue Lagoon for the spectacle, Sky Lagoon for the sea view, the Secret Lagoon at Flúðir for something older and simpler — or your neighbourhood municipal pool, which is what Icelanders actually do. Book the famous two ahead; they sell out.

The northern lights

Between late September and March, on a clear night, away from town lights. No tour can promise them and any that does is selling you something. What a good tour does buy you is a driver who knows where the cloud is not.

Best time to visit & getting there

Iceland has two seasons that matter and they want opposite things from the sky. From late May to early August the sun barely sets: at midsummer Reykjavík gets about twenty-one hours of usable light and the north gets more, which means you can drive at ten at night in full daylight and photograph a waterfall at midnight. That is the season for the Ring Road, the highlands and the puffins. It is also the season of full car parks and rooms booked six months out.

From late September the dark returns, and with it the aurora. December has barely four hours of daylight in the capital, which sounds grim and is in practice rather beautiful — a long blue morning, a low gold afternoon, and the lights if you are lucky. The trade is real, though: interior roads close, the north can be genuinely difficult, and a winter self-drive is not a beginner’s undertaking.

If someone asked us for one month, we would say early September — still green, roads all open, most of the crowds gone home, prices easing, and the first proper dark nights arriving in the last week. Late May runs it close.

Getting there is the easy part. Keflavík is one of the best-connected small airports in the world, five to seven hours from the US east coast, and Icelandair has long allowed a stopover of up to a week at no extra airfare on the way to Europe — still the cheapest way an American ever sees this country. Confirm the current terms when you book.

Iceland in your later years

A country that suits older travellers well, provided you respect two things: the wind and the footing.

What makes it easy

Everyone speaks English. The country is safe, calm and well organised, medical care is excellent, and the famous sights sit close to their car parks with level paths and real toilets. Hotels are small and staff are unhurried. Nobody is trying to sell you anything on the street.

The wind is the real hazard

Not the cold — the wind, which regularly reaches speeds that make opening a car door hazardous and can knock a person off their feet on an exposed path. Icelandic rental agreements exclude wind damage to doors for good reason. Hold the door with both hands, park facing into the wind, and on a bad day simply do not go to the exposed viewpoint. There will be another day.

Footing and poles

Wet lava rock, loose gravel, boardwalks in rain, and ice from October to April. Bring boots with real grip and a pair of walking poles even if you do not use them at home; in winter, add a set of slip-on ice cleats, which cost a few dollars and are sold in every petrol station.

Let somebody else drive

There is no shame in it and in winter it is the wiser choice. Reykjavík and Akureyri both have full day-tour networks that will collect you from your hotel, and small-group tours in a Mercedes van are a great deal more comfortable than a rental car on a gravel shoulder in sleet.

Ask about the lift

Guesthouses and converted farmhouses are a large part of Iceland’s charm and a large part of its accommodation. Many have no lift, and some have the bedrooms up a steep timber stair. This is often not stated in the listing. Ask directly before booking.

The pools are the kindest thing here

Warm water, handrails, steps into every hot pot, and a few dollars to get in. If a day’s driving has stiffened you up, the local pool at the end of it is the best thing in this guide.

Iceland travel FAQ

When is the best time to visit Iceland?

There are two good answers and they are opposites. For long days, open roads and the whole island reachable, come between late May and early September — and inside that window, late May, early June and the first half of September give you nearly all the daylight with a fraction of the crowds and noticeably lower prices. For the northern lights you need darkness, which means late September through March, when the weather is harder, the days are short and a good deal of the interior is shut. July and August are the busiest and most expensive weeks of the year. What you cannot have is the midnight sun and the aurora on the same trip.

Do you need a car in Iceland?

For most trips, yes, and that surprises people. Iceland has no passenger railway at all — not one mile of it — so the choice is a rental car, a coach, or a guided tour. Reykjavík itself needs no car and parking there is a nuisance. But the waterfalls, the glacier lagoon, the black beaches and the fjords are spread along a ring road of some 830 miles, and outside high summer the scheduled bus network is thin. If you would rather not drive on gravel in a crosswind — a perfectly sensible preference — base yourself in Reykjavík and Akureyri and use day tours, or take a small-group tour that drives for you.

How expensive is Iceland, really?

Expensive, and there is no polite way around it. Expect restaurant main courses in the range of 4,000–7,000 krónur, a bowl of lamb soup around 2,500–3,000, and a beer in a bar often over 1,500. Two things soften it more than any coupon. First, alcohol is sold at ordinary shops only through the state chain, Vínbúðin, and is dramatically cheaper there than in a bar. Second, the tap water is superb and free everywhere, so buying bottled water in Iceland is money set on fire. Petrol stations sell decent hot food, guesthouses often include breakfast, and a swim at the municipal pool costs a fraction of the famous lagoons.

Is Iceland safe with the volcanoes erupting?

Iceland is one of the safest countries in the world to travel in, and the volcanic activity of the past three years has been confined to a small, closely watched stretch of the Reykjanes peninsula in the south-west. The most recent eruption in that sequence ended on 5 August 2025, and as of this writing no eruption is under way, though scientists expect the series to continue. Through all of it, Keflavík airport stayed open, Reykjavík was unaffected and the Ring Road never closed. The town of Grindavík is the real casualty and remains largely emptied of its people. Check safetravel.is before you set out and follow whatever the authorities say on the day; they are exceptionally good at this.

What is the weather actually like?

Milder than the name suggests and far windier than you expect. Reykjavík in July averages a high near 57°F; in January it hovers around freezing, warmer than Chicago. The problem is never the temperature, it is the wind and the speed of change — sun, sleet and sun again inside an hour is an ordinary Icelandic morning. Dress in layers with a genuinely waterproof and windproof outer shell, and treat an umbrella as a comic prop. Two websites matter more than any forecast app: vedur.is for weather and road.is for road conditions and closures.

Do Americans need a visa for Iceland?

US and Canadian citizens do not currently need a visa for short tourist stays; Iceland is in the Schengen area though not in the European Union. The EU Entry/Exit System has been fully live since April 2026, so on your first arrival your photograph and fingerprints are recorded and your passport is no longer stamped. The separate ETIAS travel authorisation is not yet in operation and has no confirmed start date, so confirm the requirement close to your departure date rather than trusting what was true on your last trip.

Is Iceland a good country for older travellers?

It is one of the better ones, with one honest caution. In its favour: everyone speaks excellent English, the country is safe and orderly, the healthcare is first-rate, the food is served early, and the great sights are close to the road — you can stand in front of Skógafoss or Reynisfjara within a few minutes of the car park. The caution is footing and weather. Many viewpoints involve wet steps, loose gravel or a boardwalk in a gale, and the wind can genuinely knock a person down. Walking poles earn their place here. Choose accommodation with a lift if stairs are difficult, and prefer guided coaches to self-driving in winter.

How many days do you need?

Five days does Reykjavík, the Golden Circle and the south coast as far as Vík without hurrying. Eight to ten days is the comfortable length for the whole Ring Road, which is the trip most people picture when they picture Iceland. Under four days, stay in the south-west and do it properly rather than driving all night to say you circled the island — the distances are long, the light in winter is short, and Iceland punishes a rushed itinerary more than most countries.
