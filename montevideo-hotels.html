Montevideo — sixteen miles of Rambla and a colonial port

Where to Stay in Montevideo — the Best Areas

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Montevideo is a long, low city strung out along the north bank of the Río de la Plata, and where you sleep in it is really a question of which stretch of the Rambla — the sixteen-mile waterfront promenade — you want outside your door. Almost all of the architecture worth seeing is in Ciudad Vieja at the western end; almost all of the comfortable sleeping is three to eight miles east of it in Pocitos, Punta Carretas and Carrasco. Below is each district, what it is actually like at night, and the one piece of practical advice that answers most of the city’s crime problem.

Pocitos — the first choice for most visitors

A 1930s apartment district built around its own crescent of city beach, and where we would send a first-time visitor. The Rambla runs along the front, the streets behind it are leafy and residential, and there are enough restaurants and cafes within walking distance that you never need a taxi in the evening. It is about fifteen minutes by car from the old town and about twenty-five from the airport. Nothing about it is spectacular and everything about it works, which is the honest recommendation for a city where the spectacular parts are not the parts you want to sleep in.

Ciudad Vieja — inside the old city

The colonial peninsula at the western end, between the port and the Plaza Independencia: iron-balconied houses, the Mercado del Puerto with its wood-fired grills, the best of the museums and most of the architecture anyone photographs. Sleeping inside it puts all of that outside your door, and a handful of converted old houses do it very well. Two honest cautions. It is a working port quarter and it empties hard after office hours, and the Foreign Office advice naming the downtown and port areas as places not to walk alone after dark and at weekends is talking about these streets. It is also cobbled and uneven, which matters if you walk with a stick. Go for the character, take a taxi back at night, and do not expect a neighbourhood that is alive at eleven on a Tuesday.

Punta Carretas — quiet, safe and slightly smug

The headland immediately east of Pocitos, wrapped around a lighthouse, a golf course and a large shopping centre that used to be a prison. It is the most comfortable part of the city and the least characterful: wide streets, good apartment blocks, a long flat stretch of Rambla and a short walk to the Pocitos restaurants. People who want to feel entirely at ease in a strange city, and who do not mind that it could be a prosperous suburb of almost anywhere, are happiest here.

Centro and Barrio Sur — central, cheaper, mixed

The grid between the old town and Pocitos holds the bus routes, the big theatres, a good deal of the mid-priced hotel stock and the lowest prices in the city for a central bed. Barrio Sur, on the water side, is the heart of candombe drumming and comes alive in carnival season. The trade-off is straightforward: this is a real working city centre with the mixture that implies, it is noisier than the beach districts, and the same advice about walking alone at night applies more here than in Pocitos. If the budget is the deciding factor and you will use taxis after dark, it is a sound choice.

Carrasco — out by the airport, and the best beach

A garden suburb twenty minutes east of the centre with the widest and cleanest of the city beaches and the airport ten minutes further on. It suits two sorts of traveller: anyone with an early flight, and anyone who wants a quiet, green, low-rise base and does not mind a fifteen-dollar taxi every time they want dinner downtown. Families do well here. People who want to walk to things do not.

What to know before you book

The Rambla is the spine of the city. Sixteen miles of promenade from the old town to Carrasco, with a beach every mile or so. Which stretch of it you sleep on matters more than which hotel, and that is the one decision worth taking time over.

The city beaches are genuinely used. This is river water rather than ocean — brown, warm, calm and perfectly swimmable in summer. Pocitos and Carrasco are the two to sleep beside; Ramirez, nearest the centre, is the one to walk past.

Use a ride app or a hotel taxi. This is the single practical thing that answers most of the crime advice for central Montevideo. Do not hail in the street at night, and do not leave anything visible in a parked car — the Foreign Office names smash-and-grab thefts from cars stopped at junctions.

Sunday is slow and that is the point. Much of the city closes, families take over the Rambla with thermos and mate, and the Tristan Narvaja street market runs through the morning. Do not plan errands; plan a walk.

Cards work, cash is for the small stuff. Hotels, restaurants and shops take cards. Markets, buses, taxis and tips want pesos. A foreign card has in recent years earned an automatic tax rebate on hotel and restaurant bills, and the terms change, so treat it as a bonus rather than a budget line.

Winter is cool and damp, not cold. July days sit around 57 Fahrenheit with a wind off the water, and a good deal of older housing is heated less than a northern visitor expects. Ask about heating if you are coming between June and August.

Montevideo — questions

Which part of Montevideo should I stay in?

Pocitos, for a first visit. It sits on its own crescent of city beach with the Rambla along the front, the streets behind are leafy and residential, there are plenty of restaurants within walking distance and it is about fifteen minutes by car from the old town. Ciudad Vieja is the choice if you want to sleep inside the colonial quarter and will accept that it empties after dark; Punta Carretas if you want the most comfortable and least characterful part of the city; Carrasco if you have an early flight or want a quiet garden suburb with the best beach.

Is Montevideo safe?

No part of Uruguay carries advice against travel, and Montevideo is a calm capital by regional standards, but it has a real street-crime problem rather than a theoretical one. The UK Foreign Office describes bag-snatching, pickpocketing and muggings that are sometimes armed, names break-ins and smash-and-grab thefts from cars stopped at junctions, and advises against walking alone in the downtown and port areas, particularly at night and at weekends, and around the Tres Cruces bus terminal. The US State Department asks visitors to exercise increased caution. In practice: stay in Pocitos or Punta Carretas, use a ride app rather than hailing a taxi, keep your phone out of your hand on the street, and leave nothing visible in a car.

How many nights do I need in Montevideo?

Two is enough to see the city properly and three if you want a day in the Canelones wine country, which starts within an hour of town. The old quarter, the Mercado del Puerto, a stretch of the Rambla and one museum fill a comfortable first day; a market morning and a bodega afternoon fill the second. Montevideo is not a city that needs a week, and most visitors use it as the bookend to Colonia and Punta del Este.

Can I swim at the Montevideo beaches?

Yes, in summer, and local people do. The water is the Rio de la Plata rather than open ocean, so it is brown with river sediment rather than dirty, calm, shallow and warm &mdash; about 73 Fahrenheit, or 23 Celsius, in January and February. Pocitos and Carrasco are the pleasantest. Water quality is monitored and occasionally a beach is flagged after heavy rain, so look for the notice boards. In winter the sea drops to around 53 Fahrenheit and nobody swims.

How far is the airport, and is Carrasco worth it for an early flight?

Carrasco airport is about twenty-five to thirty minutes from Pocitos and the centre in normal traffic, and ten minutes from the Carrasco suburb. For a genuinely early departure the suburb is worth it, and it has the best beach in the city as a consolation. For anything after mid-morning there is no need to move out there, because the run in from the beach districts is straightforward.

Is Montevideo a good base for Punta del Este and Colonia?

It is the natural base for Colonia, which is about two and a half hours west by road or bus, and it works as a base for Punta del Este at a little over two hours east, though most people prefer to move rather than day-trip to the coast. Long-distance buses are frequent, comfortable and inexpensive and they all leave from the Tres Cruces terminal. If you only have four or five nights in Uruguay, two in Montevideo and two on the coast beats five in one place.
