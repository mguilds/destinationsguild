Where to Stay in Medellín

Where to Stay in Medellín — Best Hotels & Areas

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated August 2026

Medellín fills a narrow green valley at about 4,900 feet, and the temperature there scarcely moves off 72°F from one January to the next — which is why Colombians have called it the city of eternal spring for a century. It is also the city that has changed more than any other in the Americas in living memory, and visitors arrive with expectations formed thirty years out of date in both directions: some are frightened of a place that is now perfectly ordinary to visit, and others are careless in a place that still rewards sense.

Three districts matter. El Poblado is where nearly every visitor stays: green, hilly, safe, on the metro, and holding almost all the hotels — though within it the quiet avenue of the Milla de Oro and the nightlife streets around Parque Lleras and Provenza are two very different propositions. Laureles is the local favourite and our own: flat, leafy, residential, full of good restaurants and far fewer foreigners. Envigado, just south, is quieter still and feels like a small town that the city grew around. The historic centre is a guided morning, not a place to sleep.

One warning we would give a friend, and so we give it to you. The U.S. Embassy in Bogotá has issued repeated security alerts about visitors to Medellín being drugged and robbed after meeting people through dating apps, and several deaths have been investigated. The pattern is consistent: a friendly stranger, a drink, and a traveller who wakes with an empty wallet or does not wake at all. The rules are simple and they work — never leave a drink unattended, do not invite a new acquaintance to your hotel room, meet only in busy public places, and be suspicious of enthusiasm that arrives too fast. This is not a reason to avoid Medellín. It is a reason to read this paragraph twice.

Luxury

Medellín’s top end is business-city rather than resort: large, comfortable, well-run hotels with pools, lifts and level access, which for many of our readers is exactly right.

Family-friendly

What You Need to Know Before You Book

Four things that decide how Medellín goes.

Which district, honestly

El Poblado firstLaureles second

El Poblado: green, hilly, safe and on the metro, holding almost every hotel worth having. Within it, the Milla de Oro is a quiet avenue of towers and shopping centres, and Provenza and Parque Lleras are the nightlife heart, loud until three. Both are El Poblado; they are not the same holiday. Choose deliberately.

Laureles: the local favourite, and ours. Flat — which matters a great deal in this valley — leafy, residential, with excellent restaurants along the Segunda Circular and very few foreigners. Fewer hotels, so book early.

Envigado: a separate town swallowed by the city, quieter still, with a lovely square. Furthest from the sights and the cheapest of the three.

The centre: the Museo de Antioquia, the Botero sculptures in the plaza, and the hard, working heart of the city. Go with a guide in the morning; do not book a bed there and do not linger after dark.

The metro, the cable cars and getting about

Metro is excellentDon’t drive

The metro is the pride of Medellín and the city treats it accordingly: it is clean, cheap, punctual and safe by day, and nobody eats or litters on it. The Metrocable lines that climb the valley walls to the hillside barrios are part of the same system — ordinary public transport that happens to have one of the great urban views in the world. Line K to Santo Domingo and on to Parque Arví is the classic ride; go in the morning.

For evenings and for anywhere off the line, use a ride app rather than hailing. Do not rent a car: the valley roads are steep and congested, and a pico y placa licence-plate restriction limits which cars may drive on which days. The airport sits over the mountain about forty minutes away — allow an hour, and rather more in the evening.

The hills are the real accessibility question

Steep streetsLaureles is flat

The altitude here is a non-issue — 4,900 feet, roughly Denver, and almost nobody notices. What does catch visitors out is the gradient. El Poblado is built up the side of a valley, and a hotel that looks four blocks from a restaurant on the map may be four blocks and eighty vertical feet, on uneven pavement, in the rain.

So if walking is difficult: stay in Laureles, which is genuinely flat, or on the Milla de Oro, which is level along its length; ask the hotel directly whether the street outside is steep; and take rides that would look absurdly short at home. Nobody will think anything of it.

When to come

72°F all yearAugust for the Feria

The temperature does not change: around 82°F by day and 62°F at night in every month, which is why no hotel here needs air conditioning. What changes is the rain — April, May, September and October are the wet months, with heavy afternoon showers, and December to March and July to August are drier.

Two weeks are worth planning around. The Feria de las Flores fills the first ten days of August and ends with the desfile de silleteros, the parade of flower-carriers — the best week of the year here and the one week the hotels are genuinely hard to book. And in December the alumbrados, the Christmas lights along the river, are extraordinary and free. See our guide to the best time to visit Colombia for the month-by-month picture.

Frequently Asked Questions

The questions Guild readers ask most about staying in Medellín.

Which part of Medellín should I stay in?

El Poblado for a first visit: green, safe, well served by the metro, and holding almost every hotel worth having. Within it, the Milla de Oro is a quiet avenue of towers and shopping centres, while Provenza and Parque Lleras are the nightlife heart and can be loud until three in the morning. Laureles is the local favourite: flat, leafy, residential, with good restaurants and far fewer visitors, and it is where we would send a second-time traveller. Envigado, just south, is quieter still. The city centre is worth a guided morning and is not a place to sleep.

Is Medellín safe for visitors?

In El Poblado, Laureles and Envigado, yes, with ordinary city precautions, and the metro is clean, cheap and safe by day. The specific warning worth taking seriously is a different one: the U.S. Embassy in Bogotá has repeatedly warned about visitors being drugged and robbed after meeting people through dating apps in Medellín, with several deaths investigated. Do not invite strangers to your hotel, do not leave a drink unattended, and treat an unexpectedly enthusiastic new acquaintance with real caution. Beyond that: use a ride app rather than hailing, keep the phone in a pocket, and do not walk in the centre or in unfamiliar hillside barrios after dark.

What is the weather like in Medellín?

They call it the city of eternal spring and it earns the name: about 82°F by day and 62°F at night, in every month of the year, at an altitude of roughly 4,900 feet. Almost no hotel has or needs air conditioning. What changes is the rain — April, May, September and October are the wet months, with heavy afternoon showers, while December to March and July to August are drier. Pack a light jacket for the evening and an umbrella whatever the month.

Are there adults-only hotels in Medellín?

We could not verify a genuine adults-only hotel in Medellín through our booking partner, and we would rather say so than point a booking button at something that is not what it claims. There is a further reason for caution here: Medellín has been working hard to shut down sex tourism, and some properties that market themselves to single male visitors are not places we would send a Guild reader. What does exist in abundance is quiet, grown-up boutique hotels in Laureles and the calmer parts of El Poblado where children are simply rare.

Do I need to worry about altitude in Medellín?

No. Medellín sits at about 4,900 feet, roughly Denver’s altitude, and it troubles very few people. The city is built on the sides of a narrow valley, though, so the streets can be steep and the pavements uneven — that matters more for most visitors than the height does. If your trip also takes in Bogotá at 8,660 feet, doing Medellín first is a gentle way to acclimatise.

How do I get around Medellín?

The metro is the pride of the city: clean, cheap, safe and genuinely useful, and the cable cars that climb to the hillside barrios are part of the same system rather than a tourist ride. Use it by day. For evenings and for anything off the line, use a ride app rather than hailing a taxi in the street. Do not rent a car — the valley’s roads are steep and congested and there is a pico y placa licence-plate restriction. The airport is about forty minutes away over the mountain, so allow an hour.
