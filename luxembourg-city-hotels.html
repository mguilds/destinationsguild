Luxembourg City

Where to Stay in Luxembourg City

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Luxembourg City is a capital built on a cliff. The old town sits on a sandstone promontory with the Alzette and the Pétrusse cutting deep wooded gorges around three sides of it, and the quarters down in those gorges — Grund, Clausen, Pfaffenthal — are a hundred feet below the squares you walk between. What was for centuries one of the strongest fortresses in Europe is now a UNESCO World Heritage site you can walk the top and the inside of, and the modern city of banks and European institutions sits on the Kirchberg plateau across a bridge, three tram stops away. It is small, it is vertical, and every bus, tram and train in it is free.

Where to stay in Luxembourg City, area by area

The parts of Luxembourg City a visitor actually chooses between, and who each one suits.

Ville Haute: the old town on the rock

Most centralWalk to everythingDearest

The historic centre on top of the promontory, between the Place d’Armes, the Place Guillaume II and the cathedral, with the palace in a side street and the Chemin de la Corniche along the ramparts at the edge of it. Nearly everything a short-stay visitor wants is within ten minutes on foot, including the casemates and the lifts down into the valley. It is also where the city’s most expensive rooms are, and the hotel stock up here is small and old, so it goes early. If you have two nights and do not want to think about it, stay here.

Gare: the station quarter

Best valueTrains and tramMixed streets

South of the Pétrusse across the Adolphe and Passerelle bridges, the station district holds most of the city’s mid-range and chain hotels, and it is where the value is — the same money gets you noticeably more room here than up on the rock. The station is the hub of the whole country’s free rail network and the tram runs from the front of it to the old town in minutes. The quarter is ordinary rather than pretty, and a couple of streets immediately around the station are scruffier at night than anywhere else in the city, which locals will tell you plainly; walk a block or two out and it is perfectly pleasant.

Grund, Clausen and Pfaffenthal: down in the valley

Best atmosphereFew bedsA climb or a lift

The old lower town along the Alzette, directly beneath the ramparts: stone houses, bridges, a river, the brewery quarter at Clausen with the bars in it, and the quietest mornings in the city. Very little of it is hotel — what you find is apartments, guesthouses and a handful of small places — and the catch is the hundred feet of cliff between you and everything else. There are two free public lifts, one from the Plateau du Saint-Esprit into Grund and the glass one at Pfaffenthal, and once you know where they are the valley stops being a problem and becomes the best place in the city to wake up.

Kirchberg: the plateau

ModernBusiness tradeQuiet at weekends

Across the Red Bridge on the high ground north-east of the old town: the European institutions, the banks, the Philharmonie, the Mudam art museum and the conference centre, all in glass and open space. The hotels here are newer, larger and built for the working week, which makes them busy and dear midweek and often very good value from Friday. It is three or four tram stops to the old town and the tram is free, so the distance costs you nothing but a few minutes.

Belair and Limpertsberg: the residential west and north

QuietLocalTram or bus in

Two well-off residential quarters either side of the centre — Belair to the west, Limpertsberg up past the Glacis where the Schueberfouer is held — of avenues, gardens, schools and small shops. There are few hotels and a reasonable number of apartments, and they suit a longer stay or a family better than a two-night visit. Both are a short ride in on a free tram or bus, and Limpertsberg is within walking distance of the old town if you do not mind a slope.

Getting around Luxembourg City

On foot

The old town is small, flat on top and crossed in a quarter of an hour, and walking is how you should see it. The only real effort is the valley: the paths and staircases down to Grund are steep and slippery after rain, which is what the lifts are for.

The tram

A modern tram line runs from the station through the old town and across the Red Bridge to Kirchberg and beyond, and it is the backbone of getting about the capital. Like all public transport inside Luxembourg it is free: there is no ticket, nothing to validate and nothing to buy.

The lifts

Two public lifts cut out the climb between the upper city and the valley — one from the Plateau du Saint-Esprit down to Grund, and the panoramic glass one at Pfaffenthal that also connects to the tram stop on the Red Bridge. Both are free. Knowing they exist changes which parts of the city you are willing to stay in.

Trains and buses

The station is the centre of the national rail network, with the whole country within about an hour and a half and every journey inside Luxembourg free in standard class. City and regional buses cover everything the tram does not, including the airport at Findel, and cost nothing either.

Driving

A car is of no use in the capital and a nuisance in it: the old town is largely pedestrianised, the valley streets are narrow, and paid parking is the rule. If you are hiring one for the Mullerthal or the wine road, pick it up on the day you leave the city rather than on arrival.

When to come, and what it costs

Luxembourg City prices run on the working week rather than the tourist season, which is unusual and useful to know: the rooms fill from Monday to Thursday with people here for the banks and the institutions, and Friday to Sunday is often markedly cheaper for exactly the same hotel. Layered on top of that, the expensive dates are National Day on 23 June and the night before it, the fortnight of the Schueberfouer funfair from late August into early September, and the December market weekends. The cheapest stretch is January to March. The Guild would come on a weekend in May or September and let the business travellers pay for the rest of the year.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Luxembourg is in the Schengen area from its beginning, so the land borders with Belgium, Germany and France carry no checks at all and an arrival from outside Schengen is a single check at Findel airport. There is no separate permission of any kind to visit the Grand Duchy.

Luxembourg City — questions

Where should I stay for a first visit of two nights?

The Ville Haute, if the budget allows, because the old town is the reason you came and walking out of your hotel into it at eight in the morning before the day visitors arrive is the best thing the city offers. If the price is uncomfortable, take the station quarter instead: it is five minutes away by free tram, the rooms are better value, and you lose nothing except the walk home.

Is the station area safe?

Yes, with the ordinary caveat that applies to station districts everywhere. The two or three streets immediately around the station are the scruffiest in an otherwise very safe city and have a visible drug problem that locals discuss openly; you may find it unpleasant rather than dangerous. A block or two further into the quarter it is an unremarkable neighbourhood of hotels, restaurants and offices, and the city as a whole is one of the safest capitals in Europe.

How do I get in from the airport?

Findel is only about six kilometres from the centre and frequent buses run between the airport and the city and the station. Like all public transport inside the country they are free, so there is nothing to buy and no queue for a machine. Taxis are available and expensive by comparison, and there is very little reason to use one.

Is it worth staying down in Grund?

If atmosphere matters more to you than convenience, yes, and it is the part of the city people remember. You are on a river under a floodlit rampart, it is quiet at night, and the walk along the Alzette in the morning is lovely. Be clear-eyed about the climb: everything else is a hundred feet up, the staircases are steep, and your plan depends on using the free lifts. There is also very little to book down there, so decide early.

Do I need a car in the capital?

No, and you will be happier without one. The city is walkable, the tram and buses are free and frequent, the old town is largely closed to traffic and parking is paid and awkward. The whole country is reachable from the station by free train and bus, so hire a car only for the days you intend to spend in the Mullerthal ravines or on the wine road.

What is actually free, and what is not?

All public transport inside Luxembourg — train, tram and bus — is free to everybody, resident or visitor, with no ticket of any kind. First class on the trains is the exception and must be paid for. The two public lifts between the upper city and the valley are free as well. What the free travel does not cover is the foreign end of a cross-border journey, so if you are going on to Trier or Metz, check what your ticket needs to cover.
