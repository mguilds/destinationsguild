Uzbekistan Travel Guide

Researched and compiled by the Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

The Registan, Samarkand. Photo: Wikimedia Commons.

Uzbekistan holds three of the great cities of the Silk Road within a few hours of each other by train, and it is the rare country where the famous photograph turns out to undersell the place. Samarkand has the monuments, Bukhara has a whole old town still being lived in, and Khiva has a walled town that empties out at dusk and becomes something close to silent. This guide covers the four cities where our booking partner holds a real choice of rooms — those three plus Tashkent, the capital — and it is straight with you about the parts of the country where it holds few or none.

The regions of Uzbekistan

Six pieces of country. The three Silk Road cities sit on one railway line and are the reason most people come; the rest asks more of you. Where our partner lists rooms we link them, and where it lists none we say so.

Tashkent & the eastPART I

The capital and the way in: a city of four million rebuilt on a wide plan after the 1966 earthquake, with the Khast Imam complex, the Chorsu bazaar under its blue dome, and a metro whose older stations were built to be looked at. East of it the Fergana valley is the green, crowded, silk-and-ceramics heart of the country, and far less visited.

Samarkand & the Zarafshon valleyPART II

The one everybody has heard of, and it does not disappoint: the Registan with its three tiled madrasas, the ribbed dome of the Gur-e-Amir over Timur’s grave, and the lane of tombs at Shah-i-Zinda. Shahrisabz, Timur’s birthplace, is over the hills to the south.

Bukhara & the desertPART III

A whole working old town rather than a set of restored monuments — sand-coloured brick, the Kalyan minaret that Genghis Khan is said to have spared, covered trading domes still full of traders, and courtyard houses turned into small hotels. Many visitors end up liking Bukhara best.

Khiva & KhorezmPART IV

The far north-west, and the strangest of the three. The Itchan Kala is a walled inner town a few hundred metres across, almost entirely intact, which empties in the late afternoon when the day visitors go back to Urgench. Urgench itself has the airport and the main station.

Karakalpakstan & the AralPART V

The autonomous republic in the west, and the hardest travelling in the country. Nukus holds the Savitsky Museum — a collection of Soviet avant-garde painting saved from destruction and kept out here because out here nobody was watching — and beyond it Moynaq sits on the dry bed of what was the Aral Sea, ships rusting on the sand. Our partner lists rooms in Nukus; past that you are in the hands of a driver.

The south and the mountainsPART VI

Termez on the Afghan border has the country’s Buddhist archaeology, and the mountains east of Tashkent — Chimgan and the Charvak reservoir — are where the capital goes at weekends. We will not dress this up: our booking partner’s coverage thins out quickly here, and outside Termez we found little worth pointing a button at. These are places to reach with a driver and to book through whoever arranges him.

Before you book

Visas — and why we are not printing the rule

Uzbekistan has opened up a great deal since 2018: many nationalities now enter visa-free for a set number of days, and many more can apply for an electronic visa online. That is the shape of it. What we are not going to do is print the list or the day counts, because we could not reach the government’s own visa site from here today to read them, and an entry rule copied out of a travel article and left to go stale is exactly the kind of thing that leaves someone standing at a border. Check the official e-visa portal of the Republic of Uzbekistan, or the Uzbek embassy or consulate for your country, and check it close to the date you travel. If your trip includes a side trip into a neighbouring country, check that separately too.

Registration — the thing most visitors have not heard of

Uzbekistan has long expected visitors to be registered for the nights they spend in the country. If you sleep in hotels, this is done for you: the hotel registers you and gives you a small slip for each stay. Keep every slip until you have left. If you are staying in private homes, camping or travelling in a way that leaves gaps, look into what is currently required before you go rather than at the airport on the way out. The rules here have been relaxed more than once in recent years, which is a reason to check the current position rather than to assume either the old strictness or a clean slate.

Money

The currency is the Uzbek som. Cash still matters more than it does in most places a visitor will have come from, particularly outside Tashkent and in the small family-run hotels in Bukhara and Khiva, although cards are increasingly accepted in hotels and the better restaurants. Change money at banks and official exchange desks. We do not quote exchange rates on these pages; by the time you read it, any figure we printed would be wrong.

Getting about — take the train

The best thing about travelling in Uzbekistan is the railway. A high-speed service runs west from Tashkent through Samarkand to Bukhara, with slower trains on the same route and a rail connection on to Khorezm for Khiva. It is comfortable, punctual and far better than the road. Seats sell out in April, May, September and October, so book as soon as your dates are fixed. We do not print timetables or fares here because they change with the season — the railway’s own booking site has the current ones. Within cities, Tashkent has the metro; everywhere else, short taxi rides.

Fishing here

Uzbekistan is a doubly landlocked desert country, and what fishing there is happens on reservoirs such as Charvak above Tashkent and on the Amu Darya and Syr Darya rather than on anything a visitor would plan a trip around; the Aral Sea fishery that once supplied the whole region is largely gone. We have not written an Uzbekistan fishing guide, and rather than invent one we will point you at the shelf: the whole of it is in The Guild Fishing Library, and the nearest neighbour in spirit is our Mongolia fly fishing guide.

Uzbekistan questions, answered

How many days do you need in Uzbekistan?

Eight to ten for the classic run, which is Tashkent, Samarkand, Bukhara and Khiva. A workable shape is two nights in Tashkent, two or three in Samarkand, two or three in Bukhara and one or two in Khiva, moving between them by train. Five or six days is enough for Samarkand and Bukhara alone and makes a good first visit; two weeks lets you add the Fergana valley or the long haul out to Nukus and the dry Aral bed.

Which is better, Samarkand or Bukhara?

Samarkand has the greater monuments and Bukhara the better town. Samarkand’s Registan, Gur-e-Amir and Shah-i-Zinda are on a scale nothing in Bukhara matches, but they sit in a modern city and you travel between them. Bukhara has kept a whole low brick old town that you can walk out into at any hour, and most of its hotels are inside it. Visitors who care most about architecture tend to say Samarkand; visitors who care most about atmosphere tend to say Bukhara. Go to both — they are two hours apart by train.

When is the best time to visit Uzbekistan?

April and May, then September and October. This is a desert country with a hard continental climate: July afternoons average around 100°F (38°C) in Tashkent and about 104°F (40°C) in Bukhara and Khiva, and winter nights drop below freezing across the whole country. The two shoulder seasons are comfortable for walking, which is what sightseeing in these cities mostly is. Our month-by-month figures for Tashkent, Samarkand, Bukhara and Khiva are on the best time to visit Uzbekistan page.

Is Uzbekistan easy to travel in?

Easier than most first-time visitors expect. The train network between the main cities is good, hotels in the four cities covered here are used to foreign visitors, and the sites themselves are well kept and clearly signed. The two things that catch people out are the heat in high summer and the habit of assuming cards will work everywhere. English is not widely spoken outside hotels and tourist sites; Uzbek and Russian are.
