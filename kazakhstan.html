Kazakhstan Travel Guide

Researched and compiled by the Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Charyn Canyon, southeast of Almaty. Photo: Wikimedia Commons.

Kazakhstan is the ninth largest country on earth and the largest with no coastline on any ocean — roughly the size of Western Europe, with about twenty million people in it. Most of that space is steppe, and the steppe is the point: it is the old north road of the Silk Road, the ground the Golden Horde rode over, and the reason a traveller here spends as much time on a train or a plane as in a city. What most visitors come for sits at the two ends of the country’s spine. Almaty, the former capital and still the larger city, is pressed up against the snow line of the Tian Shan, with canyons, alpine lakes and a ski resort inside a morning’s drive. Astana, the capital since 1997, stands out on open steppe eight hundred miles north and is a twenty-first-century city built more or less from nothing — glass, steel and very cold winters. South of Almaty the Silk Road cities begin: Shymkent, and beyond it Turkestan and the great unfinished mausoleum of Khoja Ahmed Yasawi. This is a two-page Guild entry, a hub and a month-by-month page, and we will say plainly on it which rooms we have checked and which we have not.

The regions of Kazakhstan

Five parts of the country account for nearly every visit. Where our booking partner holds a real choice of rooms we give you a pinned link into its own city list; where it holds none we say so and leave the button off.

Almaty and the Tian ShanPART I

The country’s commercial capital and the city nearly everyone arrives in, laid out on a grid that tilts gently uphill toward a wall of mountains — which is how you navigate it, because “up” is always south, toward the peaks. The name comes from the apple: the wild Malus sieversii of these foothills is the ancestor of the domestic apple, and the orchards are still there. In the city there is the Central State Museum, the Soviet-era Ascension Cathedral standing in Panfilov Park without a single nail in it, and the Green Bazaar. Above the city, a road climbs to the Medeu skating rink and on to the Shymbulak ski area; further out lie Big Almaty Lake, the Kolsai Lakes, the drowned forest of Lake Kaindy and the red walls of Charyn Canyon. Four or five days here is not too many.

Astana and the northern steppePART II

The capital moved here from Almaty in 1997, and the city that followed was drawn on paper: a ceremonial axis running from the Khan Shatyr tent to the Bayterek tower and the presidential palace, with the Nur-Astana mosque and the opera house along the way. It is genuinely interesting to look at and genuinely hard in winter, when the wind comes off open grassland with nothing to slow it; this is one of the coldest capital cities in the world. Spend two days on the axis and the Nazarbayev Centre, then use the city as a hinge — the Talgo trains run south to Almaty overnight, and Burabay, the lake-and-pine country Kazakhs themselves take holidays in, is a few hours north by road.

Shymkent, Turkestan and the Silk Road southPART III

The warm end of the country and the oldest-settled one. Shymkent is the third city and the gateway; from it you reach Turkestan, where the mausoleum of Khoja Ahmed Yasawi has stood unfinished since Timur’s masons put down their tools in 1405, and which went on the UNESCO World Heritage list in 2003. Nearby are the ruins of Otrar and the Sauran walls, and the Aksu-Zhabagly nature reserve in the western Tian Shan. The food changes here too: rice dishes, laghman, melons, and tea taken seriously. Mangystau and the Caspian westPART IV

The strangest landscape in the country and the one photographers come for: chalk cliffs and lone rock towers standing out of a dry seabed at Bozzhyra, the Valley of Balls at Torysh, and underground mosques cut into the limestone at Shakpak-Ata and Beket-Ata. The regional city is Aktau, on the Caspian shore, and almost everything worth seeing is a long drive from it in a vehicle with high clearance and a driver who knows the tracks — this is not a region to improvise. We have not written a page for Aktau and we are not pinning a hotel here, because we have checked nothing on the ground; arrange the rooms with the same outfit that arranges the driver.

The east, the Altai and BaikonurPART V

East of Astana the steppe rises into the Kazakh Altai around Oskemen and Lake Markakol, which is forest and river country and the greenest part of Kazakhstan. North of Almaty, Altyn-Emel national park holds the Singing Dunes, which really do hum in dry wind. And out in the southern desert sits Baikonur, the oldest and largest working spaceport on earth, leased to Russia and reached only on an organised visit arranged well in advance — it is not a day trip and not somewhere to turn up hoping. We print no launch dates here: they move, and a date we printed would be wrong by the time you read it.

One honest note about those addresses

Two of the chain pages we read still print the capital’s former name, Nur-Sultan, in their own address block. The city was called Astana until 2019, Nur-Sultan from 2019, and Astana again from September 2022; the hotels have not all caught up with their own stationery. We have written Astana throughout, which is the city’s name today, and we are telling you about the discrepancy rather than quietly tidying it away — if you are filling in a booking form and it insists on Nur-Sultan, it means the same place.

Before you book

Money

The currency is the Kazakhstani tenge. Cards are accepted widely in Almaty, Astana and Shymkent — more widely than most visitors expect — and contactless payment is normal in city shops, cafes and taxis. Carry cash for bazaars, village stops, park entry gates and anything out in Mangystau or the Altai. We do not print exchange rates or room prices on these pages, because any figure we printed would be wrong by the time you read it.

Getting there, and getting about

The two international gateways are Almaty (ALA) and Astana (NQZ); Air Astana is the national carrier and FlyArystan its low-cost arm, and both airports take direct flights from Europe, the Gulf, Turkey and across Asia. Inside the country, distances are the thing you keep underestimating: Almaty to Astana is a short flight or an overnight train, and the trains are a genuinely good way to travel here — the Talgo services are modern, and the slower long-haul trains are how you see the steppe. Driving is on the right. Intercity roads between the big cities are good; beyond them, hire the vehicle with the driver. Both big cities have metered taxis and ride-hailing apps that work the way you would expect.

Entry rules, and why we are not printing them

Kazakhstan has spent the last several years loosening its entry rules, and the list of nationalities who may arrive without a visa has changed more than once in that time. That is exactly the kind of fact we refuse to print: a visa rule copied into a travel page goes stale quietly and costs somebody a flight. Check your own passport’s position with the Kazakh Ministry of Foreign Affairs or your nearest Kazakh embassy or consulate, and check it again close to departure. Registration requirements for longer stays are worth asking about at the same time — your hotel normally handles this for you, but ask rather than assume.

Fishing here

Kazakhstan has a great deal of water for a country with no sea: the Ili and Irtysh rivers, Lake Balkhash, the Caspian shore in the west, and the Altai lakes in the east, which hold grayling and taimen. We have not written a Kazakhstan fishing guide, and rather than invent one we will point you at the shelf — the whole of it is in The Guild Fishing Library. If you want to fish while you are here, arrange it through a licensed local outfit and ask about permits and closed seasons when you book rather than when you arrive, because several of these waters are protected and the rules are not the same from one oblast to the next.

Kazakhstan questions, answered

How many days do you need in Kazakhstan?

A week is the honest minimum for a country this size, and ten days is better. Four or five days in Almaty, with a day out to Charyn Canyon and another up to Big Almaty Lake or Shymbulak, is a complete trip on its own. Add two or three days in Astana for the capital’s axis and its museums, and travel between the two on the overnight train rather than flying if you have the time — the steppe out of the window is part of what you came for. If you want the Silk Road south as well, or Mangystau in the west, you are planning a second week and not a side trip.

Is Kazakhstan safe for visitors?

The U.S. State Department currently places Kazakhstan at Level 1, “exercise normal precautions”, which is its lowest advisory level and the same level it gives much of Western Europe. We checked that today rather than from memory; the State Department’s own pages would not answer our fetcher, so we read it off current reporting and we would rather tell you that than pretend otherwise. Check travel.state.gov yourself before you book, because advisory levels change. The ordinary cautions apply as they would anywhere: watch your pockets in the bazaars, use metered taxis or an app rather than an unmarked car, and do not improvise a trip into Mangystau or the Altai without a driver who knows the ground.

Almaty or Astana — which should you fly into?

Almaty, for a first visit, almost every time. It is the bigger city, the greener one, the one with mountains at the end of the street and the one with most of the country’s day trips within reach. Astana is worth two or three days and is genuinely unlike anywhere else, but it is a capital built for the state rather than for visitors, and in deep winter it is hard going. The practical answer is to fly into one and out of the other, and let the train or a one-hour flight join them up.

What should you eat in Kazakhstan?

Beshbarmak is the national dish — boiled meat, usually horse or lamb, over wide flat noodles with onion broth, eaten with the hands at a table of people, which is what the name means. Around it you will meet kazy, horse sausage; baursak, fried dough that arrives with everything; manty, steamed dumplings; and in the south, rice dishes and laghman noodles that belong to the Silk Road rather than the steppe. To drink, there is tea constantly, and if you are offered kumys, fermented mare’s milk, or shubat, its camel-milk cousin, try it — it is sour, fizzy and much older than anything else on the table.
