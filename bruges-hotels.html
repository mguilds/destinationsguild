Bruges

Where to Stay in Bruges

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Bruges is a small medieval city that survived by being left behind: the sea channel that made it rich silted up in the fifteenth century, the money went to Antwerp, and the stopped clock is now the whole attraction. The historic centre sits inside an egg-shaped ring of canals and is almost entirely walkable in half an hour end to end. The single most useful thing to know is that between about ten in the morning and five in the afternoon the centre belongs to coach parties and day-trippers from Brussels, and before and after that it belongs to about twenty thousand residents and the people who stayed the night. Where you sleep here is largely a question of how far from that daytime crush you want to be.

The Markt and the Burg — the middle of it all

Most centralBelfry on the doorstepBusiest by day

The two great squares sit side by side: the Markt with the Belfry and its 366 steps, and the Burg with the town hall and the Basilica of the Holy Blood. Hotels on and immediately around them are the most expensive in the city and the most convenient by a distance — you walk out of the door into the picture. They are also at the centre of the daytime crowd and of the carillon, which rings on the hour and the quarter and does not stop for guests. If you want to be in the heart of it and will be out walking most of the day, this is the obvious choice; ask about the bells and about a courtyard-facing room.

The north-east — Sint-Anna and the windmills

QuietestResidentialTen minutes in

Walk north-east from the Burg and within five minutes the coaches stop. This is the quiet quarter: brick almshouses, the Jerusalem Chapel, the lace centre, and the old windmills standing on the ramparts along the eastern canal. The hotels and guesthouses here are smaller and often family-run in converted houses, the prices are gentler than on the Markt, and you still walk into the centre in ten minutes. For a second or third visit to Bruges, or for anybody who wants to feel that they are staying in a town rather than an attraction, this is the part we would pick.

’t Zand and the station side — the west

Easiest arrivalModern hotelsGood value

Bruges station sits just outside the ring to the south-west, and the square called ’t Zand is the modern end of the centre: a big open space with the concert hall on it, rebuilt rather than preserved, and a weekly market. Hotels here tend to be larger and newer, with lifts and parking, and they are usually cheaper than the equivalent room near the Markt. It is a flat ten to fifteen minute walk in along shopping streets, or five minutes by bus. If you are arriving by train with luggage, or driving — and the historic centre is a difficult place to drive — this side of town makes the practical argument.

The Minnewater and the Begijnhof — the southern edge

Prettiest walk inCalm eveningsParkside

The southern corner of the egg holds the Minnewater, the so-called Lake of Love, and beside it the Begijnhof, a walled court of white houses under lime trees that was founded in the thirteenth century and is still a quiet religious house. The streets here are residential and green, the canal walk into the centre takes about ten minutes and is the loveliest approach in the city, and the evenings are properly still. It suits a slower trip. The one caution is that it is a fair walk from the Markt with a suitcase over cobbles, so consider how you will get from the station or the car park.

Getting around

Walk. The historic centre is about two kilometres across at its widest and there is nothing in this guide that is more than twenty-five minutes on foot from the Markt. The surface is cobbles almost everywhere, which is hard on wheeled luggage, on heels and on anybody unsteady, so flat shoes matter more here than in most cities. De Lijn buses circle the ring and connect the station to the centre if you would rather not walk in; a contactless card or a ticket from the machine covers it. Cars are a liability — much of the centre is restricted and the underground car park at ’t Zand is usually the sensible answer. Bruges is also a serious cycling town and the ride out along the canal to Damme is flat, pretty and about seven kilometres.

When to come, and what it costs

May, June and September are the best of it: long light, the terraces out, and the canal boats running without the high-summer queues. July and August are the busiest weeks of the year and the centre at midday can be genuinely uncomfortable. The winter market and the ice sculptures bring a second, shorter rush around December, and January and February are cold, quiet and the cheapest the city gets — with the useful detail that the museums are empty and several restaurants that need booking weeks ahead in summer will seat you on the night. Whatever the month, the rhythm of the day matters more than the season: be out early, go in somewhere at lunchtime, and come out again at five when the coaches have gone.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS — the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years — is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today — nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Belgium is about as straightforward as Europe gets. It is a founding member of both the European Union and the Schengen area, it uses the euro, and its land borders with the Netherlands, Germany, Luxembourg and France are internal Schengen crossings that you will cross on a train or a motorway without noticing. Arriving by air from another Schengen country means no passport control at all. The one border that is still a proper frontier is the Channel: the Eurostar to and from London is checked at the station before you board, so allow the time the operator asks for rather than turning up as you would for a domestic train.

Staying in Bruges — questions

Is it worth staying the night in Bruges rather than visiting for the day?

This is the one piece of advice we would press on anybody. The great majority of visitors arrive after ten in the morning and leave before six, and for those hours the centre is as crowded as any attraction in Europe. If you stay, you get the city at seven in the morning and at nine at night, lit, nearly empty and quiet enough to hear your own footsteps on the cobbles, which is what people actually mean when they say Bruges is beautiful. One night changes the trip entirely.

How many days does Bruges need?

Two nights is the sweet spot. One full day covers the Markt, the Burg, the Belfry climb, a canal boat and the Groeningemuseum without rushing. A second day lets you add the Begijnhof and the Minnewater, the quiet north-east and the windmills, or the flat bicycle ride out to Damme. Three nights is not too many if you are using it as a base for Ghent or the coast, but the city itself does not need a week.

Do I need to speak Dutch?

No. Bruges is in Flanders and the language is Dutch &mdash; Flemish is the usual name for the local varieties of it &mdash; but English is spoken easily and everywhere in a city that has lived on visitors for a century. A dag or a dank u is appreciated and nothing more is expected. Signs are in Dutch, so it is worth knowing that Brugge is Bruges, Gent is Ghent and Antwerpen is Antwerp when you are reading a departure board.

Is Bruges difficult if you do not walk well?

It can be, and it is worth planning around. The centre is flat, which helps, but it is cobbled almost throughout and the cobbles are large, uneven and slippery in rain. The Belfry has 366 narrow steps and no lift. Canal boats involve a step down into the boat. The practical answer is to stay close to the Markt or near &rsquo;t Zand rather than at the far edges, to use the buses on the ring, and to treat the horse-drawn carriages and the boats as the sightseeing rather than the walking.

Can I see Bruges and Ghent in the same trip?

Easily, and they complement each other. They are about half an hour apart by train, with several services an hour. Bruges is the smaller, prettier, more visited of the two; Ghent is bigger, younger because of the university, and feels like a working city with a medieval centre rather than a preserved one. If you have three nights in Flanders, two in Bruges and one in Ghent is a good division, and Brussels or Antwerp is a short hop from either.
