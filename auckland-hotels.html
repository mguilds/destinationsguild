Auckland

Where to Stay in Auckland

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Auckland holds about a third of all New Zealanders and is spread across two harbours and fifty old volcanic cones, which means it is much bigger on the ground than its skyline suggests and that the word “central” covers a lot of ground. For most visitors the choice is simple enough: the city centre if you are here for a night or two between flights, the waterfront if you want the harbour at your feet, Ponsonby if you came to eat, and Devonport if you would rather arrive by ferry each evening. Below is what each is like to walk out of in the morning.

Where to stay in Auckland, area by area

Five parts of the city, what each is actually like, and who each one suits.

City centre - Queen Street and Britomart

Most convenientTransport hubBest for a short stay

The obvious base, and the right one if your time in the city is short. Britomart station sits at the bottom of Queen Street and is where the trains, most buses and the airport coach converge, with the ferry terminal next door; the Sky Tower, the galleries and the museums are a walk or a short bus ride away. This is where the big international chains and the serviced apartments are, so rates soften at weekends when the business traffic goes home. Two honest caveats: Queen Street itself is a working main street rather than a pretty one, and the city is still finishing a long programme of transport works, so expect hoardings and a detour or two.

The Viaduct and Wynyard Quarter

Harbour viewsRestaurantsWalk to the centre

The old working harbour west of the ferry terminal, rebuilt as a waterfront quarter of marinas, restaurants and apartment towers. It is a five to fifteen minute flat walk from Britomart, it is where most of the harbour cruises and the America's Cup bases are, and the eating is better and more expensive than in the centre. This is the area that photographs well and delivers on it, particularly on a summer evening. It is quieter in winter, and a harbour-view room here costs real money - the inland side of the same buildings is markedly cheaper for the same bed.

Ponsonby and Grey Lynn

Where Auckland eatsVillas and boutiquesBus or 25-minute walk

A ridge of restored timber villas a mile west of the centre, and the city's eating and drinking district: Ponsonby Road runs for a mile of restaurants, cafes, bars and small shops, with Karangahape Road at its eastern end. Accommodation here is boutique hotels, guesthouses and apartments rather than towers, and the atmosphere is residential in the best sense. It is an easy bus ride or a twenty-five-minute walk into town, though the walk involves a real hill in each direction. Our pick if you have more than two nights and want to feel you are living somewhere.

Parnell and Newmarket

QuieterMuseum and gardensGood value for space

East and south-east of the centre, on the other ridge. Parnell is the oldest suburb in the city, with a village high street, the Anglican cathedral, the Domain parkland and the Auckland War Memorial Museum at the top of it; Newmarket, just beyond, is the shopping district and a train stop. Rooms tend to be larger for the money than in the centre and the streets are quiet at night. You will use a bus, a train or a twenty-minute walk to get downtown, and the walk back up from the Domain is steeper than it looks on a map.

Devonport and the North Shore

Arrive by ferryBeachesNot for a one-night stay

Across the harbour, twelve minutes on the passenger ferry from the city terminal, Devonport is a Victorian seaside village with cafes, two old volcanic headlands to climb and views straight back at the skyline. The ferry runs frequently until late and the crossing is one of the pleasures of the city. Stay here for a relaxed few days, a family trip or a beach-and-ferry rhythm; do not stay here if you have an early flight or a single night, because the ferry does not run all night and a taxi round the harbour bridge is long and dear.

Getting around

The AT HOP card covers the trains, buses and the harbour ferries and is worth buying on arrival; it is cheaper per trip than paper fares and the same card works on everything. Trains run on four lines from Britomart, buses are frequent along the main ridges, and the ferries are both transport and sightseeing. One fare rule catches visitors out: your journey is priced by the zones you cross, and transfers within the time limit are free, so tagging on and off properly matters.

From the airport, the dedicated coach to the city runs through the day and evening and takes about an hour in traffic; there is no rail link. Allow more time than the timetable says on a weekday afternoon. In the city itself, central Auckland is walkable along the flat harbour edge and distinctly hilly the moment you turn inland, which is the single thing visitors most often misjudge. Taxis and ride-hailing are plentiful and metered.

When to come, and what it costs

Auckland is mild all year and never really closes. December to March is the season: warm, humid, the harbour busy with sails, the beaches on both coasts in use and the city emptying out over Christmas as locals head to the coast. April and May are our own pick for the city itself - still warm, far quieter, and the rates noticeably lower. Winter, June to August, is grey and wet rather than cold, and it is the cheapest and easiest time to get a good room at short notice.

On price, the city follows business travel: midweek is dearest and weekends are better value, which is the opposite of most holiday destinations and worth planning around. The exception is the Christmas and New Year fortnight and any weekend with a test match or a large concert at Eden Park, when the whole city fills. Getting in: what you need, and what you pay

Most visitors from visa-waiver countries need a New Zealand Electronic Travel Authority, the NZeTA, requested before you fly, and most also pay the International Visitor Conservation and Tourism Levy, which is normally collected at the same time. A separate New Zealand Traveller Declaration is completed shortly before you arrive and has replaced the old paper arrival card.

Both the NZeTA fee and the levy have changed more than once in recent years, so we do not print the figures here — read them off the official page at immigration.govt.nz when you book, and request the NZeTA through Immigration New Zealand’s own app or website rather than any agent offering to do it for you. Australian citizens need neither; Australian permanent residents who are not citizens do need the NZeTA. The airline checks it before you board, so do it when you book rather than at the airport.

Take the biosecurity questions on arrival seriously: declare all food, and clean the soil off boots, tents and fishing tackle before you pack them. Declaring something costs nothing; not declaring it can cost you an instant fine. The full picture is in our New Zealand travel guide.

Staying in Auckland — questions

Is Auckland city centre a good place to stay?

Yes, for convenience, and particularly for a short stay or a night either side of a flight. Britomart puts the trains, the buses, the airport coach and the ferries within a few minutes of each other, and the main sights are walkable. What the centre does not give you is charm: Queen Street is a busy commercial street and the ongoing transport works mean hoardings in places. If you have three nights or more, Ponsonby or Parnell will give you a nicer week for similar money.

How do I get from Auckland airport to the city?

The straightforward way is the dedicated airport coach, which runs through the day and much of the evening and takes about an hour to the city centre depending on traffic. There is no train to the airport. Taxis and ride-hailing cars are available at both terminals and cost several times the coach fare. If you land late, check the last departure before you plan to use the coach, and if you are staying on the North Shore allow considerably longer for the harbour bridge.

Do I need a car in Auckland?

No, and it is a nuisance to have one. Central parking is expensive, the one-way system is awkward and the motorways through the city are slow at both peaks. The trains, buses and ferries cover everything a visitor wants. If your trip includes the Bay of Islands, the Coromandel or a drive south, the sensible pattern is to see Auckland first without a car and pick the hire car up on the day you leave - the city-centre depots and the airport both work for this.

Which Auckland beaches are worth the trip?

The city sits between two different coasts, which is unusual and worth using. On the eastern, harbour side the beaches at Mission Bay and St Heliers are calm, close and reachable by bus - good for a swim and an ice cream. On the western, Tasman side, Piha and Muriwai are dramatic black-sand surf beaches about forty-five minutes' drive away, with strong rip currents; swim only between the flags when the surf club is patrolling, and treat that advice as serious rather than cautious.
