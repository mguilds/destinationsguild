Where to Stay in Krakow

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Krakow is a small city with a very concentrated middle, which makes choosing where to sleep easier here than in most European capitals — and raises the stakes on one question: how close to the main square can you bear to be? The Rynek is magnificent and it is also loud until two in the morning at weekends. What follows is the four parts of town visitors actually stay in, what each is like to walk out of in the morning, and who each one suits.

Where to stay in Krakow, area by area

Four parts of the city, what each is really like, and the trade-off each one asks of you.

The Guild does not name a hotel until an Explorer has confirmed it on the hotel’s own website, so this guide sets out the areas and sends you to the full, current list for each one rather than printing a shortlist we cannot stand behind yet. Named picks will be added to this page as they are checked.

Stare Miasto — the Old Town inside the Planty ring

Most centralWalk everywhereNoisy at weekends

The historic core, ringed by the Planty — a band of park laid out where the city walls stood, which makes the Old Town an easy shape to understand and about twenty minutes to cross on foot. Everything most people come for is inside it or just outside: the Rynek Główny with the Cloth Hall, St Mary’s basilica and its hourly trumpeter, the Jagiellonian University’s old college, and the Wawel castle and cathedral on their hill at the southern edge. Staying here means walking out of the door into the thing itself, and it is what we would choose for a first visit of two or three nights. Two honest drawbacks. The square and the streets off it are genuinely loud at weekends, when Krakow takes a great deal of stag and hen traffic, so ask for a courtyard room rather than one over the street. And the whole area is cobbled with large uneven stones, which matters more than any hill.

Kazimierz — the old Jewish quarter, and where Krakow eats

Restaurants and barsAtmospheric15 minutes to the square

South-east of the Old Town and the most interesting part of the city to stay in. Kazimierz was a separate town for centuries and the Jewish quarter of Krakow until the war; seven synagogues survive, several in use, along with the old cemeteries and the Galicia Jewish Museum, and the district carries its history plainly rather than prettily. Since the nineties it has also become the eating and drinking quarter: Plac Nowy with its round market hall and its late-night snack windows, the restaurants along Szeroka and Józefa, and more small bars per street than anywhere else in Poland. It is fifteen minutes’ walk or a short tram ride to the main square. Rooms are a little cheaper than inside the ring and often in converted townhouses with real character. It has its own noise, concentrated around Plac Nowy, but it is a local noise rather than a stag-party one.

Podgórze — over the river, quieter and better value

QuieterBetter valueTrams and a footbridge

Across the Vistula from Kazimierz, reached by the Bernatka footbridge in five minutes, Podgórze is where a good many returning visitors now stay. It is a real neighbourhood rather than a visitor district, with its own market, ordinary prices and a hill with a view over the whole city. It also holds some of the most important places in Krakow: Ghetto Heroes Square, where the ghetto was cleared and where sixty-eight empty chairs now stand as a memorial; the Eagle Pharmacy museum on the square; Schindler’s Enamel Factory, now a museum of the occupation; and MOCAK, the contemporary art museum, next door to it. Rooms cost noticeably less than over the water and the trams into town are frequent. The trade-off is simply that you are twenty-five minutes’ walk from the main square rather than in it.

Around the main station — Grzegórzki and the new centre

Arrivals and departuresGood valueShopping and transport

The quarter immediately east and north-east of the Old Town, around Kraków Główny station and the Galeria Krakowska shopping centre. Nobody comes to Krakow for this part of town, and it earns its place here on pure convenience: the airport train terminates at the station, every intercity train and long-distance bus leaves from the same complex, and it is a flat ten-minute walk through the Planty to the main square. The hotels are mostly modern, mid-market and international, with lifts, air conditioning and larger rooms than the old centre can offer, and they are usually the best value in the city. Choose it for an early flight, a late arrival, a single night between trains, or if steps and cobbles are a concern. Do not choose it for atmosphere, because there is none.

Getting around

Krakow is small enough that walking is the default: the Old Town to Kazimierz takes fifteen minutes, Kazimierz to Podgórze another five over the footbridge. The Old Town itself is largely closed to cars, so a hire car is a genuine nuisance here and we would not bother with one unless you are heading into the mountains afterwards.

For everything else there are trams, which are frequent, cheap and easy to follow, plus buses where the trams do not reach. One ticket covers both and is sold by time rather than by journey, so a twenty-minute ticket lets you change as often as you like within it. Buy from the machines at the stops or on board the newer trams, both of which take contactless cards, and validate it as you get on. Ticket inspections are frequent and fines are issued on the spot.

From the airport, the train to Kraków Główny takes about twenty minutes and runs every half hour; it is much the simplest way in. Taxis and ride-hailing apps both work well and are inexpensive by western European standards. For Auschwitz-Birkenau and Wieliczka, scheduled buses and trains go from near the main station, and booking the museum entry in advance matters far more than how you travel there.

When to come, and what it costs

Krakow is a year-round city break, with one caveat: July and August are very busy indeed, and the main square in August is shoulder to shoulder. The city is at its best in late May, June and September, when the weather is kind, the tables are out and you can still get a table. December brings one of the best Christmas markets in Europe to the Rynek, which is worth planning a trip around, and January and February are cold, quiet and much the cheapest months to come.

On price, Krakow is no longer the bargain it was, and in high summer and over the Christmas market weekends it is priced much like any popular European city. Where it is still very good value is midweek out of season, and in the modern hotels just outside the Old Town ring rather than the townhouses inside it. Poland uses the Polish zloty (PLN, written zł), not the euro. Poland is a member of the European Union but has kept its own currency and has set no date for changing it, so euro notes are not legal tender and the few places that take them give a poor rate. Pay in zloty. Cards and phones are accepted almost everywhere, including on trams, buses and at ticket machines, and you need very little cash — though a small amount is handy for market stalls, church donation boxes and the odd rural bus. Prices in shops include tax. Tipping is modest and not chased: rounding up, or ten per cent for good table service, is plenty. Decline any card machine that offers to charge you in dollars; pay in zloty and let your own bank convert. Beware the standalone currency-exchange booths at the airports, which are much worse than the ones in town or simply drawing cash from a bank machine.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS — the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years — is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today — nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Poland is a straightforward case. It has been in the European Union since 2004 and in the Schengen area since 2007, so everything above applies here in full, and flying in from Berlin, Vienna or Amsterdam involves no passport control at all. What Poland has not joined is the euro, so the currency is the zloty. Note too that Poland’s eastern border — with Belarus and with the Russian enclave at Kaliningrad — is an external Schengen frontier and is not a casual crossing; the crossings into Ukraine are open but busy. None of this affects an ordinary trip to the cities in this guide.

Staying in Krakow — questions

Which area is best for a first visit to Krakow?

The Old Town inside the Planty ring, if your budget runs to it and noise is not a worry. Krakow's centre is compact enough that staying in it means walking out of your door into the main square, the Cloth Hall and the Wawel hill, and for a two or three night visit that convenience is worth paying for. If you would rather have character and good food than the shortest possible walk, stay in Kazimierz instead and accept a quarter of an hour on foot. If you want value and quiet, cross the river to Podgorze.

Is the Old Town too noisy to sleep in?

At weekends, on the square and the streets immediately off it, it genuinely can be. Krakow receives a lot of stag and hen traffic and the bars on and around the Rynek run late. This is easily managed rather than a reason to stay away: ask for a room on a courtyard or an upper floor away from the street, avoid the handful of streets directly off the square if you are a light sleeper, and note that midweek is much calmer than Friday and Saturday. Kazimierz has its own late-night noise around Plac Nowy, and Podgorze and the station quarter are quiet.

How do you get to Auschwitz-Birkenau from Krakow?

Buses and trains run from near Krakow's main station to Oswiecim, about an hour and a half each way. The important part is not the journey but the booking: entry to the memorial is free, but a personal entry pass must be reserved in advance on the museum's own website, and in summer it goes weeks ahead. In the busiest hours a guided tour is required rather than optional. Allow a full day, dress for standing outdoors, and treat it as the memorial and burial ground it is. The museum itself does not recommend it for children under fourteen.

Is Krakow walkable, and what about the cobbles?

Very walkable, and almost entirely flat. The Old Town is about twenty minutes across, Kazimierz is fifteen minutes from the square and Podgorze five minutes beyond that. The one real obstacle is the surface rather than the distance: the Old Town and much of Kazimierz are laid with large, uneven granite setts that are hard going with a wheeled suitcase and genuinely awkward with a stick or a frame. Sensible shoes matter, and so does asking about a lift before booking a townhouse hotel.

Do I need to stay near the station for an early flight?

It helps, but it is not essential. The airport train from Krakow Glowny takes about twenty minutes and runs from early morning, and the Old Town is a flat ten-minute walk from the station through the park, so even a central hotel is manageable. For a flight before about seven in the morning, when the train has not yet started, the modern hotels around the station are the easier choice and are usually cheaper than the centre anyway.

Is Kazimierz a good place to stay with children?

It can be, with one thing to think about. The district is full of good, informal places to eat, the streets are flat, and the distances are short, all of which suits a family. The noise around Plac Nowy on a Friday or Saturday night is the consideration, so look a few streets away from it rather than on it. Podgorze, over the footbridge, is quieter again and has a park and a market, and the station quarter has the modern hotels with family rooms and lifts.
