Ayia Napa

Where to Stay in Ayia Napa

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

The south-east corner has the best sand in Cyprus by a distance — fine, pale and backed by clear shallow water — and it has two quite different holidays built on it. Ayia Napa itself grew from a fishing village into a resort with a famous beach at Nissi, a small harbour, a sixteenth-century monastery improbably intact in the middle of the square, and a nightlife reputation that still shapes what people expect of it. Ten minutes up the coast, around the headland at Cape Greco, Protaras and Pernera are the family half of the same coastline: the same sand and water, a fraction of the noise, and a coastal path between the bays instead of a club strip. Choosing between them is the single most important decision on this page, and people get it wrong in both directions.

Nissi Beach and the west end

The famous sandYoung and loud in seasonWalk to the strip

Nissi is the beach that put the resort on the map: a long curve of pale sand with a small islet you can wade out to, water that stays shallow a long way out, and a line of beach bars behind it. The hotels along this stretch are the busiest and the most party-minded in Cyprus, and in July and August the beach itself runs music through the afternoon. It is a wonderful beach and an exhausting address for anyone who wants an early night. Perfect for groups in their twenties; a mistake for most families with young children, who should look east.

The centre and the harbour

Everything on footRestaurantsThe club streets are here

The middle of Ayia Napa holds the monastery, the square, the bus stops and the great majority of the restaurants, with the fishing harbour — genuinely pretty, and the departure point for the boat trips — a few minutes downhill. Staying central means you walk to everything and need a taxi for nothing. It also means you are near the club streets, which in season run until dawn and are concentrated in a small area around the square. A room three or four streets back, towards the harbour side, keeps the convenience and loses most of the noise.

Protaras and Fig Tree Bay

Best for familiesCalm waterQuiet evenings

Round the cape and about fifteen minutes north, Protaras is the answer for anybody who wants this coastline without the late nights. Fig Tree Bay is the main beach — sheltered, shallow, with a rocky islet offshore and a coastal footpath running north and south from it — and the strip behind is restaurants and ice cream rather than clubs. The crowd is families and older couples, the evening ends earlier, and the sand is every bit as good as Nissi’s. If you are travelling with children or want to sleep, book here and visit Ayia Napa for the day.

Pernera and Kapparis

Quietest on the coastSmall baysNeed wheels for Ayia Napa

North again from Protaras, Pernera and then Kapparis string along a coast of small sandy coves and low-rise apartment blocks, ending near the edge of the buffer zone where the road stops. This is the calmest stretch of the south-east and the most residential; a good deal of the accommodation is self-catering. You get the sea and the quiet and very little else, and you will drive or take the bus for a change of scene. Cape Greco: the headland in between

Day trips onlySea caves and archesNo accommodation

Between the two resorts the land rises into a national forest park of low cliffs, juniper, sea caves and a natural limestone arch, with a coastal path and cycle route running right round it. This is the best walking in the district and the thing most sunbed holidays never see; the swimming at Konnos Bay on the Protaras side is the finest on the coast. There is nowhere to stay on the headland itself — it is protected — so base yourself at either end and come for the morning.

Getting around Ayia Napa

On foot

Within Ayia Napa the centre, the harbour and the nearer beaches are an easy flat walk. Nissi is about half an hour west of the square on foot, which is further than it looks in the heat. Protaras is a separate town and not walkable from Ayia Napa — the distance round the cape is a good deal more than the map suggests.

Buses

The local network is better than in most Cypriot resorts, with frequent services along the coast between Ayia Napa, Protaras, Pernera and Paralimni and regular connections to Larnaca and its airport. In summer an open-top tourist bus runs the same route. Services drop sharply out of season, when a good deal of the coast closes down altogether.

Driving and parking

A car makes Cape Greco, the sea caves and the villages inland easy, and is otherwise optional if you are staying put. Driving is on the left. Parking in Ayia Napa centre is difficult on summer evenings and the beach car parks at Nissi and Fig Tree Bay fill early in the day; arriving before about ten in the morning solves both. The motorway runs to Larnaca in around forty minutes.

Boats

Trips run all season from Ayia Napa harbour: round the cape to the sea caves and the arch, along the coast to the Blue Lagoon at the tip of the island’s eastern shore, and glass-bottomed boats for families. It is the best way to see the coastline and the one activity we would put ahead of another day on a sunbed.

When to come, and what it costs

This coast runs on a short, intense season. From June to September it is hot, dry, expensive and extremely busy, with July and August the peak in every sense; the sea reaches its warmest from late August through October. May and early June, and the second half of September into October, give you the same sand and water with noticeably fewer people and lower prices, and are what the Guild would book. From November to March a great deal of Ayia Napa simply closes — hotels, restaurants and bars shut for the winter and the place becomes a quiet, half-empty town, which some people seek out and most visitors should know about before booking a winter week here.

Getting in: Cyprus is in the EU but outside Schengen

The Republic of Cyprus belongs to the European Union and uses the euro, but it is not in the Schengen area. Days you spend in Cyprus do not count against the ninety days in any hundred and eighty that Schengen allows, and days spent in Schengen countries do not count against your Cyprus allowance; the two are tracked separately. Cyprus is also outside the EU’s Entry/Exit System, so there is no biometric registration on arrival. Citizens of the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, Australia and the European Union need no visa for a holiday of up to ninety days. British advice is a passport issued within the last ten years and valid three months beyond departure; United States advice recommends six months of validity. Confirm your own nationality’s terms with your own foreign ministry.

One rule matters more than any other here. Enter the Republic only through its own points of entry — the airports at Larnaca and Paphos and the southern seaports. Arriving through the north and crossing south is treated as illegal entry and can mean a fine or a refusal. Crossing north for a day from the south is the opposite: legal, routine and well worth doing, with your passport in your pocket. Time spent in the north still counts towards your ninety days, there are limits on goods brought back, and many hire companies restrict taking their cars across — check your rental agreement first.

Ayia Napa — questions

Ayia Napa or Protaras?

Protaras for families, couples and anyone who wants to sleep; Ayia Napa for groups who want the nightlife and do not mind it. They share the same coastline and the same quality of sand, and they are fifteen minutes apart, so whichever you choose the other is an easy day out. The commonest booking mistake in Cyprus is a family taking a cheap deal at the Nissi Beach end in August and discovering what the evening sounds like.

Is Ayia Napa only about the clubbing?

No, though the reputation is earned and the club strip is real. The clubbing is concentrated in a few streets in the centre and in a short summer season; outside that area the town has a working fishing harbour, a beautiful sixteenth-century monastery in the main square, a marine life museum and some of the best beaches in the Mediterranean. Plenty of families stay in Ayia Napa perfectly happily by choosing the eastern side of town rather than Nissi.

When does everything close for winter?

Roughly from the end of October to the middle of March, though it varies year by year and by business. Ayia Napa in January is a strange and rather melancholy place: the sand and the sea are still there, the weather is mild, and most of the hotels, restaurants and bars have their shutters down. Protaras and Paralimni keep rather more open because people live there year-round. If you want this coast out of season, check directly with the hotel that it is actually trading.

Do I need a car?

Not if you are staying in one place and using the beach, because the coastal bus between Ayia Napa, Protaras and Paralimni is frequent and cheap in season and Larnaca airport is a direct bus ride. A car is worth it if you want Cape Greco early in the morning, the inland villages, or days out to the rest of the island, since this corner is the furthest from everywhere else.

How far is the rest of Cyprus?

Larnaca and its airport are about forty minutes by motorway, Nicosia around an hour, Limassol an hour and a half, and Paphos closer to two and a quarter. That makes the south-east excellent for a beach week and the least convenient base for touring the island. If you want both, most people take a few nights here and a few somewhere more central.
