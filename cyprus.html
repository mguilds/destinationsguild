Cyprus

The Cyprus Travel Guide

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Cyprus is a small, hot, unhurried island at the far eastern end of the Mediterranean, closer to Beirut than to Athens, with more layers of history per square mile than almost anywhere and one complication no honest guide can skip. Since 1974 the island has been divided. The Republic of Cyprus governs the south, belongs to the European Union and uses the euro; the north is administered by Turkish Cypriots and is recognised by no country but Turkey. A United Nations buffer zone runs the width of the island and straight through the middle of Nicosia, which is the last divided capital in Europe. The Guild’s guides cover the Republic, where virtually every visitor lands, sleeps and books, and we set out plainly further down what crossing to the north does and does not involve. Everything else here is refreshingly simple: nowhere in the Republic is much more than two hours from anywhere else, the driving is on the left, English is spoken more or less universally, and you can swim before breakfast and walk in a mountain pine forest after lunch.

Why visit Cyprus

The season runs long after everywhere else has closed

Cyprus is the warmest corner of the Mediterranean and it holds that warmth stubbornly. The sea is at its most comfortable from the end of summer well into the autumn, long after Greece and Italy have cooled, and the coastal winter is mild enough that people walk the promenades in shirtsleeves in January. If your holidays fall outside the school summer, this is one of the few places in Europe that rewards you for it.

The archaeology is first rank and still uncrowded

The mosaic floors at Kato Paphos, the Roman theatre at Kourion above the sea, the neolithic village at Choirokoitia and the painted Byzantine churches hidden in the Troodos are all on the UNESCO list, and on most days you will share them with very few people. Nothing here has the queues of Pompeii or the Acropolis, and almost all of it is open sky and loose stone rather than museum glass.

Mountains stand behind every beach

The Troodos range rises to just under two thousand metres in the middle of the island, which means pine forest, cold streams, hill villages with one taverna, and in a good winter enough snow to ski on. The drive from the coast takes around an hour from almost anywhere, so the mountains are a day trip rather than a separate holiday.

It is an unusually easy country to travel in

English is spoken almost everywhere, road signs are in Greek and English, the plugs and the driving side are British, the currency is the euro, and the health and food standards are the EU’s. For nervous travellers, first-timers and anyone bringing older parents or small children, Cyprus asks very little of you.

The food is its own thing, not a copy of Greece’s

Meze here runs to twenty or thirty small plates and is a whole evening rather than a starter. Halloumi is Cypriot, protected by name, and best grilled until it squeaks. Commandaria, made in a handful of hill villages, has a fair claim to being the oldest named wine still in production, and the local firewater, zivania, will be offered to you whether or not you asked.

Two communities, one island, and no pretending otherwise

The division is the first thing you notice in Nicosia and the thing most holiday brochures leave out. Walking across the line at Ledra Street, hearing the call to prayer from the far side of a square with a Gothic cathedral in it, is the most interesting hour on the island, and it is entirely ordinary and legal to do. We would not send you to Cyprus and leave that out.

Cyprus at a glance

Capital

Nicosia, known locally as Lefkosia and in the north as Lefkoşa. It sits inland in the middle of the island and is divided by the buffer zone, which makes it the only divided capital city in Europe. It is the seat of government, the university town and the business centre, and it is almost entirely missed by package visitors.

Money

The euro, in the Republic, with cards taken everywhere a visitor is likely to go. Across the line in the north the currency is the Turkish lira, although euros are widely accepted at a poor rate; take a modest amount of cash for a day trip and do not expect your card to work as it does in the south.

Language

Greek in the Republic and Turkish in the north, both written in their own alphabets, with English spoken so widely that you will rarely need either. Place names are the one real trap: many have a Greek and a Turkish form and an older English spelling as well, so Paphos is also Pafos, Nicosia is Lefkosia, and Limassol is Lemesos.

Borders

A full member of the European Union and of the eurozone, but not in the Schengen area. That matters more than it sounds: days spent in Cyprus do not count against a Schengen allowance and days spent in Schengen do not count against Cyprus. Cyprus is also outside the EU’s Entry/Exit System, so there is no biometric registration on arrival.

Getting about

Driving is on the left, a legacy of British rule, and a hire car is close to essential — the motorways are good and empty, the distances are short, and the public buses are thin outside the towns. Taxis are plentiful in the resorts. There is no passenger railway anywhere on the island.

Shape of it

About nine thousand square kilometres, which makes it the third largest island in the Mediterranean after Sicily and Sardinia, with roughly a million people living in the Republic. The Troodos massif fills the centre and west and tops out at Mount Olympus, just under two thousand metres, with the long Kyrenia range running along the northern coast.

The regions

Six of them, and the island is small enough that the furthest two are a little over two hours apart — but they make genuinely different holidays.

The west: Paphos and the Akamas

The western end is the holiday Cyprus most British and Irish visitors picture: a working harbour with a Crusader fort at the end of it, world-class Roman mosaics a two-minute walk away, and a run of beaches north towards Coral Bay. Beyond them the Akamas peninsula is the last stretch of undeveloped coast on the island, a roadless-in-places headland of gorge, scrub and turtle beaches that needs either a proper four-wheel drive or a boat.

The south coast: Limassol, Kourion and the wine villages

Limassol is the island’s commercial engine, with the big container port, the marina, a genuinely good restored old town and a seafront park that runs for miles. West of it the Roman city of Kourion sits on a cliff above the sea with a theatre still in use for summer performances, and behind it the Commandaria villages climb towards the mountains through terraced vineyards that have been worked for a very long time.

The south-east: Larnaca, Ayia Napa and Protaras

The flat corner of the island, and the one with the best sand. Larnaca is the main airport and a pleasant, slightly scruffy seaside town with a salt lake behind it that fills with flamingos in winter. Half an hour further east the resort coast begins: Ayia Napa for the famous beaches and the late nights, Protaras and Pernera for families, and the Cape Greco headland of sea caves and limestone arches between them.

The Troodos mountains

The forested centre of the island, an hour up from any coast, and the reason Cyprus is more than a beach. Cool air, pine and cedar, stone villages built along contour lines, monasteries with gold-ground icons, and ten small painted Byzantine churches on the UNESCO list whose frescoes are the finest thing on the island. In a cold winter there is enough snow on Mount Olympus for a handful of short ski runs, which is a genuine curiosity rather than a serious ski holiday.

Nicosia and the centre

Inland, hot in summer and almost entirely missed by holidaymakers, the capital rewards a day or an overnight more than most people expect. Inside the star-shaped Venetian walls there is a dense old city of lanes, a restored craft quarter, two excellent museums and the only pedestrian crossing point through the buffer zone in the middle of a European capital. Outside the walls it is a modern, prosperous and rather ordinary city.

The north, and what we do and do not cover

The northern third of the island — Kyrenia, Famagusta, the Karpas peninsula and the northern half of Nicosia — is administered by Turkish Cypriots and recognised only by Turkey. Visiting for the day from the south is ordinary, legal and done by thousands of people a week: you show your passport at a designated crossing point and walk or drive through. What you must not do is enter the Republic through the north, which the Republic treats as illegal entry. The Guild has no guide to the north and lists no accommodation there, because we have no inventory we can stand behind and no partner booking we could honestly sell you. We would rather say that than sell you a room we have not checked.

Things to do in Cyprus

Stand on the mosaic floors at Kato Paphos

The archaeological park at the edge of Paphos harbour holds the floors of several grand Roman houses, and the mosaics in them — Dionysus, Theseus, Orpheus — are among the best preserved anywhere in the Mediterranean. They are laid out more or less where they were found, under light modern roofs, so you walk the rooms of the houses rather than looking at panels on a wall. Go early: there is very little shade and the site is bigger than it looks from the gate.

Sit in the theatre at Kourion

The Greco-Roman theatre is cut into a cliff west of Limassol with nothing but sea behind the stage, and it is still used for performances on summer evenings. The rest of the city — a bath house, a basilica, and the house of Eustolios with its own mosaics — spreads along the headland. The wind up there is constant and welcome, and the view down the coast is the best on the south shore.

Find the painted churches in the Troodos

Ten small barn-roofed churches scattered through the mountains hold Byzantine wall paintings of extraordinary quality, kept dark and cool for eight hundred years. Asinou and Agios Nikolaos tis Stegis are the usual first two. Several are kept locked with the key held by someone in the village, which is part of the experience; opening hours are informal, so build slack into the day and do not plan to see more than two or three.

Take the Akamas coast to Lara Bay

North of the Baths of Aphrodite the tarmac gives out and a rough track runs along the top of the peninsula above a wild, empty coast to Lara Bay, where loggerhead and green turtles nest through the summer under a conservation programme. It needs a real four-wheel drive — ordinary hire cars are not insured on that track and people get stuck every week — or a boat trip from Latchi, which is the easier way and nearly as good.

Walk across the line at Ledra Street

In the middle of Nicosia’s old city a pedestrian street runs up to a checkpoint, and you simply show your passport and walk through into the other half of the same city. The change of alphabet, currency and call to prayer happens over about forty metres. Take your passport, keep the goods limits in mind on the way back, and give it a whole afternoon rather than twenty minutes.

Dive the Zenobia off Larnaca

A roll-on roll-off ferry that capsized on her maiden voyage in 1980 lies on her side in clear water just outside Larnaca harbour, still loaded with lorries. She is consistently rated among the best wreck dives in the world, shallow enough for a qualified recreational diver at the upper levels and deep enough to occupy technical divers for years. Every dive shop in Larnaca runs trips; conditions decide the day.

When to go, and what it costs

Cyprus has the longest and driest summer in the Mediterranean and a mild, green, surprisingly wet winter, and the two halves of the year feel like different countries. From June to September the coast is hot and reliably rainless and the inland basin around Nicosia becomes genuinely punishing in the middle of the day; July and August are also when prices peak and the resorts fill. The Guild would choose April to early June, when the island is green, the wildflowers are out, the archaeology can be walked at midday and the sea has just become swimmable, or September to early November, when the sea is at its warmest of the whole year and the crowds have gone home. Winter on the coast is mild and cheap, with short bursts of heavy rain, while snow lies on the Troodos tops for a few weeks most years. Orthodox Easter is the one date worth checking before you book: it moves from year to year, it is the biggest festival on the island, and villages that are quiet all spring are suddenly full.

Fishing here

Cyprus fishing is mostly saltwater and mostly from small boats: skippers run half-day trips out of Latchi, Paphos, Limassol, Larnaca and Ayia Napa for amberjack, bream, grouper and the occasional tuna, and shore fishing from the rocks and breakwaters is a normal evening pastime. There are no rivers that run all year, but the mountain dams and reservoirs are stocked, and trout fishing in the Troodos is managed by the Department of Fisheries and Marine Research, which issues the licences — a permit is required and is the sort of thing best arranged locally rather than puzzled out from abroad. Travelling in your later years

The heat is the one real obstacle

Cyprus in July and August is hot in a way that catches people out, and Nicosia inland is the worst of it. Nothing about the island is physically demanding in spring or autumn, and almost everything is harder in high summer. If you have any choice of months, come between April and early June or between September and early November — that is our advice at any age and emphatically past seventy.

Book on the flat line

Every coastal town here has one long level walk, and staying on it changes the whole holiday. Larnaca has the Finikoudes palm promenade, Limassol has the Molos seafront park running for kilometres beside the sea, Paphos has the harbour front at Kato Paphos, and Protaras has the coastal path between the bays. Staying on that line rather than up the hill behind it is worth more than any hotel star rating.

The ruins are rough underfoot, and shadeless

Kourion, the Paphos archaeological park and the Tombs of the Kings are all loose stone, worn marble and uneven ground, with very little shade and few handrails. They are entirely doable at a slow pace early in the day with a hat and water, and much less so at two in the afternoon in August. Several sites have a long walk from the car park to the ticket office before you have seen anything.

Driving is easy, on the familiar side

For British and Irish visitors the driving is on the left with familiar signs, the motorways are modern and lightly used, and the distances are genuinely small. For American and European visitors it is the opposite — the left-hand driving is the thing to think hardest about, and an automatic is worth booking well in advance. Either way a car turns the mountains and the quiet beaches from an expedition into an afternoon.

Getting in: Cyprus is in the EU but outside Schengen

The Republic of Cyprus is a full member of the European Union and of the eurozone, but it is not in the Schengen area, and that has two useful consequences. Days spent in Cyprus do not count against the ninety days in any hundred and eighty that Schengen allows, and days spent in Schengen countries do not count against your Cyprus allowance — the two are tracked separately. Cyprus is also outside the EU’s Entry/Exit System, so there is no biometric registration to complete when you arrive. Citizens of the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, Australia and the European Union do not need a visa for a holiday, with ninety days in any hundred and eighty the usual allowance. British government advice is that your passport should have been issued within the last ten years, should be valid for at least three months beyond the day you leave, and should have a blank page for the stamp; United States government advice recommends six months of validity and two blank pages. Confirm your own nationality’s terms with your own foreign ministry rather than with any website, including this one.

The division of the island adds one rule that genuinely matters, and getting it wrong can cost you your holiday. Enter the Republic only through its own legal points of entry — the international airports at Larnaca and Paphos, and the seaports in the south. Arriving on the island through the north, for example at Ercan airport or a northern port, and then crossing south is treated by the Republic as illegal entry, and can mean a fine, refusal of entry or exit, or being stopped from crossing back. Going the other way is entirely ordinary: crossing to the north for a day or two at a designated crossing point is legal, routine and done constantly. Take your passport, because you will show it at both sides. Ledra Street in the middle of the old city of Nicosia is a pedestrian crossing; Agios Dometios on the edge of the capital is the usual one for cars, and there are several others across the island. Two practical points people trip over: time you spend in the north still counts towards the Republic’s ninety days, and there are limits on what you may bring back south, with tobacco the one that catches most people. If you are driving a hire car, check your rental agreement before you set off — many southern companies either forbid taking the car north or require separate insurance bought at the crossing.

Cyprus — questions

Is Cyprus in the Schengen area?

No. Cyprus is a full member of the European Union and uses the euro, but it is not in the Schengen area. For a traveller that is good news on two counts: the days you spend in Cyprus do not come out of your ninety-day Schengen allowance, and days you have already spent in Schengen countries do not come out of your Cyprus allowance. The two are counted entirely separately. Cyprus is also outside the EU's Entry/Exit System, so there is no biometric registration when you land. Accession to Schengen has been agreed in principle and is working its way through the process, so this is worth re-checking close to the time you travel.

Which airport should I fly into?

Larnaca is the island's main international airport and is the right choice for Larnaca itself, Nicosia, Ayia Napa and Protaras, and it is a reasonable hour from Limassol. Paphos is the second airport and is much the better option if you are staying anywhere in the west, including Coral Bay, Polis and Latchi. Both are in the Republic and both are legal points of entry. Ercan, in the north, is not a legal point of entry for the Republic, and arriving through it and crossing south can mean a fine or a refusal.

Do I really need a hire car?

For most visitors, yes. The motorways are modern and almost empty, nothing is more than about two hours from anything else, and a car is what turns the Troodos villages, Kourion, the Akamas and the quieter beaches from an expedition into an easy afternoon. You can manage without one if you are staying put in Ayia Napa, Protaras or central Paphos and using taxis for the occasional trip out. Remember that driving is on the left, and that automatics are limited in number and worth booking early.

Can I visit the north, and how does it work?

Yes, and it is entirely ordinary. You cross at one of the designated crossing points with your passport, show it on both sides, and carry on. Ledra Street in the old city of Nicosia is the pedestrian crossing and the most interesting one to use; Agios Dometios is the usual crossing for cars in the capital, and there are others across the island. Three things to know: entering the Republic through the north rather than the other way round is treated as illegal entry; the time you spend in the north counts towards your ninety days in the Republic; and there are limits on goods brought back south. If you are driving a hire car, check first whether your rental agreement allows it and whether you need insurance bought at the crossing.

When is the sea warm enough to swim?

Longer than most of the Mediterranean. The water is usually comfortable from around late May and stays that way deep into the autumn, and it is at its warmest not in high summer but at the end of it, from about late August through October, when the sea has had the whole summer to heat up. Plenty of people swim into November on the south coast. In the depths of winter it is cold but not impossible, and you will see locals in the water in January.
