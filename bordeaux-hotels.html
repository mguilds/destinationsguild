Where to Stay in Bordeaux — Best Hotels & Areas

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated August 2026

Bordeaux — the graceful wine capital on the Garonne, its honey-stone eighteenth-century centre a UNESCO World Heritage site — is one of France's most walkable cities: flat, compact and stitched together by a modern tram. Where you stay is mostly a question of character — the grand Golden Triangle, the atmospheric old town by the river, or the quieter Chartrons wine quarter. Below are the city's most enduring hotels arranged by style, each with an honest note on what it is like, how central it is and whether it has a lift, and a Book Me link straight to Trip.com.

The areas, and how central each one is

Bordeaux is a joy to walk — the historic core is flat and compact, and a clean modern tramway (lines A to D) fills in the longer hops, with the riverside quays level and pleasant underfoot. The Triangle d'Or and the streets around the Grand Théâtre are the smartest, most central base; Saint-Pierre by the river is the atmospheric old town, lovely but cobbled in places; and Chartrons, the old wine-merchants' quarter, is quieter and charming for a second visit. We say plainly under each hotel how central it is and whether a lift is confirmed.

The Golden Triangle (Triangle d'Or)

The elegant eighteenth-century heart — the Grand Théâtre, Place de la Comédie, the smart shopping of the Cours de l'Intendance. Flat, central and walkable, with most of the city's best hotels: our first suggestion for older travellers.

Saint-Pierre & the riverfront

The medieval old town between the Golden Triangle and the Garonne — narrow lanes, the Place de la Bourse and its famous miroir d'eau, and the long river quays. Wonderfully atmospheric and central, though some lanes are cobbled.

Chartrons

The old wine-merchants' quarter north of the centre, along the river — antique shops, wine bars and a Sunday market, quieter and more residential. A charming base for a return visit, a short walk or one tram stop from the action.

Saint-Jean station & the Bastide

Around the Saint-Jean station — where the TGV from Paris arrives in about two hours — and the regenerated Bastide across the river. Handy for trains and often better value, a tram ride from the historic centre.

Bordeaux — common questions

Which area of Bordeaux should an older traveller stay in?

The Golden Triangle and the streets around the Grand Théâtre are our first suggestion: flat, central, walkable, and where most of the good hotels are, with the tram and the best shopping and dining on the doorstep. Saint-Pierre, the old town by the river, is the most atmospheric but has cobbled lanes, and Chartrons, the wine quarter to the north, is quieter and lovely for a second visit — a short walk or one tram stop from the centre.

Is Bordeaux a walkable city?

Very — the historic centre is flat and compact, and you can cross it on foot in well under an hour, with the long riverside quays level and pleasant. A clean, modern tramway (lines A to D) fills in the longer hops and reaches the station and the outer districts. The one thing to mind is the cobbles in the old-town lanes around Saint-Pierre and the river, which can be uneven underfoot.

How do I get to Bordeaux, and in from the airport?

The fastest arrival is by train: the TGV from Paris reaches Bordeaux Saint-Jean station in about two hours, and from the station tram line C or a short taxi brings you into the centre. From Bordeaux–Mérignac airport, the “30'Direct” shuttle bus runs to the centre and the station in roughly thirty to forty-five minutes, and a taxi takes about half an hour depending on traffic.

What will I pay in Bordeaux, and are cards accepted?

France uses the euro, and cards — including contactless and phone payments — are taken almost everywhere, so you need little cash beyond a few euros for a market, a wine bar or a tip. A small nightly taxe de séjour (tourist tax) is usually added at check-out rather than shown in the booked rate. When a terminal offers to charge you in dollars, decline and pay in euros to avoid a poor built-in exchange rate.

Is Bordeaux safe, and is it a good base for the wine country?

Bordeaux is generally a safe, easy-going city to walk in, with the usual sensible caution for petty theft around Saint-Jean station and on busy shopping streets — keep valuables in a zipped inside pocket, and watch your footing on the riverside cobbles. It is also an excellent base for the vineyards: Saint-Émilion is about forty minutes by train, and many wine châteaux of the Médoc and the Right Bank run day tours from the city, so you need not drive.
