Poland

Poland Travel Guide

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Poland is a country of about thirty-seven million people on the great flat plain that runs across the middle of Europe — the plain its name comes from, and the one every army between Berlin and Moscow has marched over. That history is the first thing to understand about travelling here, because it explains both what you see and what you do not: cities rebuilt stone by stone from photographs, a border that has moved five times in a century, and a people who are notably warm once you are through the front door. The second thing is more cheerful. The trains are fast, the food is better than its reputation, and your money goes further here than almost anywhere else in the European Union.

Why visit Poland

A big, serious, inexpensive country that most visitors arrive at knowing almost nothing about, and leave meaning to return to.

The value is real

A good dinner, a fast train across the country, a museum ticket, a taxi — all of it costs noticeably less than in western Europe, and the quality has caught up. This is the last large EU country where a careful traveller still feels well off.

The squares are extraordinary

Krakow’s is the largest medieval market square in Europe and came through the war intact. Wroclaw’s and Poznan’s are nearly as fine. You can spend a week doing very little else and not feel short-changed.

The history is told honestly

Poland does not tidy away its twentieth century. The Warsaw Rising Museum, the European Solidarity Centre in Gdansk and the memorial at Auschwitz-Birkenau are among the most carefully made museums anywhere, and none of them flatters anybody.

The food deserves better than its name

Dumplings and sour soups are only the start of it. There is serious cooking in Warsaw and Krakow now, a good bread and cake tradition, and in the milk bars a plain, cheap, entirely unselfconscious lunch that has outlived the system that invented it.

Poland at a glance

Capital

Warsaw, in the middle of the country on the Vistula, and much the biggest city — though Krakow is the one most visitors see first.

Money

The Polish zloty (PLN, written zł). Not the euro, and no date set for joining it. Cards and phones are taken nearly everywhere.

Language

Polish. English is widely and well spoken in the cities by younger people; much less so in small towns and among the old.

Borders

In the European Union since 2004 and in the Schengen area since 2007. Seven land neighbours, from Germany round to Lithuania.

Getting about

Fast, cheap, comfortable express trains between every city in this guide. Buses fill the gaps. You do not need a car.

Shape of it

Compact and flat, with mountains only along the southern edge. Nothing in this guide is more than about three hours from anything else.

The regions

Five regions hold everything most visitors come for, and the trains between them are the easy part.

Lesser Poland & Krakow

The south, and the part of the country most people see. Krakow, the old royal capital, with the Wawel hill and an undamaged medieval centre; the salt mine at Wieliczka and the memorial at Oswiecim within an easy day; and the Tatra mountains at the border.

Things to do in Poland

Krakow’s market square

The Rynek Główny is the largest medieval square in Europe, with the Cloth Hall down the middle and a trumpeter sounding the hour from the church tower. Sit in it for an evening before you do anything else.

Auschwitz-Birkenau

The memorial and museum at Oswiecim, an hour and a half from Krakow. Entry is free but a pass must be reserved in advance on the museum’s own site, and in summer it goes weeks ahead. A full day, and not a sight — a burial ground.

The Wieliczka salt mine

Seven hundred years of digging, a hundred metres down, with chapels and figures carved out of the salt itself. It is a UNESCO site, it is a lift and a long easy walk rather than a scramble, and it is the best wet-weather day in southern Poland.

The Warsaw Rising Museum

The 1944 uprising told street by street in a former power station. It is loud, crowded and extremely well made, and it explains why the capital you are standing in is almost entirely new.

Gdansk’s Long Market

A tall, narrow, gabled merchant street running down to the river, with the great crane at the end of it, and the European Solidarity Centre a short walk away in the shipyard where the strikes began.

The Tatras from Zakopane

Poland’s only real mountains, shared with Slovakia. A cable car up Kasprowy Wierch, a funicular up Gubałówka and a lake walk to Morskie Oko give you the range without a climb.

Best time to visit & getting there

Poland has a continental climate, which in plain terms means proper summers and proper winters with not much hedging in between. Late spring, from the middle of May to the end of June, is our first choice: long days, blossom and then green, warm afternoons without the August heat, and the cities not yet full. Early September is the other one, and arguably better still — the weather holds, the water in the Baltic is at its warmest of the year, and the summer crowds have gone home.

July and August are hot, busy and cheerful. This is when Poles themselves take their holidays, so the Baltic coast and the Masurian lakes are packed and dear while the cities, oddly, can be quieter than you expect. Krakow is the exception and is very full indeed. Winter is cold, often grey and genuinely inexpensive, with Christmas markets in every main square from late November into early January; in the Tatras it is a real ski season, and Zakopane is busy at New Year. Our month-by-month guide to the best time to visit Poland sets it out properly.

Getting there is easy and getting cheaper. Warsaw has the long-haul airport, with nonstops from several North American cities and connections across Europe and the Gulf; Krakow, Gdansk, Wroclaw, Poznan and Katowice all take European flights directly and are well served by the low-cost airlines. Most North American travellers will connect through Warsaw, Frankfurt, Amsterdam, Munich or Istanbul. Once you are in the country the trains take over: book a few days ahead on the national railway’s own site and Warsaw to Krakow, Gdansk or Poznan costs less than a taxi to the airport at home.

Poland uses the Polish zloty (PLN, written zł), not the euro. Poland is a member of the European Union but has kept its own currency and has set no date for changing it, so euro notes are not legal tender and the few places that take them give a poor rate. Pay in zloty. Cards and phones are accepted almost everywhere, including on trams, buses and at ticket machines, and you need very little cash — though a small amount is handy for market stalls, church donation boxes and the odd rural bus. Prices in shops include tax. Tipping is modest and not chased: rounding up, or ten per cent for good table service, is plenty. Decline any card machine that offers to charge you in dollars; pay in zloty and let your own bank convert. Beware the standalone currency-exchange booths at the airports, which are much worse than the ones in town or simply drawing cash from a bank machine.

Fishing here

Poland is a quietly good fishing country and hardly anybody outside it knows. The Masurian lake district in the north-east is the heart of it — two thousand lakes linked by canals, with pike, zander, perch and bream, fished from a boat or from the reeds, and a long tradition of taking a sailing yacht out for a week and fishing off the back of it. The Baltic coast gives you sea trout and cod from the beach or a charter out of Gdynia or Hel, and the mountain rivers of the south hold grayling and brown trout. The paperwork is simple but it is real: you need a state angling card for most freshwater fishing, plus a permit for the particular water, both sold locally or online, and sea angling from the shore needs no licence. We have no Polish fishing book on the shelf yet; the whole collection is in The Guild Fishing Library.

Travelling in your later years

An easy country to travel slowly and cheaply, with two or three things worth knowing before you book.

The trains are the answer

Express trains between the cities are modern, level-boarding, air-conditioned and have proper lavatories and a buffet. Reserved seats come with the ticket. It is the most comfortable way to cross Poland and much the cheapest.

The old towns are cobbled

Every one of them, and the stones are big and uneven. Krakow’s square, Gdansk’s Long Market and Wroclaw’s Rynek are flat but rough underfoot. Sensible shoes matter more here than hills do.

Lifts are not universal

A great many hotels in the old centres are in converted townhouses of three or four floors, and some have no lift at all. Ask before you book rather than after — the newer hotels just outside the ring are often easier and cheaper both.

Care is good and cheap

Pharmacies are everywhere, well stocked and used to advising; many medicines sold only on prescription elsewhere are available over the counter. Private clinics are inexpensive and quick. Bring prescriptions in their original packaging with a copy of the prescription.

Winter means winter

January and February regularly sit below freezing for weeks and the pavements ice up. It is a lovely time to be in a warm cafe in Krakow and a hard time to be walking to one. If the cold is a worry, come between May and September.

Concessions are generous

Museums, galleries and the state railway all give reduced fares to older visitors, and the discount usually applies to foreign passports as well as Polish ones. It is rarely advertised in English, so ask at the window.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS — the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years — is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today — nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Poland is a straightforward case. It has been in the European Union since 2004 and in the Schengen area since 2007, so everything above applies here in full, and flying in from Berlin, Vienna or Amsterdam involves no passport control at all. What Poland has not joined is the euro, so the currency is the zloty. Note too that Poland’s eastern border — with Belarus and with the Russian enclave at Kaliningrad — is an external Schengen frontier and is not a casual crossing; the crossings into Ukraine are open but busy. None of this affects an ordinary trip to the cities in this guide.

Poland travel FAQ

When is the best time to visit Poland?

Late May to mid June, and the first three weeks of September, are the two windows we would pick. The days are long, the weather is usually kind, the city squares have their tables out and the queues at Wawel and Wieliczka are a fraction of what they are in August. July and August are warm and lively but they are also when Krakow is at its fullest and the Baltic coast is booked out by Polish families. Winter is cold, often grey, and genuinely cheap, with Christmas markets in every main square from late November; January and February in the mountains are a proper ski season.

Is Poland cheap for visitors?

It is one of the better-value countries in the European Union, though it is no longer the bargain it was ten years ago and Krakow in high summer is dearer than people expect. Where the value shows is in the ordinary things: a good restaurant meal, a long train journey, a museum ticket, a taxi across town. A milk bar lunch, the plain subsidised canteens that survive from the communist period, still costs a few dollars and is one of the honest pleasures of travelling here. Hotels in Warsaw and Krakow are priced much like the rest of central Europe; outside those two cities they are noticeably less.

Does Poland use the euro?

No. Poland is in the European Union and in the Schengen area, but it has kept its own currency, the zloty, and has set no date for joining the euro. Some hotels and tour desks will quote you a price in euros and a few will take euro notes, usually at a poor rate. Pay in zloty. Cards are accepted almost everywhere, including on buses and trams, and contactless is the norm; you need very little cash. Decline any card machine that offers to charge you in dollars and let your own bank do the conversion.

Do I need a car in Poland?

For the five cities in this guide, no, and a car is a liability in all of them. The express trains are the thing Poland does best: Warsaw to Krakow in about two and a half hours, Warsaw to Gdansk in under three, Warsaw to Poznan in about two and a half, all on comfortable trains with reserved seats and a buffet. Book ahead and the fares are low. Where a car does earn its keep is the Masurian lakes, the Bieszczady mountains in the far south-east and the small Baltic villages, where buses are thin on the ground.

Can you visit Auschwitz-Birkenau from Krakow?

Yes. The Auschwitz-Birkenau Memorial and Museum is at Oswiecim, about an hour and a half west of Krakow by bus or train, and it is the reason a great many people come to this part of Poland. Entry to the grounds is free, but a personal entry pass must be reserved in advance through the museum’s own website and in practice it books out weeks ahead in summer. During the busiest hours a guided tour is required rather than optional. Allow a full day, dress for standing outdoors in whatever the weather is doing, and understand that it is a memorial and a burial ground before it is a sight. Children under fourteen are not recommended by the museum itself.

Are the old towns really original?

Some are and some are not, and the difference is worth knowing before you go. Krakow came through the Second World War structurally intact, so its old town and the Wawel hill are genuinely old. Warsaw was deliberately destroyed and its old town is a post-war reconstruction, rebuilt from paintings, photographs and measured drawings with such care that UNESCO listed the reconstruction itself. Gdansk and Wroclaw are the same story. Knowing this does not spoil them. It is the point: these are the squares of a country that refused to let its cities be erased, and read that way they are more moving than any undisturbed old town.

Is English widely spoken?

In the cities, among anyone under about forty, and in hotels, restaurants and museums, yes, and often very well. Away from the cities and among older people it thins out quickly, and a bus timetable or a small-town pharmacy may need a translation app. Polish is a hard language to pick at, but czesc, dziekuje and przepraszam, said badly, go a long way. Station and airport signage is bilingual, and the ticket machines have an English setting.

How many days do you need?

Four days is enough for Krakow and a day at Auschwitz-Birkenau or Wieliczka. Seven lets you add Warsaw, with the train between them costing you only a morning. Ten gives you a proper trip: Krakow, Warsaw and Gdansk, with the Baltic as a change of air at the end, or Wroclaw and Poznan if you would rather stay inland. Poland is compact by central European standards and the trains are fast, so the usual mistake here is not over-reaching but under-staying, treating Krakow as a two-night stop on a longer tour.
