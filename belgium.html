Belgium

Belgium Travel Guide

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Belgium is small, dense and very easy to travel, and most people see it from a train window on the way somewhere else. That is their loss. Inside an area about the size of Maryland you have four cities worth two days each, some of the best painting in Europe hanging in the churches that paid for it, a brewing tradition on the UNESCO heritage list, the Western Front, and a forested southern upland that hardly any visitor reaches. This guide covers Brussels, Bruges, Ghent and Antwerp city by city, with an honest account of when to come and what it costs.

Why visit Belgium

A small, dense, often underrated country that most people pass through rather than stop in.

Four cities within an hour of each other

Brussels, Ghent, Antwerp and Bruges are each half an hour to an hour apart by frequent, cheap trains. You can change city as easily as changing neighbourhood, which makes Belgium unusually forgiving to plan.

The art is extraordinary for the size

The Van Eyck altarpiece in Ghent, Rubens in Antwerp, Bruegel and Magritte in Brussels, and the Flemish Primitives in Bruges. For a country this small the concentration of first-rank painting is hard to match anywhere.

It eats and drinks very well

Chips from a friture, mussels in season, chocolate that deserves its name, and a brewing tradition on the UNESCO heritage list — Trappist ales from the monasteries and sour lambics from the valleys west of Brussels.

Two countries in one

Dutch-speaking Flanders and French-speaking Wallonia are genuinely different in language, landscape and temperament, with forested hills and river valleys in the south that most visitors never see at all.

Belgium at a glance

Capital

Brussels, officially bilingual in French and Dutch, and the seat of the European Commission and Council and of NATO.

Money

The euro. Cards and phones are taken almost everywhere, contactless is normal on trams and at machines, and you need very little cash. Decline any card machine offering to charge you in dollars.

Language

Dutch in Flanders to the north, French in Wallonia to the south, a small German-speaking community in the east, and both French and Dutch official in Brussels. English is widely spoken throughout.

Borders

A founding member of the European Union and of the Schengen area. The land borders with the Netherlands, Germany, Luxembourg and France are internal crossings; the Eurostar to London is checked before boarding.

Getting about

Dense, frequent and inexpensive railways run by the national operator, with trams, buses and metro in the cities. Nothing in this guide is more than about two and a half hours from Brussels.

Shape of it

Small — about 30,000 square kilometres — and flat in the north, rising to the forested hills of the Ardennes in the south-east. Atlantic weather: mild, changeable and wet in every month.

The regions

Five parts of a very small country, and what each is for.

Brussels and the centre

The capital and its ring: the Grand-Place and the Lower Town, the museums on the hill, the European institutions, and the nineteenth-century districts to the south where the Art Nouveau and the good restaurants are. Leuven, a half-hour away, adds one of the oldest universities in Europe and the handsomest town hall in the country.

Flanders — Bruges, Ghent and Antwerp

The Dutch-speaking north and the part most visitors come for. Medieval cloth towns made rich by wool and trade, flat and thoroughly cycled, with the three cities in this guide within half an hour of each other and the Flemish Primitives hanging in the churches that commissioned them.

The Westhoek and Flanders Fields

The far west around Ypres, where the Western Front stood for four years. The town was rebuilt from photographs after 1918; the Menin Gate carries the names of tens of thousands of missing, and the Last Post has been sounded beneath it every evening since 1928. The cemeteries and the In Flanders Fields Museum are the reason to come.

Wallonia and the Meuse

The French-speaking south: Namur under its citadel, Liege with its vast stairway and its Sunday market, and Dinant wedged between a cliff and the river. Quieter, cheaper and far less visited than Flanders, with abbeys, caves and castles strung along the river valleys.

The Ardennes

Forested hills, deep valleys and small stone towns in the south-east, running on into Luxembourg and Germany. Walking, kayaking on the Lesse and the Ourthe, wild boar and game on the menus, and Bastogne, where the Battle of the Bulge is remembered. Autumn is its season, and this is the one part of Belgium where a car is worth having.

Things to do in Belgium

The Grand-Place, early

Brussels’ enclosed square of gilded guild houses is at its best before the crowds and again when it is lit at night. Go at both ends of the day and skip the restaurant touts in the lanes behind it.

The Ghent Altarpiece

The Van Eyck brothers’ Adoration of the Mystic Lamb, finished in 1432 and usually credited with beginning the Northern Renaissance, in Sint-Baafs Cathedral in Ghent. Book a timed ticket ahead and allow an hour.

Bruges before ten and after five

The canals, the Markt and the Belfry belong to day-trippers in the middle of the day and to almost nobody at either end of it. This is the single best argument for sleeping in Bruges rather than visiting it.

A Trappist beer where it is brewed

Belgian beer culture is on the UNESCO list. Westmalle, Chimay, Orval and Rochefort are monastery breweries; the lambics and gueuzes of the Pajottenland west of Brussels are sour, wild-fermented and quite unlike anything else.

The Last Post at the Menin Gate

Sounded by buglers of the local fire brigade at Ypres every evening at eight, without a break since 1928 apart from the years of German occupation. It is short, public, free and unlike any other war memorial in Europe.

Rubens in Antwerp

His altarpieces are in the Cathedral of Our Lady, his house and studio are a few streets away, and the Museum of Fine Arts holds more. Antwerp was his city and it is still arranged around him.

Best time to visit & getting there

June and September are the two months we would pick: long northern light — it stays bright until about ten in the evening in midsummer — warm rather than hot days, and the terraces out. July and August are the busiest weeks, which matters most in Bruges and on the coast and matters least in Brussels, whose own residents leave in August. From late November the Christmas markets fill all four cities. November and early January are the hardest months, and also the cheapest. Whatever the season, this is an Atlantic climate and rain is spread through the whole year, so pack for showers in any month and keep an indoor option in each day. The full month-by-month account is in our best time to visit Belgium guide.

Getting there is easy. Brussels Airport is about twenty minutes from the centre by train; Charleroi, badged Brussels South by the low-cost airlines, is about an hour away by coach. By rail Brussels is roughly two hours from Paris and from Amsterdam, under two from London by Eurostar and under two from Cologne. Antwerp sits on the Amsterdam line, so you can arrive at one end of the country and leave from the other.

Fishing here

Belgium is mostly a coarse and canal fishing country — carp, bream, pike and zander in the canals, ponds and the slower rivers of the north, fished hard and well by a large domestic club scene. The fly fishing is in the south: the Ardennes rivers, the Ourthe, the Ambleve, the Lesse and the Semois, hold brown trout and grayling in pocket water under the trees, and a number of beats are managed by clubs or let through local permits. The paperwork is real and regional rather than national — Wallonia and Flanders each run their own licence, and many individual waters want a day ticket on top — so arrange it locally before you fish. We have no Belgian fishing book on the shelf yet; the whole collection is in The Guild Fishing Library.

Travelling in your later years

An honest word about the ground underfoot, written for travellers who would rather know in advance.

The cobbles are the real obstacle

Bruges, Ghent and the older parts of Antwerp are cobbled almost throughout, with large, uneven, often slippery stones. Flat shoes with a decent sole matter more here than in most countries, and wheeled luggage is hard work. Taxis cannot always reach a hotel door in a restricted centre; ask when you book.

Brussels has a hill in the middle of it

The Lower Town and the Upper Town are separated by a genuine climb. There are lifts, escalators and a frequent metro that take the sting out of it, but plan the direction of your day so the hill comes early, and consider staying up near the Sablon rather than walking up to the museums each morning.

Flat, short distances everywhere else

Against the cobbles, the distances are forgiving. All four cities in this guide are walkable in twenty to thirty minutes end to end, Flanders is flat, and the trains between them take half an hour to an hour with level or assisted access at the main stations, which can be arranged in advance with the operator.

The weather asks more than the terrain

Damp cold is the thing that shortens a day here, not heat or altitude. From October to March, build the day around indoor stops — and Belgium is unusually well supplied with them, since the museums, cafes, churches and chocolate shops are the point rather than a fallback.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS — the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years — is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today — nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Belgium is about as straightforward as Europe gets. It is a founding member of both the European Union and the Schengen area, it uses the euro, and its land borders with the Netherlands, Germany, Luxembourg and France are internal Schengen crossings that you will cross on a train or a motorway without noticing. Arriving by air from another Schengen country means no passport control at all. The one border that is still a proper frontier is the Channel: the Eurostar to and from London is checked at the station before you board, so allow the time the operator asks for rather than turning up as you would for a domestic train.

Belgium travel FAQ

How many days do you need in Belgium?

Five nights is a good first trip: two in Brussels, two in Bruges or Ghent and one in Antwerp, with trains of half an hour to an hour between them. Three nights will give you the capital and one Flemish city without rushing. Ten lets you add the Ardennes in the south, the Flanders Fields battlefields around Ypres, and a slower pace in each place. The country is small — nothing in this guide is more than about two and a half hours by train from Brussels — so the usual mistake here is not over-reaching but treating the whole of Belgium as a day between Paris and Amsterdam.

What language do they speak in Belgium?

Three official languages, divided by region rather than mixed. Flanders in the north — Bruges, Ghent, Antwerp, Leuven — is Dutch-speaking. Wallonia in the south — Liege, Namur, Dinant, the Ardennes — is French-speaking. Brussels is officially bilingual in both, though what you hear on the street is mostly French. A small German-speaking community sits in the east near the border. English is very widely and willingly spoken throughout, particularly in Flanders. The practical tip for a visitor is to open in English rather than guessing: French in Flanders can land badly for reasons that are about Belgian history rather than about you.

Is Belgium expensive?

It sits in the middle of western Europe: dearer than Portugal or central Europe, a little cheaper than the Netherlands and noticeably cheaper than Paris or London for food and drink. Hotels are the main cost and the pattern is unusual — Brussels is built for business and European institutions, so its rooms are often cheaper at weekends than midweek, while Bruges runs the normal tourist way round and peaks in summer and at weekends. Eating is good value if you avoid the restaurant streets immediately around the main squares. Trains, museums and beer are all reasonable.

Do I need a car?

For the cities, no, and a car is a genuine liability in them. Belgian trains are frequent, cheap and quick, the four cities in this guide are all within an hour of one another, and the historic centres of Bruges, Ghent and Antwerp are restricted, cobbled or both. Where a car does earn its keep is the south: the Ardennes, the Meuse valley around Dinant, and the smaller battlefield sites around Ypres are all much easier with one. The sensible shape for a two-week trip is trains for the cities and a hire car for a few days at the end.

Bruges or Ghent — which should I choose?

Bruges is smaller, prettier and far more visited; you will recognise it before you arrive. Ghent is three times the size, has a university and sixty thousand students, holds the Van Eyck altarpiece in its cathedral, and feels like a working city that happens to be medieval rather than a preserved one. If you want the postcard and quiet evenings after the coaches leave, Bruges. If you want somewhere alive on a Tuesday night, Ghent. They are half an hour apart, so for most trips the honest answer is both.

Is Belgian beer really that good, and how do you approach it?

Yes, and it is taken seriously enough that Belgian beer culture is on the UNESCO intangible heritage list. The things worth seeking out are the Trappist beers, brewed at a handful of monasteries including Westmalle, Chimay, Orval and Rochefort, and the lambics and gueuzes of Brussels and the Pajottenland to its west, which are fermented with wild yeasts and taste sour and cidery in a way that surprises people the first time. Strengths are high — eight or nine per cent is ordinary — so treat a Belgian beer as you would a glass of wine rather than a pint.

What is the food actually like?

Better than its reputation and rather more French than people expect. The staples are mussels with chips in season, steak and chips, waterzooi, stoofvlees or carbonnade cooked in beer, shrimp croquettes on the coast, and chips themselves, which are a serious matter here and eaten from a paper cone with mayonnaise from a friture rather than as a side dish. Waffles are two different things — the light rectangular Brussels one and the dense sweet Liege one — and the chocolate is as good as the shops suggest. Avoid the restaurants whose staff call you in from the pavement, particularly in the lanes around the Grand-Place.

Is it easy to get there and to move on?

Very. Brussels Airport has long-haul and European flights and a train into the city in about twenty minutes, and Charleroi, badged Brussels South by low-cost airlines, is about an hour away by coach. By rail Brussels is about two hours from Paris and from Amsterdam, under two from London by Eurostar, and under two from Cologne, which makes Belgium one of the easiest countries in Europe to slot into a wider trip. Antwerp is also on the Amsterdam line, so you can enter at one end of the country and leave at the other without doubling back.
