Tallinn

Where to Stay in Tallinn

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Tallinn is a walled medieval merchant town with a modern northern city grown around it, and the two halves are only a few minutes’ walk apart. The old lower town is where the guild halls, the Town Hall Square and the city wall are, and it is still a working piece of town rather than a museum; above it on the limestone hill sits Toompea, the castle and cathedral quarter, reached by two steep ramps. Immediately outside the eastern gate the modern centre begins, with the shops, the larger hotels and the port behind it, and north-west of all of it lies Kalamaja, a district of painted wooden houses that has quietly become the best place in the city to eat. The whole of this fits inside about a half-hour walk, which is the single most useful thing to know before you book.

Where to stay in Tallinn, area by area

The parts of Tallinn a visitor actually chooses between, and who each one suits.

Vanalinn: inside the old walls

Most centralWalk to everythingCobbles and crowds

The lower town is the Tallinn on the postcards and, for a first visit, the right answer. It is a complete walled medieval merchant town — Town Hall Square, the guild halls, the Gothic churches, long stretches of wall with their towers still standing — and it is small enough to cross on foot in fifteen minutes. Staying inside the walls means stepping out of your door into it, which is worth a great deal in a northern winter when nobody wants a long walk in the dark. The trades are real, though: the streets are limestone cobble and hard going with a wheeled case, cars are largely kept out so your taxi may stop at a gate, and the central square has late nights and summer stag parties that the quieter lanes behind it do not.

Toompea: the upper town on the hill

Quietest central optionThe two famous viewpointsSteep in and out

Toompea is the limestone outcrop the castle and the cathedral sit on, historically the aristocrats’ and the governors’ half of the city while the merchants lived below. It has the Alexander Nevsky cathedral, the parliament, the Toomkirik and the two viewing platforms that everyone photographs the red roofs from. There is far less accommodation up here than below and it is quieter for it. What you pay is the climb: Pikk jalg and Lühike jalg, the long leg and the short leg, are the two old ramps between the towns, and they are steep, uneven and slippery in the wet. Lovely if you are sure-footed, tiring otherwise.

Kesklinn and the Rotermann Quarter

Modern hotelsNext to the Old Town gateWhere the shops are

The modern city centre begins immediately outside the eastern wall at Viru Gate and runs down towards the harbour. This is where most of the larger business and chain hotels stand, where the department stores and the main shopping streets are, and where you will find the bus and rail links out of town. The Rotermann Quarter, a block of converted industrial buildings between the centre and the port, has turned into a good district of restaurants and small shops in its own right. The attraction is simple: you are three or four minutes’ walk from the Old Town gate, on flat modern pavement, in a building with a lift and a loading bay. It is not picturesque, and it does not pretend to be.

Kalamaja and Telliskivi

Wooden housesBest eating and drinkingTwenty minutes’ walk out

North-west of the Old Town, past the railway station, Kalamaja is a district of painted wooden houses that has become the part of Tallinn people talk about. Telliskivi Creative City, a yard of old railway workshops, holds galleries, studios, a street-food market and a good share of the city’s best restaurants and bars; the Seaplane Harbour maritime museum, with a submarine indoors, is at the bottom of it by the water. There is less conventional hotel stock here and more apartments and small guesthouses. It is a flat fifteen to twenty-five minutes’ walk into the Old Town along the harbour or through the station, which is pleasant in June and a long cold trudge in January.

Kadriorg and Pirita

Parkland and the seaFamiliesNeed the tram or a car

East of the centre the city turns green. Kadriorg is a baroque palace and its park, built for Peter the Great, with the KUMU art museum at the top end and quiet residential streets of wooden villas around it; the tram from the centre takes a few minutes. Further out, Pirita has the long city beach, the marina built for the sailing events of the 1980 Olympics, and the ruin of a fifteenth-century convent. This is where Tallinn families go in summer and where a visitor with children might reasonably want to be. You are committed to the tram, the bus or a car for anything in the Old Town, and in winter the appeal of a beach suburb drops away sharply.

Getting around Tallinn

On foot

The Old Town, the centre and the harbour are all within a half-hour walk of one another, and walking is how most visitors see the city. Two cautions worth taking seriously. The Old Town’s cobbles are rounded limestone and genuinely slippery when wet, and in winter they ice over; flat soles with grip are worth more here than any other piece of kit. And the ramps up to Toompea are steeper than they look in photographs.

Trams, buses and trolleybuses

Tallinn has a compact, frequent network that covers everywhere a visitor is likely to go, including the airport, Kadriorg and Pirita. One thing catches people out: public transport is free for registered Tallinn residents, which is not the same as being free for visitors. You still need a ticket, bought with a card on board or through the Tallinn Card if you are using the museums as well.

The airport

Lennart Meri Tallinn Airport is unusually close to the city — a few kilometres out, with a tram from outside the terminal that reaches the centre in roughly a quarter of an hour. It is small, calm and quick to clear, and it is one of the shortest airport-to-hotel journeys of any European capital. Taxis and ride apps are plentiful and the run is short.

The ferry to Helsinki

Fast ferries cross the Gulf of Finland from Tallinn’s passenger port to Helsinki in about two hours, and they run several times a day for most of the year. A day return is an entirely normal thing for a visitor to do, and the terminal is a fifteen-minute walk or a short tram ride from the Old Town. Both countries are in Schengen, so there is no border check in the ordinary way; sailings can be cancelled in bad weather, which is worth knowing before you build a day around one.

Driving

You do not need a car in Tallinn and it is a mild nuisance to have one: the Old Town is largely closed to traffic, parking in the centre is paid and tight, and everything is walkable or a tram ride away. A car earns its keep the moment you leave — Lahemaa national park, the islands and the road south to Latvia are all far easier with one. Driving is on the right, and dipped headlights are required day and night all year round, which hire companies will usually have set for you.

When to come, and what it costs

Tallinn has a short, bright summer and a long, dark winter, and the two are completely different holidays. From June to August the days are enormous — the light barely goes at midsummer — the terraces fill, the Old Town courtyards open up and the city is at its busiest and dearest. May and September are the Guild’s own choice: mild, light enough, and noticeably quieter. From late November the Christmas market fills Town Hall Square and the city is genuinely lovely in the cold and the dark, which is a real reason to come rather than a consolation. January and February are the cheapest and the hardest: short days, ice underfoot and a cold that is dry rather than raw but is still a long way below freezing for weeks at a time.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Estonia itself is straightforward: in the European Union since 2004, in the Schengen area since the end of 2007 and on the euro since the beginning of 2011. An arrival from another Schengen country has no passport control at all, and an arrival from outside it is a single check at the airport you land in. The land borders with Latvia and the sea crossing from Finland are internal Schengen movements. The eastern border with Russia is an external EU frontier and the arrangements at its crossings have changed more than once in recent years, so if any part of your plan depends on one, check with the Estonian Police and Border Guard Board before you book rather than trusting anything written earlier, including this page.

One point of confusion worth clearing up: Estonian e-Residency is a digital identity for running a business online. It is not residency, it is not a visa and it gives you no right to enter or stay. If you are coming as a visitor, the Schengen rules above are the ones that apply to you.

Tallinn — questions

Old Town or Kalamaja?

Old Town for a first visit, Kalamaja if you have been before or if eating and drinking well matters more to you than stepping out of the door into the Middle Ages. The Old Town puts you inside the thing you came to see and costs you cobbles, a bit of noise around the square and a higher price. Kalamaja gives you the most interesting food in the city, wooden streets that feel like a different town, and a fifteen to twenty-five minute walk each way that is a pleasure in summer and a chore in winter. A useful compromise is the quieter northern end of the Old Town, which is close to both.

Is public transport really free in Tallinn?

Free for registered residents of the city, not for visitors. This is one of the most repeated half-truths about Tallinn and it catches people every week. As a visitor you buy a ticket, tap a contactless card on board, or use the Tallinn Card if you are also going to the museums. The good news is that the network is cheap, frequent and covers the airport, Kadriorg and Pirita, and that most visitors walk nearly everywhere in the centre anyway.

How long do I need in Tallinn?

Two full days covers the Old Town properly without rushing: the lower town and the guild halls on one, Toompea and the walls on the other, with time for a museum. A third day lets you add Kalamaja and the Seaplane Harbour or Kadriorg and KUMU, which is when the city stops being a day-trip stop and starts being a place. Add a fourth if you want Lahemaa national park or a day across the water in Helsinki. Many people arrive off a cruise ship with six hours, which is enough for the lower town and nothing else.

Is the day trip to Helsinki worth it?

It is about two hours each way by fast ferry and it is a reasonable thing to do if you have four or five days and want to see a second capital. It is not a good use of a short trip. Be honest about the arithmetic: a day return leaves you perhaps six hours ashore, much of it walking from the Helsinki terminal into the centre and back, and Tallinn still has more to show you than that day costs. Check the weather before you commit, because winter sailings are cancelled often enough to matter.

Is Tallinn difficult in winter?

It is cold and dark rather than difficult, and the city is thoroughly set up for it. The practical problems are ice on the Old Town cobbles, which is the real hazard and argues for boots with grip, and daylight that is short enough that you should plan outdoor sightseeing for the middle of the day. Everything indoors is heated to the point of being too warm. From late November the Christmas market makes the dark an attraction rather than a problem, and prices outside that few weeks are the lowest of the year.
