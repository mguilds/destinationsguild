San Ignacio, Cayo

Where to Stay in San Ignacio

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

San Ignacio is the capital of inland Belize in everything but name: a hill town where two rivers meet, an hour and a half west of Belmopan and a few miles short of Guatemala, with a Saturday market, a street of tour operators and a Maya site on the ridge above the houses. Nearly everything people come inland for is reached from here — Xunantunich across the hand-cranked ferry, Caracol deep in the Chiquibul, the caves under the limestone, the waterfalls of the Mountain Pine Ridge — and the town itself is the pleasantest place in Belize simply to spend an evening.

Where to stay in San Ignacio, area by area

San Ignacio sits where the Macal and the Mopan rivers meet to become the Belize River, in hill country an hour and a half west of Belmopan and a few miles short of the Guatemalan border. The choice here is between the town, the ridge above it and the river lodges out in the valleys — and they make genuinely different holidays.

The town centre

A compact grid around Burns Avenue and the market, on flat ground by the river. Staying in town means restaurants within a few minutes’ walk, tour operators on the street who will arrange anything, the Saturday market, buses from the terminal and the cheapest rooms in the district. It is a working Belizean town rather than a resort, lively in the evening and noisy in the morning when the buses start. For travellers without a car, and for anyone who wants to be among people rather than in a forest, town is the practical answer.

The ridge above town

The ground rises steeply west of the centre towards Cahal Pech, and a number of hotels sit up there with a view back over the river valley. It is a ten- or fifteen-minute walk down into town and a considerably longer one back up, which in the heat is a real consideration. You get the view, the breeze and the quiet, and you are still close enough to walk to dinner if you are reasonably fit. A taxi up is cheap if you are not.

The river lodges on the Macal

Upstream of town, along the Macal River, a string of jungle lodges sit in their own grounds with the river below them. These are the places people picture when they imagine inland Belize: cabanas, birdsong, howler monkeys at dawn, dinner cooked for you because there is nowhere else to eat. They are a holiday in themselves rather than a base, and they run their own guided trips. Several are reached by a track that wants ground clearance, and some by boat.

The Mopan valley and out towards Benque

West of town the road runs through San Jose Succotz towards Benque Viejo and the Guatemalan border, past the ferry for Xunantunich. Lodges and small hotels out here put you closest to the big western sites and to the crossing, in farm country rather than deep forest, usually at lower prices than the Macal lodges.

Mountain Pine Ridge

South of town the road climbs into the pine ridge, a high plateau of pine forest, waterfalls and granite that looks nothing like the rest of Belize. There are a small number of remote lodges up there. It is beautiful and genuinely isolated; the roads are rough, the journey is long, and you will not be going into town for anything.

Getting around San Ignacio

Getting here

By road from the international airport near Belize City it is about two hours on the George Price Highway, which is surfaced and straightforward the whole way. Buses run frequently along that corridor and are cheap. There is no airstrip at San Ignacio, so unlike the cayes and Placencia this is a place you drive or ride to.

On foot

The town centre is small and walkable, and the market, the restaurants and the bus terminal are all within a few blocks. The one thing to know is the hill: the ground west of the centre is steep, and a hotel described as a short walk from town may be a short walk down and a hard climb back.

Taxis and tours

Taxis are plentiful and cheap in town. Nearly every visitor reaches the sites by organised tour, because the trips to Caracol, the caves and the pine ridge all involve rough roads, permits or guides, and in the case of the cave systems a licensed guide is required. Operators cluster on Burns Avenue and most hotels will book for you.

Hiring a car

Useful for Xunantunich, the Mopan valley and the Belize Zoo on the way back, and for reaching lodges off the highway. Not necessary for the big guided days. Anything up the pine ridge or out to Caracol wants high clearance and, in the wet season, four-wheel drive.

When to come, and what it costs

Inland Belize is at its best in the dry season from about January to April, when the roads are firm, the river levels are low enough for the cave trips to run, and the heat is manageable. Cayo is noticeably cooler than the coast at night, particularly up in the pine ridge, and the humidity is lower than on the cayes.

The green season brings heavy afternoon rain from June to November, and September and October can put the rougher roads and the cave trips out of action for days at a time — operators cancel when the rivers are high, and that is a safety judgement rather than a commercial one. Against that, the forest is at its most alive and the birding and the howler monkeys are at their best. The fuller picture is in Best Time to Visit Belize.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Most visitors from the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, the European Union and much of the Commonwealth may enter Belize as tourists without arranging a visa in advance, and are admitted for a limited stay that can usually be extended at an immigration office for a fee. A passport with sufficient remaining validity, onward or return travel, and an address for the first night are the ordinary requirements.

Beyond that we do not print the numbers, and nor should anyone else. Entry rules, the length of stay granted, and the departure and border fees are exactly the details that change without notice. Check the current position with the Belize Immigration and Nationality Department and with your own government’s travel advice shortly before you fly.

San Ignacio — questions

How many nights do I need in San Ignacio?

Three is the usual answer and it works: one for Xunantunich and the town, one for a cave or the pine ridge, and one for Caracol or a river day. Two forces a choice. Four or five is comfortable if you want Caracol, a cave, the pine ridge and a day doing nothing, and Cayo rewards the extra time more than most places in Belize because the excursions are long.

Town or a jungle lodge?

Town if you want restaurants, company, cheap rooms and the freedom to book a different trip each morning. A lodge if you want the forest itself — birds at dawn, howler monkeys, the river — and are content to eat where you sleep and to take that lodge's own excursions. Many people do two nights in town and two at a lodge, which is a good way to have both.

Is the ATM cave worth it, and is it hard?

Actun Tunichil Muknal is the most remarkable thing many visitors do in Belize: a river cave with Maya ceremonial remains and pottery still in place where they were left. It is also genuinely demanding. You wade and swim through the river to get in, scramble over rock in the dark, and remove your shoes for the final chamber. A licensed guide is compulsory and cameras have not been permitted inside for years, after a visitor damaged a skull. If you are not a confident swimmer or are unsteady on wet rock, choose another cave — there are gentler ones.

How do I get to Xunantunich?

Drive or take a bus west to San Jose Succotz, then cross the Mopan River on the hand-cranked cable ferry, which carries foot passengers and cars and costs nothing. From the far bank it is a mile uphill to the site, walkable or by taxi. It is the easiest of the major sites to reach independently and can be done in a morning.

Can I cross into Guatemala from here?

Yes — the border at Benque Viejo is a short drive west, and the trip on to Flores and Tikal is a well-trodden day or overnight run that every operator in town sells. Both countries charge their own fees at the crossing and the arrangements change, so check the current position rather than relying on a figure in any guide, and take your passport.

Is San Ignacio safe?

It is one of the easier towns in Belize, used to travellers and small enough to read quickly, and ordinary town sense covers the evening. The practical cautions inland are environmental rather than human: rough roads after rain, river levels, sun and heat on the sites, and insects in the forest. As always, read your own government's current advice rather than relying on a guide.
