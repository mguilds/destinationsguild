Kiruna

Where to Stay in Kiruna

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Kiruna is the northernmost town in Sweden of any size, a hundred and forty-five kilometres above the Arctic Circle, and it exists because of the iron mountain it is built beside — which is also why the town is being moved. The ore body runs underneath the old centre, so the centre is being rebuilt a few kilometres east, building by building, with a new town hall and high street already open and the older ones coming down or being carried across. For a visitor none of that is an obstacle: Kiruna is the practical base for the aurora, the midnight sun, the ice hotel at Jukkasjärvi and the mountains at Abisko, and the question is simply which of four very different places to sleep in.

The new town centre

Most convenientShops and restaurantsStep-free

The rebuilt centre east of the old one, around the new town hall, is where the practical business of a Kiruna trip happens: the hotels, the restaurants, the supermarket, the pharmacy and the bus station. Everything is new, flat, warm and designed for a town where the temperature sits below freezing for half the year, which makes it a great deal more comfortable in January than its looks suggest. This is the right base for a first visit, for a trip built around day excursions, and for anyone who would rather not drive on ice. The airport is about ten minutes away.

The old centre and the mine side

CheapestThe church and the museumChanging

The original grid west of the new centre, below the mine, still holds the wooden church, the old homestead museum and a number of guesthouses and smaller hotels, and it is generally the cheapest place to sleep in town. It is also the part of Kiruna that is being wound down and moved, so the picture here changes from year to year and some streets are building sites while others are simply quiet. Worth knowing rather than worth avoiding: ask what is open nearby before you book, and expect a short bus ride or a taxi to the new centre for dinner.

Jukkasjärvi — the Torne river village

Ice hotelDog sleddingSeventeen kilometres out

A village of a few hundred people on the Torne river, seventeen kilometres east of Kiruna, and the reason a great many people come this far north at all: the ice hotel is rebuilt here out of river ice every winter and melts back into the river every spring, and the village is also a base for dog sledding and for the river itself in summer. There is accommodation here beyond the ice — warm rooms and cabins — and staying in the village means you are not driving back to town at midnight in February. You will need a transfer, a bus or a car for anything in Kiruna proper.

Abisko and the mountain road west

Best for auroraNational parkNinety minutes out

The valley running west towards the Norwegian border is the single best place in Sweden to look for the northern lights, and the reason is meteorological: Abisko sits in a rain shadow that keeps its sky clear when the forest and the coast are clouded over. The national park begins at the village, the Kungsleden long-distance path starts there, and the mountain station and the lodges at Björkliden and Riksgränsen sit along the same road and railway. It is about an hour and a half from Kiruna by train or bus. Stay here if the aurora is the point of the trip; stay in Kiruna if it is one item among several.

Getting around

Kiruna airport is about ten minutes from the new centre by taxi or airport bus, and there are daily flights from Stockholm. The alternative, and the better story, is the night train: it leaves Stockholm in the evening and arrives the following day, carries sleeping compartments and a restaurant car, and continues west through Abisko to Narvik on the Norwegian coast — which makes the stretch between Kiruna and the fjords one of the great rail journeys in Europe and, incidentally, the cheapest way to see it.

Within the region, scheduled buses connect Kiruna with Jukkasjärvi, Nikkaluokta and the villages, and the railway runs west to Abisko, Björkliden and Riksgränsen several times a day. Between them they cover almost everything a visitor wants without a car. Excursion operators collect from the hotels, which is how most people reach the aurora, the dog teams and the ice.

If you do drive in winter, take it seriously. Winter tyres are a legal requirement in the cold months and the hire company will fit them, but the roads are snow-covered rather than cleared to tarmac, daylight is short to non-existent, reindeer wander on to the carriageway without warning and there is a great deal of nothing between settlements. Fill the tank when you can, carry warm clothing in the car rather than in the boot, and tell someone where you are going.

When to come, and what it costs

There are two seasons here and almost nothing in between. Winter, from December to March, is what most visitors come for: the aurora, the dog sleds, the snowmobiles, the ice hotel and the polar night, which at this latitude means the sun does not rise at all for about a month around the solstice. It is not pitch dark — the middle of the day is a long blue twilight and the snow holds what light there is — but it is dark, and it is cold enough that the lodges lend you clothing rather than expecting you to own it. February and March are the sweet spot: the light is coming back, the snow is at its most reliable and the aurora is still strong.

Summer, from June to August, is the other Kiruna and it is badly underrated. The sun does not set at all from the end of May to the middle of July, the mountains at Abisko are open for walking, the Kungsleden starts its season and the rivers run high. Bring something to cover your head and arms: the mosquitoes in July are a genuine feature of the landscape rather than a nuisance to be shrugged off.

April to May and September to November are the gaps, and we would avoid them for a first visit. The winter activities have stopped or not started, the lake ice is unsafe, the walking paths are wet or snowed over, and a good many lodges simply close. The compensation is that late September has dark enough nights for the aurora with the autumn colour still on the birches, which is a fine combination if you do not mind that little else is running.

Sweden uses the Swedish krona (SEK, written kr), not the euro, and is one of the most nearly cashless countries in the world — a great many cafés, buses and museums take cards only and will politely refuse notes, so bring a card you can tap and do not bother changing money. Wine, spirits and stronger beer are sold only through the state shops, Systembolaget, which keep short hours and are shut on Sundays; weaker beer is in the supermarkets. Tipping is not expected and never chased — rounding up a bill is plenty. Decline any card machine that offers to charge you in dollars; pay in kronor and let your own bank convert.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS — the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years — is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today — nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave. Sweden is in both the European Union and the Schengen area, so arriving from another Schengen country there is no passport control at all.

Staying in Kiruna — questions

Where should I stay in Kiruna for the northern lights?

If the aurora is the main point of the trip, stay out at Abisko or along the mountain road west towards Bjorkliden - the valley sits in a rain shadow that keeps its sky clear far more often than Kiruna itself, which is why aurora watchers go there rather than anywhere prettier. If the lights are one item among several, stay in the new town centre in Kiruna and take an excursion out on the clearest nights; operators collect from the hotels and go where the sky is open. Either way, give yourself three or four nights at least.

Is the town of Kiruna really being moved?

Yes. The iron ore body runs underneath the original town centre, and as the mine follows it down the ground above is no longer safe to build on, so the centre is being rebuilt a few kilometres east. A new town hall and high street are already open and operating, and a number of historic wooden buildings have been carried across whole rather than demolished. For a visitor it means the new centre is where the hotels, shops and restaurants are, while parts of the old grid are quiet or under works - worth asking about when you book, and not a reason to stay away.

When can I see the midnight sun in Kiruna?

From roughly the end of May to the middle of July the sun stays above the horizon in Kiruna for twenty-four hours a day. In practice the effect lasts longer than the strict dates, because for several weeks either side it never gets properly dark. It is a strange and very pleasant thing to experience and it plays havoc with sleep, so take an eye mask - not every hotel room this far north has blackout curtains that earn the name.

What is the ice hotel and do I have to sleep in it?

The ice hotel at Jukkasjarvi, seventeen kilometres east of Kiruna, is built each winter out of ice cut from the Torne river and snow, with carved rooms and an ice bar, and it melts back into the river each spring. You do not have to sleep in it: most people visit it for a few hours, and the site also has ordinary warm rooms and cabins. Those who do spend a night in the cold rooms are given proper sleeping bags and briefed on how to use them, and nearly everyone does one night on the ice and the rest in the warm. Book well ahead for the winter season.

How cold does it get, and what should I bring?

Through the depths of winter temperatures commonly sit well below freezing and can drop a great deal further during a cold spell. The practical answer is layers rather than one thick coat: thermal base layers, a fleece or wool mid-layer, a proper windproof outer shell, warm boots with grip, and a hat, scarf and mittens rather than gloves. The important thing to know is that the excursion operators and lodges lend heavy outer suits and boots as a matter of course, so you do not need to buy polar kit for one trip - bring good base layers and let them supply the rest.

How do I get to Kiruna from Stockholm?

Two ways, and both are good. Daily flights take about ninety minutes and land ten minutes from the new town centre. The night train leaves Stockholm in the evening and arrives the following day, with sleeping compartments and a restaurant car, and it is both cheaper than flying and a considerable experience in its own right - the same line carries on west through Abisko to the Norwegian coast at Narvik, which is one of the great railway journeys in Europe.
