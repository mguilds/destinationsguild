Rovaniemi

Where to Stay in Rovaniemi

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Rovaniemi is the capital of Finnish Lapland, the place nearly every first Arctic trip starts, and it is a working town of about sixty thousand people rather than a resort. Almost all of it was destroyed in 1944 as the German army withdrew, and it was rebuilt to a plan by Alvar Aalto, so the centre is mid-century and practical rather than pretty. The Arctic Circle crosses the main road about eight kilometres north of town, and Santa Claus Village sits on the line, which is why a great many visitors end up sleeping out there instead of in the centre. Where you stay in Rovaniemi really answers one question: do you want shops, restaurants and buses on your doorstep, or do you want to be out where the sky is dark?

The centre, around Lordi’s Square

Shops and restaurantsBuses and the stationBest in bad weather

The centre is compact, flat and walkable, and it is the sensible base for a first visit. You have the restaurants, the supermarkets and the outdoor shops, the bus and railway stations, and the two museums worth your time — Arktikum, which explains the Arctic and Lapland properly, and the Korundi art museum. Nearly every activity operator picks up from the central hotels, which matters more than it sounds when it is minus twenty and dark. The honest limitation is the sky: streetlights make the centre the worst place in the region to watch for the aurora, so plan on being driven out of town for that.

Santa Claus Village and the Arctic Circle

Christmas tradeNearest the airportTaxi or bus into town

Eight kilometres north of the centre, on the Arctic Circle itself, Santa Claus Village is free to walk into, open all year, and the single busiest attraction in Lapland between late November and early January. Hotels, cabins and glass-roofed rooms have grown up around it, and the airport is five minutes away, which makes it a tempting place to land and stay. Be clear about the trade: you are in a visitor attraction rather than a town, meals are mostly where you sleep, and getting to the centre means the local bus or a taxi. In high season the whole area is busy by day and quiet by nine.

Ounasvaara, across the river

Ski slopesViews over townShort drive in

Ounasvaara is the wooded hill on the east bank of the Kemijoki, ten minutes from the centre by road and walkable across the bridge in good weather. It has the town’s downhill slopes, a web of cross-country and snowshoe trails, and hotels at the top with a view over the river and the lights of Rovaniemi. For a family that wants to ski or sledge without arranging transport every morning it is the obvious choice. You will want a car, a bus or a taxi for dinner in town, and the skies here are better than the centre’s but not properly dark.

The river roads and the cabins out of town

Darkest skiesFull boardCar or transfers needed

Along the Ounasjoki and the Kemijoki, and on the roads out towards Ranua and Sinettä, are the wilderness hotels, log cabins and glass-roofed rooms that most people picture when they imagine Lapland — along with the husky and reindeer farms. Twenty to forty kilometres out, the sky is genuinely dark and the aurora, when it appears, is visible from your own doorstep. These places sell full board and activity packages because there is nothing else within reach, the stock is small and fixed, and the good ones are booked months ahead for December and February. Nobody can promise you the lights, whatever the brochure implies.

Getting around

Rovaniemi’s centre is small enough to walk, and in winter that is a pleasure rather than a chore as long as your boots have grip. Beyond it you need wheels. Local buses run to Santa Claus Village and the airport through the day and are cheap; taxis are available but not plentiful, and should be booked rather than hailed. A hire car gives you the aurora chase on your own terms and comes on proper winter tyres as a matter of course — drive slowly, watch for reindeer standing in the road, and remember that side roads are packed snow from November to April. The airport is ten minutes north of town, and the overnight train from Helsinki arrives in the centre for breakfast and will carry your car. Almost every activity — huskies, snowmobiles, reindeer, aurora hunting — includes a pick-up from the central hotels, which is the simplest way to do it.

When to come, and what it costs

Rovaniemi has two peaks and two quiet spells. From late November the Christmas trade builds to a fortnight of full hotels and the highest prices of the year around Christmas and New Year; February and March are the second peak, and in our view the better one, with deep snow, lengthening days and the aurora season still running. January is cold, dark, quiet and the best value of the winter. Summer is a different town altogether: green, mild, cheap, almost empty of foreign visitors, with the sun up all night around midsummer and the rivers and fells there for walking and fishing. The two months to be careful with are October and November, when the snow has usually not settled and many operators are between seasons, and late April into May, when the snow is going and the summer has not begun. Whatever the season, the cabins and glass-roofed rooms are a small fixed stock: if that is what you are coming for, book it before the flights.

Finland uses the euro, and it is one of the most cashless countries in Europe — cards and phones are taken everywhere, including on buses and at market stalls, and some places no longer take cash at all. Decline any card machine that offers to charge you in dollars — the rate it gives you is worse than your own bank’s.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Finland itself is straightforward: in the European Union since 1995, the Schengen area since 2001 and the euro since 1999, with the notes and coins arriving in 2002. An arrival from another Schengen country has no passport control at all, and an arrival from outside it is a single check at the airport you land in. The land borders with Sweden and Norway in the north are internal Schengen crossings and are often barely marked; the crossings on the Russian border have been closed to travellers since 2023, so check with the Finnish Border Guard before planning anything that depends on one.

Staying in Rovaniemi — questions

Will I see the northern lights in Rovaniemi?

You have a real chance and no guarantee, and any tour that promises them is selling something it cannot deliver. Rovaniemi sits on the Arctic Circle, which is far enough north for the aurora to be a reasonable hope between late August and early April. What decides it is cloud, not the aurora, and the other thing that decides it is light: you need to be away from the town’s streetlights. Stay three or four nights, take at least one guided trip out of town, and treat the lights as a bonus on a trip worth making anyway.

Is Santa Claus Village worth it, and does it cost anything?

Walking in is free, all year. You cross the painted Arctic Circle line, visit the post office, which will hold a letter to be postmarked at Christmas, and meet Santa at no charge, with photographs sold separately. The paid attractions and the reindeer and husky rides around it are extra. For a family with young children it is the reason to come to Lapland; for a couple in search of the Arctic it is twenty minutes and a photograph. In the fortnight before Christmas it is very busy indeed.

Should I stay somewhere further north instead?

If what you want is wilderness, perhaps. Rovaniemi has the best transport, the widest choice of rooms and prices, and the easiest arrival, which is why most first trips start here; it is also a town, with a town’s lights and traffic. Places like Saariselkä, Inari, Levi and Ylläs are smaller, quieter, deeper into the fells and darker overhead. A sound compromise is two nights in Rovaniemi and two or three further north.

How cold is it, and what do I need to wear?

Expect somewhere between five and twenty-five degrees below zero through the winter, with colder snaps. It is a dry cold and much more bearable than the figures suggest if you dress for it: thermal base layer, wool middle layer, windproof shell, hat, gloves, scarf and boots with room for thick socks. You do not need to buy Arctic kit for a one-week trip, because every husky, snowmobile and aurora operator lends a full oversuit and boots. Studded slip-ons for icy pavements are the cheapest useful thing you will buy here.

Do I need to hire a car?

Not if you are staying in the centre and using activity pick-ups, which is how most visitors do it. Hire a car if you are staying out of town, if you want to chase clear sky for the aurora on your own schedule, or if you are travelling with enough people to make it cheaper than transfers. If you have never driven on snow, give yourself an hour of daylight to get used to it, leave long gaps, and take the reindeer warnings seriously — they stand in the road and they do not move.
