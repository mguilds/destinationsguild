Where to Stay in Ljubljana

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Ljubljana is a capital city of fewer than three hundred thousand people, and that is the single most useful thing to know when choosing where to sleep in it. The part a visitor wants is a flat oval about a mile across, split by a small green river, with a castle on a wooded hill in the middle of it and the Alps visible at the end of the streets. Almost any central room will put you within a quarter of an hour’s walk of everything, so the districts decide atmosphere and price rather than convenience. The old town across the Triple Bridge is the handsome, car-free, occasionally noisy choice; Center on the near bank has the larger and more comfortable hotels; the streets by the railway station have the cheapest beds and the late bars; Trnovo upstream is residential and quiet; and the Tivoli park side is where to go for value and for parking.

The old town: Mestni trg, Stari trg and the castle hill

Most centralCar-free streetsRiver at the door

The strip of old town along the right bank of the Ljubljanica, from the Triple Bridge up through Mestni trg and Stari trg to the foot of the castle hill, is the postcard and the obvious place to sleep. It is almost entirely closed to cars, which makes it quiet in a way that few European old towns manage, and everything a short visit wants is within a few minutes on foot: the market colonnade, the cathedral, the funicular up to the castle, the bars along the embankment. The buildings are old and mostly small, so expect boutique hotels and apartments rather than anything large, and lifts are not a given — ask before you book if stairs are a problem. The one real trade-off is noise: the riverside bars below Cankarjevo nabrežje and around the Triple Bridge run late in summer, and a room facing the water will hear them.

Center: Prešeren Square, Miklošičeva and Čopova

Walk everywhereLarger hotelsShops and cafes

The left bank immediately across the Triple Bridge is the commercial centre, and for most visitors it is the sensible choice. Prešeren Square is the city’s meeting point, Miklošičeva runs north from it past a remarkable row of Secession-era facades towards the station, and Čopova and the streets around Kongresni trg hold the cafes and the bookshops. The hotels here are bigger and more conventional than in the old town, which usually means a lift, a proper breakfast and air conditioning, and they are five minutes’ walk from the sights without being in the middle of the evening noise. It is also the best base if you are arriving or leaving by bus or train.

The station end: Tabor, Metelkova and Mošte

Cheapest roomsTrains and busesNightlife

North-east of the centre, around the railway and bus stations, is where the cheaper hotels, hostels and apartments are. It is a ten to fifteen minute walk into the old town on flat pavement, and the practical advantage is obvious if you are catching an early train or the airport bus. This is also where Metelkova sits — a former barracks turned squat and alternative culture complex, covered in murals, with clubs that run very late at weekends and a reputation that divides people sharply. The immediate streets by the station are the least charming part of central Ljubljana and a little scruffy at night, though not dangerous; a block or two further out, Tabor is simply a normal residential quarter.

Trnovo and Krakovo, south along the river

QuietestResidentialTen-minute walk

Follow the Ljubljanica upstream past the Cobblers’ Bridge and within ten minutes you are in Krakovo and then Trnovo, low streets of small houses and gardens that were market gardens within living memory. There are few hotels and a good many apartments and guesthouses, prices are lower than in the centre, and the walk in along the willow-lined embankment is one of the nicest things about staying in Ljubljana. It suits a stay of several nights, anyone travelling with children, or a visitor who would rather hear the river than a bar. Plečnik’s own house is here and open as a museum, and the Trnovo church beside the bridge is where he worshipped.

Tivoli and Šiška, by the park

Park on the doorstepGood valueBuses in

West and north-west of the centre, behind the Tivoli park, is residential Ljubljana: apartment blocks, a sports complex, the zoo on the wooded Rožnik hill behind, and room rates lower than anything closer in. You are fifteen to twenty minutes on foot from Prešeren Square through the park, which is a pleasant commute in daylight and a dark one late at night, or a short bus ride on the city network. Choose it if you are driving — parking is far easier out here than in the centre, where it is metered, scarce and in places simply impossible — or if you want a run or a walk under trees before breakfast.

Getting around

Ljubljana is one of the easiest European capitals to get around, because almost none of it needs transport at all: the whole of the centre is within about twenty minutes’ walk end to end, it is flat apart from the castle hill, and the core is pedestrianised. A car is a liability rather than a help — the old town is closed to traffic, central parking is metered and limited, and the garages fill. City buses run on the Urbana card, a stored-value card you buy and top up at machines and kiosks and touch on boarding; drivers do not take cash fares, so get one at the station if you expect to use the buses at all. The funicular from Krekov trg saves the climb to the castle, and there is a public bike-share scheme with stands across the city. Two things worth knowing: the Kavalir electric carts that potter around the pedestrian zone are free and are there precisely for people who cannot manage the distance, so wave one down without embarrassment; and the airport at Brnik is about twenty-six kilometres north, reached by a scheduled bus, by shared shuttle or by taxi, with the shuttle usually the best value for two or more people. Confirm bus times before you rely on them, especially on a Sunday or for an early flight.

When to come, and what it costs

Ljubljana has a continental climate with a genuinely hot summer and a cold, often foggy winter, and it is a city rather than a resort, so prices move with conferences and events as much as with the season. May, June and September are the best weeks: warm without being heavy, long evenings on the embankment, everything open. July and August are hot — frequently above thirty degrees Celsius — and the city empties of locals while filling with visitors; it is also when the Ljubljana Festival runs, and when a room without air conditioning becomes a real mistake. October is underrated, with the Rožnik woods turning and the student year giving the place its life back. From November to February expect grey, damp, often freezing days and the basin’s famous fog, lifted for a few weeks in December by one of the prettier Christmas markets in central Europe, which is also the one time of year the centre is genuinely busy and expensive. Rates are lowest in January, February and early March. Whenever you come, the city is small and its good rooms are few, so book earlier than the size of the place suggests.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Slovenia itself is straightforward: in the European Union since 2004, in the Schengen area since the end of 2007 and on the euro since the beginning of that same year. An arrival from another Schengen country has no passport control at all, and an arrival from outside it is a single check at the airport you land in. The land borders with Italy, Austria and Hungary are internal Schengen crossings and are often barely marked. The border with Croatia is also now an internal Schengen crossing, since Croatia joined at the start of 2023, although checks are sometimes reintroduced temporarily at particular crossings — carry your passport when you cross and allow extra time in high summer, when the routes to the Croatian coast queue badly.

Staying in Ljubljana — questions

How many days does Ljubljana need?

Two nights is enough to see the city properly without hurrying: the old town and the market, the castle, Prešeren Square and the Plečnik set pieces along the river, a museum or two and a long dinner by the water. The better answer is three or four nights, because Ljubljana is the most comfortable base in the country and almost everything in Slovenia is a day trip from it — Bled and the lakes in under an hour, Postojna and the Karst caves in about the same, the coast in roughly an hour and a half. Plenty of visitors see the whole country without ever changing hotel.

Is the castle worth it, and do I have to walk up?

It is worth an hour or two, mostly for the view across the red roofs to the Alps on a clear day, the ramparts and the puppet museum; the castle itself is a restored shell rather than a great historic interior. You do not have to walk: a funicular runs from Krekov trg near the market and takes a couple of minutes. If you would rather walk, there are several footpaths up through the trees from the old town, the gentlest of them from Stari trg, and it takes about twenty minutes at an unhurried pace. Check current funicular hours on the day — they change with the season.

What is the Plečnik thing everyone mentions?

Jože Plečnik was the architect who, between the wars, redesigned much of central Ljubljana as a single piece of work, and he is the reason the city looks unlike its neighbours. The Triple Bridge, the colonnaded riverside market, the National and University Library, the embankments and sluice gate, the Cobblers’ Bridge and the Žale cemetery are all his. You do not need to seek them out as a tour — you will walk over and through most of them in a morning — but knowing they are one man’s plan makes the walk a good deal more interesting. His house in Trnovo is a museum and is the best single hour on the subject.

Can I do Bled as a day trip from Ljubljana?

Easily, and a great many people do. It is roughly fifty-five kilometres, which is about an hour by bus from the main station and a little less by car. Buses are frequent in summer and thin out in winter, and the train is slower and leaves you at Lesce-Bled with a connection or a walk, so the bus is usually the better choice. A day is enough for the lake walk, the island and the castle. If you want the lake at dawn or at dusk, when it is at its best and emptiest, you need to stay the night — which is the case for the quieter Lake Bohinj as well.

Is Ljubljana expensive, and is tipping expected?

It is noticeably cheaper than Vienna or Venice and noticeably dearer than it was ten years ago. Eating and drinking are the good value: a weekday set lunch, the dnevno kosilo, is the way the city itself eats and costs a fraction of dinner from the same kitchen. Coffee on the embankment is cheap and nobody will hurry you off the chair. Tipping is not expected in the American sense; rounding up the bill, or something around ten per cent for a good dinner, is normal and plenty. Tap water is excellent throughout the city and is served free as a matter of course.
