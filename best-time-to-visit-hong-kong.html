When to Go to Hong Kong

The Best Time to Visit Hong Kong

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated August 2026

Short answer: autumn — roughly late October through early December — is the clear winner, and it is not a close contest. The summer humidity finally breaks, the skies turn their cleanest and clearest of the year, the breeze comes back, and the temperature settles into the comfortable twenties. That is when the Peak is worth climbing, the harbour actually looks like the postcard, and walking around the city stops being an endurance sport. Below is the full picture: the seasons, the months one by one, the cheapest time to go, how the typhoon season really works, and how to time Chinese New Year and Mid-Autumn.

Hong Kong sits just inside the tropics, which gives it a subtropical monsoon climate and a year of real character rather than one long warm blur. The Hong Kong Observatory’s own long-run figures tell the story plainly: about 80 per cent of the year’s rain falls between May and September, summer afternoons regularly push past 31°C with nights that barely drop below 26°C, and tropical cyclones are possible from May right through to November. Then autumn arrives and the whole territory exhales. Choose your dates well and Hong Kong is one of the most rewarding cities on earth to walk; choose badly and you will spend a fortnight moving between air-conditioned rooms. Here is how to get it right.

The Seasons at a Glance

The quick read on what each season actually feels like — weather, crowds, price, and who it suits.

Autumn — late September to early December

$$$$ PeakClear & dryBest all-roundBook ahead

The finest season, and the reason people fall in love with this city. The humidity breaks through late September and October, and by November and early December you get sunshine, pleasant breezes and comfortable temperatures — the Observatory itself singles out November and December as the months many consider the most agreeable of the year. Visibility is at its best, so the Peak, the harbour and the outlying islands all show themselves properly. This is also hiking season on the Dragon’s Back and Lantau. The catch is simple: everyone else knows it too, so hotels are full and dear.

Winter — mid-December to February

$$ Good valueCool & mostly dryFestivalsCloudier

Cool, comfortable and far drier than summer, with occasional cold fronts that can push urban temperatures below 10°C for a few days — genuinely chilly in buildings not built for it, so bring a proper jacket and layers. Skies turn cloudier than in autumn but sightseeing is easy and the crowds thin outside the holidays. This is the season of the Christmas lights along the harbour and of Chinese New Year, when the city is at its most theatrical. Good value in January and much of February, with a sharp holiday spike around the New Year itself.

Spring — March to April

$$$ ShoulderWarm & humidFog & drizzleGreen hills

Warm and increasingly sticky, and the season most often misjudged by visitors. March and April bring long humid spells with drizzle, low cloud and sea fog — the Observatory notes that visibility can drop enough to disrupt ferries and flights — which means the famous harbour view may simply not be there. On the other hand the hills go brilliantly green, the temperature is kinder than summer, and the calendar is busy with events. A decent shoulder season if you accept that the skyline is a lottery.

Summer — May to early September

$ CheapestHot & very humidRainy seasonTyphoon watch

Hot, wet and heavy. Afternoon highs frequently top 31°C, nights hover around 26°C, and most of the year’s rain falls now, often as short violent downpours and thunderstorms rather than all-day drizzle. This is also the heart of the tropical cyclone season. The compensations are real: the lowest prices of the year, the beaches and outlying islands at their best, and a city that has perfected the art of air conditioning. Plan indoor mornings, outdoor evenings, and keep the schedule loose.

Month by Month

The concise version — weather, crowds, and what each month does best.

January

$$ Good valueCoolest monthDry

The coolest and one of the driest months, comfortable for walking all day, with the occasional cold front dropping urban temperatures below 10°C for a spell. Skies can be grey but rain is infrequent. Prices are among the year’s best outside any Chinese New Year spike, and the city is calm after the winter holidays. Bring a warm layer and enjoy an easy, uncrowded month of markets, museums and dim sum.

February

$$ → spikeCoolChinese New Year

Still cool and mostly dry, with humidity beginning to creep up late in the month. Chinese New Year usually falls in late January or February — in 2027 it lands on 6 February — and for that week the city fills with flower markets, a night parade and harbour fireworks, while hotel rates leap and many small family shops close for several days. Outside that window it is a quiet, well-priced month.

March

$$$ ShoulderWarmingFog begins

Mild and pleasant on a good day, but the humid, foggy spells begin: low cloud and drizzle can sit over the harbour for days and occasionally disrupt ferries and flights. The hills turn green and the events calendar is busy, with the spring rugby tournament drawing a big international crowd and pushing hotel prices up sharply for a weekend. Pack a light rain jacket and keep the Peak flexible.

April

$$$ ShoulderWarm & humidGrey spells

Warm, green and often grey, with rising humidity and the year’s murkiest visibility. Between the misty days there are lovely bright ones, and the temperatures are far kinder than what is coming. The Ching Ming grave-sweeping holiday falls in early April, a public holiday but not a travel crush. A reasonable month for food, museums and neighbourhoods; a gamble for skyline photography.

May

$$ ValueHot & humidRains begin

Summer arrives in earnest: heat, heavy humidity and the start of the wet season, and the first month in which tropical cyclones become possible. It is also the start of the value season, and the beaches, ferries and outlying islands come into their own. Buddha’s Birthday brings the famous bun festival on Cheung Chau island, one of the most enjoyable days out in the calendar. Start early, rest at midday.

June

$ Low seasonWettest stretchDragon boats

One of the two wettest months of the year, hot and thoroughly humid, with dramatic downpours and thunderstorms that usually clear as fast as they arrive. Prices are low and the sights are quiet. The Tuen Ng dragon boat races are the highlight, staged at several harbours and beaches and worth planning around. Bring an umbrella, sandals you do not mind soaking, and a relaxed attitude to the schedule.

July

$ CheapestPeak typhoon riskVery hot

Hot, sticky and firmly inside the season when tropical cyclones are most likely to strike, which runs from July to September. Rooms are at their cheapest and the city is quiet, and the summer sales fill the malls. Build slack into your plans, take travel insurance seriously, and use the mornings for outdoor sights before the heat and the afternoon storms take over.

August

$ CheapestWettest & hottestFamily holidays

Along with June, the wettest month, and among the hottest, with high humidity around the clock. It is peak school-holiday season regionally, so the theme parks and beaches are busy even while the hotels are cheap. Typhoon risk remains high. If you come now, treat the harbour ferries, the air-conditioned museums and the long lunches as the plan rather than the fallback.

September

$$ ImprovingHumidity easingMid-Autumn

A month of transition. Early September is still hot, humid and inside the main cyclone window, but by the final week the air lightens and the first properly clear days appear. Mid-Autumn usually falls in September — 25 September in 2026, 15 September in 2027 — bringing lantern displays in Victoria Park and the Tai Hang fire dragon dance. Prices are still moderate. A fine gamble if you like the odds.

October

$$$$ PrimeClear & drySweet spot

The month the city has been waiting for: warm, far less humid, largely dry and often brilliantly clear, with the harbour and the Peak at their best. Hiking on the Dragon’s Back and Lantau becomes a pleasure rather than a trial. Expect crowds and high rates, especially around the National Day holiday at the start of the month and the trade fairs later on. Book well ahead and you will not regret the price.

November

$$$$ PrimeBest weatherBreezy & sunny

For our money the single best month of the year: pleasant breezes, plenty of sunshine, comfortable temperatures and the year’s cleanest air, with the cyclone season winding down. Everything outdoors works — the islands, the hikes, the rooftop bars, the ferry rides. It is busy and priced accordingly, and the big autumn trade fairs can make midweek rooms scarce, so lock in your dates early.

December

$$$ → holiday spikeCool & comfortableChristmas lights

Early December keeps the autumn magic going with cool, dry, sunny days, and it is arguably the best-value slice of the good season. The harbourfront towers light up for the winter festival, the shopping is at its most tempting, and the temperature is ideal for walking. From about the third week the Christmas and New Year holiday sends prices up again. Aim for the first fortnight.

The Cheapest Time to Go

If price is the deciding factor, Hong Kong’s pattern is unusually clear-cut.

June to August — hot, wet and half the price

$ Rock bottomFlights & hotelsWeather trade-off

Airfares and hotel rates bottom out through the wet, humid heart of summer, roughly June to August, when the heat, the downpours and the typhoon risk thin the visitor numbers. You are trading comfort for money, and it is a real trade — but the museums, the malls, the ferries and the food are all unaffected, and a harbour-view room can cost a fraction of its November price. January is the other bargain, cool and dry, with the caveat of the Chinese New Year spike.

Want good weather and value? Target the edges of the good season: late September, just as the humidity breaks but before the autumn rush, and the first two weeks of December, after the trade fairs and before the Christmas holiday. Both give you comfortable, often clear days at well below peak rates. As a general rule, midweek beats weekends, and avoiding Chinese New Year, the National Day holiday at the start of October and the big Convention Centre trade fairs will save you real money.

Typhoons, Heat & Crowds

The things that can spoil a Hong Kong trip are all predictable, which makes them easy to plan around.

Understanding the typhoon season

May–NovemberPeak: Jul–SepSignal 1, 3, 8Rarely a whole trip

Tropical cyclones can affect Hong Kong at any point from May to November, with July, August and September the most likely months. In practice most storms pass at a distance and bring a day of rain and wind. The city runs a clear public warning system — Signal 1 means a cyclone is within range, Signal 3 brings strong winds, and at Signal 8 the city effectively closes: offices and shops shut, ferries stop, buses wind down and flights are cancelled or delayed, though the MTR generally keeps running underground. Hotels handle it calmly and will keep you fed. The sensible precautions are ordinary ones: travel insurance that covers weather disruption, a flexible flight ticket if you can, no tight connections, and the Observatory’s warnings checked each morning. A Signal 8 day is an inconvenience, not a disaster — and it makes a good story.

Coping with the humidity — and dodging the crowds

80% of rain: May–SepNights near 26°CTrade fairsHoliday weeks

Summer humidity in Hong Kong is the sort that fogs your glasses the moment you step outside, and it does not let up much at night. If you visit between May and September, front-load your sightseeing into the morning, use the covered footbridges and the MTR rather than the pavements, carry water and an umbrella that doubles as a sunshade, and remember that indoor air conditioning is fierce — a light long-sleeved layer is genuinely useful. Older travelers and anyone with heart or breathing conditions should treat July and August with real caution.

For crowds, the pinch points are Chinese New Year, the National Day holiday in the first week of October, the Easter break and the big trade fairs at the Convention Centre, which can fill every business hotel on the island at short notice. Travel midweek, start early — the Peak Tram queue before nine is a different experience from the one at noon — and consider Kowloon over the island if rates on one side have spiked. Timing the Festivals

Hong Kong’s great festivals follow the lunar calendar, so the dates move each year. These are the ones worth building a trip around — and the one worth planning around.

Chinese New Year — late January or February

2027: 6 FebruaryParade & fireworksPrices spikeShops close

The biggest event of the year, and a genuine spectacle: flower markets in the days before, a night parade through Tsim Sha Tsui, fireworks over Victoria Harbour, temples thick with incense and the whole city dressed in red and gold. The trade-offs are real, though — hotel rates jump, flights across the region fill up, and many small family-run restaurants and shops shut for three or four days, so some of the eating you came for will not be available. In 2027 Lunar New Year’s Day falls on 6 February, with public holidays on the 6th, 8th and 9th. Come for the atmosphere, or avoid the week entirely; there is no middle ground.

Mid-Autumn Festival — September or early October

2026: 25 September2027: 15 SeptemberLanternsFire dragon

Our favourite festival to travel for, because it lands right at the turn into the good weather and disrupts nothing. On the fifteenth night of the eighth lunar month the parks fill with lantern displays — Victoria Park in Causeway Bay is the big one — families gather on the promenades with mooncakes, and the Tai Hang neighbourhood stages its remarkable fire dragon dance, a rope dragon studded with burning incense sticks paraded through narrow streets. It falls on 25 September in 2026 and 15 September in 2027. Hotels are busier than usual but nothing like New Year.

The rest of the calendar

Bun festival: MayDragon boats: JuneNational Day: 1 OctoberWinterFest: December

Buddha’s Birthday in May brings the Cheung Chau bun festival, with its towers of buns and floating-children parade, an easy and delightful ferry day trip. The Tuen Ng dragon boat races follow in June, drumming their way around several harbours. National Day on 1 October means fireworks over the harbour and a genuinely busy holiday week — lovely to watch, awkward to travel in. And from late November the harbourfront lights up for the winter festival season, running through Christmas and the Western New Year countdown. Chung Yeung, in October, is a public holiday when many families visit ancestral graves and the hillside paths get busy.

Frequently Asked Questions

The questions travelers ask us most when timing a trip to Hong Kong.

What is the best month to visit Hong Kong?

November is the single best month, with October and early December just behind it. Autumn brings pleasant breezes, plenty of sunshine, comfortable temperatures and the clearest air of the year, which is exactly what you want for the Peak, the harbour, the outlying islands and the hiking trails. The Hong Kong Observatory itself describes November and December as the months many people consider the most agreeable. The trade-off is that these are the busiest and most expensive weeks, so book well ahead.

When is the typhoon season in Hong Kong?

Tropical cyclones can affect Hong Kong from May through November, with July to September the most likely months. Most storms pass at a distance and bring nothing worse than a wet, windy day. The city uses a clear signal system: Signal 1 means a cyclone is within range, Signal 3 brings strong winds, and at Signal 8 shops, offices and ferries shut down and flights are disrupted, though the underground MTR usually keeps running. Take travel insurance that covers weather disruption, avoid tight flight connections, and check the Observatory’s warnings each morning.

What is the cheapest time to visit Hong Kong?

The hot, wet heart of summer — roughly June to August — is comfortably the cheapest for both flights and hotels, because the heat, the heavy rain and the typhoon risk keep visitor numbers down. January is the other good-value month, cool and dry, provided you avoid the Chinese New Year spike. For a compromise, late September and the first two weeks of December give you much of the good autumn weather at noticeably lower rates.

How hot and humid does Hong Kong get in summer?

Very. Afternoon highs regularly pass 31°C between May and August, and overnight lows often stay around 26°C, so there is little relief after dark. About 80 per cent of the year’s rain falls between May and September, usually in heavy bursts and thunderstorms rather than all-day drizzle, with June and August typically the wettest months. Plan outdoor sightseeing for the morning, use the MTR and the covered footbridges, carry water, and pack a light long-sleeved layer for the fierce indoor air conditioning.

When are Chinese New Year and the Mid-Autumn Festival?

Both follow the lunar calendar, so the dates shift each year. Chinese New Year falls in late January or February — in 2027 Lunar New Year’s Day is 6 February, with public holidays on the 6th, 8th and 9th — and brings flower markets, a night parade and harbour fireworks, along with higher prices and several days of closed family-run shops and restaurants. The Mid-Autumn Festival lands in September or early October, on 25 September in 2026 and 15 September in 2027, with lantern displays in Victoria Park and the Tai Hang fire dragon dance.

Is spring a good time to visit Hong Kong?

It is a mixed bag, and the one season travelers most often misjudge. March and April are warm and green, but they are also the foggiest, most humid stretch of the year, when low cloud and drizzle can sit over the harbour for days and reduce visibility enough to delay ferries and flights. If the skyline view is the reason you are coming, spring is a gamble; if you are here for food, markets, museums and neighbourhoods, it is perfectly pleasant and cheaper than autumn.

How many days do you need in Hong Kong?

Three to four days covers the headline sights comfortably — the Peak, the harbour and the Star Ferry, the temples and markets of Kowloon, and a dim sum lunch or two. Add a day or two for the outlying islands and the Big Buddha on Lantau, a beach or a hike on the Dragon’s Back, or a side trip to Macau, and five to six days makes a relaxed, well-rounded trip. If you are visiting in summer, allow an extra day of slack for weather.
