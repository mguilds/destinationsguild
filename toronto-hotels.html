Toronto

Where to Stay in Toronto

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Toronto is the biggest city in Canada and the one most visitors land in, and it is far easier to choose a district in than its size suggests: the subway and the streetcars cover the parts you want, almost everything central is flat, and the distances inside the old city are short. The real question is what you want outside your door — the big-ticket downtown sights, the lake, the museums and the shops, or the eating and drinking that the west end has quietly become known for.

The downtown core and the Entertainment District — closest to everything

Most hotelsUnion StationLoud at night

The blocks between Union Station and Queen Street hold the CN Tower, the ballpark, the hockey arena, the aquarium, the theatres and much the largest share of the city’s hotel rooms. Everything on a first-time list is a walk or one subway stop away, the airport train leaves from Union and the business rates often soften at weekends, which makes this good value on a Friday or Saturday night. The honest drawbacks: the King and Peter Street corner is a serious late-night bar district and loud until two in the morning, there is always a tower going up somewhere nearby, and the streets go quiet and windy on a winter Sunday.

The Harbourfront and the lake — for the water and the islands

Quietest downtownFerry to the IslandsThin in winter

South of the railway the city turns to the water: a long boardwalk, the Harbourfront Centre, the Music Garden, a line of newer hotels and the ferry terminal for the Toronto Islands, which are the best half-day in the city and give you the postcard view of the skyline. It is five to fifteen minutes on foot from the core, noticeably calmer at night, and it suits anyone who wants to walk or run by the lake in the morning. In exchange it is a little cut off by the Gardiner Expressway, there is less to walk to for dinner, and from November to March the waterfront is a cold and largely empty place.

Yorkville, the Annex and midtown — museums, shops and trees

Best for museumsLeafy streetsSmarter hotels

Two subway stops north of the core, Yorkville is the expensive shopping quarter — the Royal Ontario Museum and the Gardiner ceramics museum are at its edge, the University of Toronto campus is beside it and the Annex behind it is a handsome run of brick Victorian houses with good small restaurants on Bloor and Harbord. The hotels are smaller and smarter than downtown and the streets are leafy and calm in the evening. It is where we would stay on a second visit. The trade-off is price in Yorkville proper and a subway ride rather than a walk to the lake and the ballpark.

Queen West, Ossington and the west end — where the city eats

Best eatingStreetcarsFew big hotels

West of Spadina the city stops performing and starts living: Kensington Market, Trinity Bellwoods park, the galleries and shops of Queen West, and the restaurants and bars of Ossington and Dundas West, which are where people who live here actually go out. Accommodation is mostly small design hotels and guest houses rather than chains, which means fewer rooms and earlier booking. Be clear about the distance: it is a fifteen to thirty-minute streetcar ride from Union Station, the streetcars are frequent but slow in traffic, and you are a long way from the airport train.

Getting around

Toronto runs on the TTC: two main subway lines in a U through the middle of the city, a network of streetcars along the big east-west streets and buses everywhere else. A PRESTO card or a contactless bank card taps you through all of it, and one fare covers transfers for two hours. The subway is quick and the streetcars are not — they share the road with traffic — so allow more time than the map suggests for a crosstown journey in the afternoon.

From Pearson, the airport train, the UP Express, runs every fifteen minutes to Union Station in about twenty-five, which is by a wide margin the sensible way in: a taxi or ride-hail can take an hour in traffic and costs several times as much. The small Billy Bishop airport on the island, used by some domestic and short regional flights, is a tunnel walk from the foot of Bathurst Street and is barely ten minutes from downtown.

Two local tricks. In winter, the PATH — a signed network of underground walkways linking the downtown towers, Union Station and several hotels and malls — lets you cross much of the core without going outside. And in summer the Toronto Islands ferry from the foot of Bay Street is the cheapest good thing to do in the city: fifteen minutes to car-free parkland, beaches and the best view of the skyline.

When to come

September and the first half of October are the best weeks of the year here: warm days, cool nights, the patios still out, the film festival in early September and the maples turning in the ravines and north of the city by the end of it. May and June are the other good stretch, green and fresh, with everything open and prices below the summer peak.

July and August are hot and genuinely humid — the city sits at the western end of Lake Ontario and holds the heat — with thunderstorms, full festivals and the islands and the beaches busy. Winter, December to February, is cold, often below freezing all day, with a wind off the lake that is the thing people remember. The city is built for it: the subway runs, the PATH is warm and the museums, restaurants and hockey are at their best. March and April are grey and slushy and the cheapest months to visit.

The one thing worth building a day around is Niagara Falls, ninety minutes south-west by road or by train and far better from the Canadian side. Our month-by-month guide to the best time to visit Canada goes through the year properly.

Getting in

Most visitors from visa-exempt countries arriving in Canada by air need an Electronic Travel Authorization, an eTA, applied for online before they fly and tied to the passport they travel on. Citizens of the United States do not need one but do need proof of citizenship. Nationals of visa-required countries need a visitor visa instead. An eTA is not required if you arrive by land or by sea. We do not print the fees here because they are revised from time to time — read them off the official pages at canada.ca when you book, and apply there rather than through an agent charging a mark-up for the same form.

Two Canada-specific points worth checking well before you travel: past criminal convictions, including an old drink-driving conviction, can make a traveller inadmissible, and there is a formal process for it that is not quick; and taking cannabis across the border in either direction is a federal offence whatever is legal at home. Toronto hotels FAQ

Which part of Toronto should I stay in?

The downtown core for a first visit: Union Station, the airport train, the CN Tower, the ballpark and the lake are all within a short walk or one subway stop, and the choice of rooms is widest. Yorkville or the Annex if you would rather have museums, trees and a quieter evening, and the Queen West or Ossington end of the west end if you came to eat and drink and do not mind a streetcar ride into the middle.

What is the best way in from the airport?

The UP Express train from Pearson to Union Station: every fifteen minutes, about twenty-five minutes, and a fraction of the cost of a taxi. A car can take an hour in traffic and will cost several times as much. If you land at Billy Bishop on the island instead, you are a short tunnel walk and a ten-minute ride from downtown.

How many days does Toronto need?

Two full days covers the downtown sights, a museum and an evening out; three is comfortable and lets you add the Toronto Islands or a long wander through Kensington Market and the west end. Add a fourth if you want Niagara Falls, which is a full day rather than a half.

Is Toronto walkable?

In parts, and flatter than people expect. The core, the Harbourfront, Yorkville and the Annex are all comfortable on foot and link up along Bloor and Queen. But the old city is a long rectangle rather than a compact centre, so crossing it east to west or going out to the west end is a streetcar or subway journey rather than a walk.

Can I do Niagara Falls from Toronto?

Easily, and it is worth it. It is about ninety minutes each way by road, with regular coach services, and there is a seasonal train. Go for a whole day rather than half of one, see it from the Canadian side where the bend of the river puts the view, and take the boat to the foot of the Horseshoe Fall if it is running.
