Where to Stay in Dublin

Where to Stay in Dublin — Best Hotels & Neighbourhoods

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated August 2026

Dublin is one of the easiest capitals in Europe to arrive in cold. Everything is in English, the historic centre is small enough to cross on foot in twenty minutes, and the Georgian half of it — St Stephen’s Green, Merrion Square, Grafton Street — is agreeably flat. The catch is underfoot rather than uphill: Temple Bar and the lanes around Dublin Castle keep their old cobbles, and a good many handsome Georgian doorways are reached by a short flight of steps with no ramp beside them. This guide takes that seriously. Below are Dublin’s most dependable hotels by style, each with an honest note and a Book Me link straight to Trip.com.

The flat half of Dublin, and the cobbled half

The line to remember runs roughly along Grafton Street. South and east of it — St Stephen’s Green, Merrion Square, Baggot Street, Ballsbridge — the ground is level, the pavements are wide and the walking is genuinely easy. West and north, into Temple Bar and around Dublin Castle and Christ Church, you meet real cobbles, which are charming to look at and hard work with a wheeled case or an unsteady ankle. None of it is off limits; it simply argues for sleeping on the level side and visiting the cobbled side in daylight, in proper shoes, at your own pace.

Where a first-timer should stay, and where a light sleeper should

For a first visit, stay within a few streets of St Stephen’s Green. Trinity College, the National Gallery, the National Museum, Grafton Street and half the city’s good restaurants are inside a fifteen-minute flat walk, and taxis are easy to find when your legs have had enough. If quiet matters more than proximity, look at Ballsbridge and the embassy streets just beyond the Grand Canal: leafy, residential, silent at night, and still only twenty-five minutes on foot from the Green. What we would avoid for sleep is the heart of Temple Bar, which is loud until very late every night of the week.

Lifts are not a given — ask before you book

This is the single most useful question an older traveller can put to a Dublin hotel. The international names and the modern boutiques — The Shelbourne, the Conrad, the Hilton, The Dean, Premier Inn — have lifts serving every floor and step-free public rooms. The Georgian townhouse guesthouses, which are often the more charming choice, are converted eighteenth-century buildings where the stairs are the original ones and there may be no lift at all, sometimes with a few steps up from the street as well. Email or telephone and ask for a ground-floor or lift-served room. Irish hotels answer that question kindly and honestly.

Take the train, not the plane, to the rest of Ireland

Ireland is smaller than most Americans expect, and its intercity trains leave from the middle of Dublin and arrive in the middle of everywhere else. Cork is about two hours fifty from Heuston, Galway about two hours forty-five, Kilkenny about an hour and a half, Belfast about two hours from Connolly. Once you add airport transfers and security to a domestic flight, the train wins on the clock as well as on comfort — and you keep your luggage beside you and watch the midlands go by. Book online in advance; the walk-up fare is the dearest one.

Luxury

Dublin’s two grandest addresses sit within a few hundred yards of each other, both on flat ground, both a short walk from everything worth seeing.

The Merrion Hotel

$$$$ LuxuryUpper Merrion StreetGeorgian townhousesSpaLevel walk to the Green

Four interlinked eighteenth-century townhouses opposite Government Buildings, hung with one of the finest private art collections in Ireland and run with the kind of old-fashioned attentiveness that is getting hard to buy. Rooms are large by European standards, the drawing rooms and walled garden are made for a slow afternoon, and St Stephen’s Green is two minutes away on entirely level ground. One honest note: the historic wing has some changes of level, so ask for a garden-wing room served by the lift if stairs are a concern.

Boutique

Character over scale — but ask about stairs, because character in Dublin usually means a Georgian staircase.

A pair of joined Georgian houses on a hidden mews off Lower Leeson Street, one of them a former coach house with a sunken 1960s living room that visitors either love or photograph in disbelief. Budget & Value

The British budget stalwart, and useful here precisely because it is unsurprising: consistent rooms, comfortable beds, lifts, modern bathrooms and a location a flat few minutes from Dublin Castle, Trinity College and the quays. Adults-only

We found exactly one hotel in Dublin that genuinely enforces an adults-only policy, so we are listing one rather than inventing a second.

Kellys Hotel Dublin

$$ Adults-onlyCreative QuarterStrictly 18+Design boutiqueVery central

A small, design-driven boutique above a bar on Fade Street, in the little Creative Quarter between Grafton Street and Dublin Castle, and listed by the major booking sites as adults-only with an 18-and-over check-in policy — a genuinely useful thing if you specifically want no children about. Two honest cautions: it is a compact converted building and we could not confirm lift access, so telephone if stairs are a problem; and being above a bar, ask for a room at the back at weekends.

Dublin Hotels — common questions

Is Dublin easy to walk for an older traveller?

Easier than most European capitals, with one qualification. The Georgian half of the city — St Stephen’s Green, Merrion Square, Baggot Street, Grafton Street — is flat, wide-pavemented and genuinely pleasant to walk, and Trinity College to the Green takes about ten minutes at an unhurried pace. The qualification is the older quarter west of Grafton Street: Temple Bar and the lanes around Dublin Castle and Christ Church keep their original cobbles, which are hard going with a wheeled case or an unsteady ankle, and many Georgian doorways have a few steps and no ramp. Sleep on the level side, visit the cobbled side by day in good shoes, and take a taxi when your legs have had enough — they are plentiful and fairly metered.

What is the best area of Dublin to stay in?

For a first visit, within a few streets of St Stephen’s Green. It is flat, it is safe, and Trinity College, the National Gallery, the National Museum, Grafton Street and a great many of the good restaurants are all inside a fifteen-minute walk. If you want quiet above all, look at Ballsbridge and the embassy streets just past the Grand Canal — leafy, residential, silent at night, and still walkable to the centre in about twenty-five minutes. What we would not choose for sleeping is the middle of Temple Bar. It is a wonderful hour of an evening and a poor night’s rest, being noisy until very late, every night of the week, all year round.

How do I get from Dublin Airport to my hotel without a fuss?

Take a taxi. It costs roughly €25 to €35, takes twenty-five to forty minutes depending on traffic, and the ranks are directly outside both terminals with staff marshalling the queue — no hunting for a bus stop with your cases. If you would rather save the money, the Aircoach and Dublin Express coaches run every fifteen minutes or so for about €10, have proper luggage racks underneath, and stop within a short walk of most central hotels. The ordinary city buses are cheapest but have very little luggage room, so they are the poorest choice on arrival day. There is still no direct train or tram from the airport.

Do Dublin hotels have lifts, or is it all Georgian stairs?

Both, and it is worth choosing on purpose rather than hoping. The international brands and the modern boutiques — The Shelbourne, the Conrad, the Hilton, The Dean, Premier Inn, the Trinity City — all have lifts serving every floor and step-free public rooms. The Georgian townhouse guesthouses, which are often the more characterful choice, are converted eighteenth-century houses: the staircase is the original one, there may be no lift, and there are sometimes a few steps up from the pavement as well. The answer is simply to ask. Send an email or telephone and request a ground-floor or lift-served room; Irish hotels answer that question directly and without fuss.

Should I take the train or fly to the rest of Ireland?

Take the train, in almost every case. Ireland is small — smaller than the state of Maine — and the intercity trains leave from the middle of Dublin and arrive in the middle of everywhere else. Cork is about two hours fifty minutes from Heuston, Galway about two hours forty-five, Kilkenny about an hour and a half, Belfast about two hours from Connolly. Add the airport transfers, the check-in and the security queue to a short domestic flight and the train wins on the clock as well as on comfort, with your luggage beside you and the midlands going past the window. Book online in advance; the fare bought on the day is always the dearest.

When is the best time of year to visit Dublin?

May, June and September, without much argument. Those months give you the longest useful daylight, the best odds of a dry afternoon, and crowds and room rates well below the July and August peak — and in June the light lasts until nearly half past ten at night. July and August bring the warmest weather but also the school holidays, the highest prices and the fullest hotels. Winter is quiet and often good value, and Dublin’s museums, galleries and pubs run exactly as they do in summer, but the daylight is short and the rain persistent. Whenever you come, pack a proper waterproof rather than an umbrella; the wind will take the umbrella.
