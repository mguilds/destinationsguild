Maldives Travel Guide

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Twenty-six coral atolls strung down 500 miles of the Indian Ocean, holding something over a thousand islands of which about two hundred are lived on and rather more than that are hotels. It is the flattest country on earth and most of it is water. For thirty years the only way to see it was to buy a private island for a week; since guesthouses were allowed on inhabited islands you can also stay in a Maldivian village for a tenth of the money. This is the Guild’s chapter on both.

Why visit the Maldives

Four reasons the Guild sends travellers here.

The water

Warm the whole year, clear enough in the dry months to see the bottom of a channel from the surface, and arranged in lagoons so shallow and so pale that the colour does not photograph properly. This is the reason people come, and it does not disappoint.

What lives in it

Manta rays gathering in their dozens in the Baa atoll, whale sharks moving along the southern edge of Ari all year, tiger sharks off Fuvahmulah, and reef life on a house reef you can swim to from the beach. You do not need to dive — a mask does most of it.

Doing nothing, properly

One transfer in, one transfer out, and nothing in between but a room, a reef and a long view. Few places are so well designed for a trip where the itinerary is the point of what you are escaping.

The other Maldives

Two hundred islands are lived on, and since guesthouses were permitted you can stay in them. A fishing village with a mosque, a football pitch, a few cafes and a bikini beach is a very different holiday from a private island — and roughly a tenth of the price.

The atolls

The country runs north to south in a double chain of atolls, and the one you choose decides your transfer, your budget and what you are likely to see in the water. Islands with a Guild guide are marked; the rest link straight to hotels through our Trip.com partner while their chapters are written.

How the country divides up

Greater Malé

Almost everything arrives here. Velana International Airport sits on its own island, Hulhulé, joined by the Sinamalé Bridge to the capital Malé on one side and the reclaimed island of Hulhumalé on the other. Malé is one of the most densely built cities anywhere — a square mile of tall buildings, scooters and a very good fish market. Hulhumalé is its opposite: wide, new, quiet, with a long public beach and the city’s most useful cluster of ordinary hotels.

The near atolls

South and east of the capital, Kaafu and Vaavu hold the islands reached by a speedboat rather than a flight — Maafushi, Guraidhoo, Thulusdhoo, Fulidhoo. This is where a short trip belongs, and where guesthouse tourism is thickest.

The Ari atolls and Baa

Ari, west of the capital, is the diving heart of the country: drift dives on the channels, and along its southern rim the South Ari Marine Protected Area, where whale sharks are seen through the year. Baa, to the north, is a UNESCO biosphere reserve and holds Hanifaru Bay, where manta rays feed in numbers in the south-west monsoon. Hanifaru is managed: permits, limited numbers, snorkelling only, no scuba. Book it through your island and expect to be told when your slot is.

The far south

Below the equator the country changes. Fuvahmulah is a single island that forms an entire atoll, with freshwater lakes inland and a harbour where tiger sharks are seen reliably enough that divers fly down for them alone. Addu, the southernmost atoll, has several islands joined by causeways, a British wartime airfield turned airport, and a lagoon big enough to have had a fleet in it.

Top things to do in the Maldives

The experiences worth building a trip around.

Swim with a whale shark off South Ari

The protected stretch along the southern rim of Ari atoll holds them all year, which almost nowhere else does. Go with a boat that keeps its distance and its numbers down; the good operators will tell you the rules before you get in.

Manta rays at Hanifaru Bay

In the south-west monsoon the current funnels plankton into one small bay in Baa atoll and the mantas arrive to feed. Snorkel only, permit only, numbers capped — and worth arranging your dates around.

The house reef at first light

The underrated one. Most islands have a reef you can swim to from the sand, and at seven in the morning it is yours. A mask and an hour will show you more than a boat trip at noon.

The fish market in Malé

Late afternoon, when the dhonis come in and the tuna are carried up the quay on shoulders. The most purely Maldivian hour in the country, and it is free.

A night on a guesthouse island

Eat where the islanders eat, watch the football on the sand at dusk, and see the version of this country that the resort brochures leave out.

Tiger sharks at Fuvahmulah

A serious dive, not a holiday excursion — deep, current-swept and run by a handful of operators on one island below the equator. Divers plan whole trips around it.

Best time to visit & getting there

When to go

The Maldives has two monsoons and no cold. December to April is iruvai, the north-east monsoon: drier, calmer, clearer underwater and sunnier, and priced accordingly. The fortnight around Christmas and New Year is the costliest two weeks of the year by a distance.

May to November is hulhangu, the south-west monsoon — windier, wetter and far cheaper. The rain tends to come in squalls and move on rather than settle, and the islands stay warm throughout. Divers often prefer these months on the eastern side of the atolls, where the current brings the plankton and the plankton brings the manta rays.

Ramadan moves through the calendar by about eleven days a year. It changes very little at a resort and a fair amount on an inhabited island, where cafes may keep different hours. Getting there, and what it costs on top

There are no direct flights from North America. Almost everyone connects through Doha, Dubai, Abu Dhabi, Istanbul, Colombo or Singapore into Velana International Airport (MLE), on the island next to the capital. From there your island is reached by speedboat, by a domestic flight and a boat, or by seaplane. Seaplanes fly in daylight only, so a late-evening arrival may mean a night near the airport before you go on.

A free tourist visa is issued on arrival to all nationalities who arrive with a valid passport, an onward ticket, a booking and funds; thirty days is the standard period. Every traveller must also submit the Maldives Traveller Declaration online within 96 hours before arrival and again within 96 hours before leaving. Confirm both with Maldives Immigration directly — and use only the official government site.

On the money: a green tax per guest per night and a tourism goods and services tax are added to your bill, at different rates for resorts and for guesthouses on inhabited islands, and a departure tax sits inside your airfare. All of these have been raised in the last two years. We do not print the rates here, because a stale number costs you more than no number at all: ask the property for the full total including taxes before you pay, and check the current rates with the Maldives Inland Revenue Authority.

Fishing here

The Guild has no fishing guide to the Maldives yet. The flats, channels and blue water here are among the great saltwater fisheries of the Indian Ocean, and the country has protected its sharks in law for years and closed parts of its reefs to fishing altogether, so the rules matter as much as the tackle. That is a chapter for somebody who has stood on those flats and knows which atolls allow what, rather than a paragraph borrowed from somewhere else. Maldives travel FAQ

When is the best time to visit the Maldives?

December to April, the north-east monsoon the Maldivians call iruvai: drier, sunnier, calmer seas and the best underwater visibility of the year. It is also the expensive season, and the fortnight around Christmas and New Year is the most expensive stretch of all. May to November is the south-west monsoon, hulhangu — wetter, windier and much cheaper, with rain that usually arrives in squalls rather than settling in for the day. Divers often prefer the wet months in the east of the atolls, where the plankton that comes with the current brings manta rays.

Do Americans need a visa for the Maldives?

No visa has to be obtained in advance. A free tourist visa is issued on arrival to visitors of every nationality who arrive with a passport valid for the stay, a confirmed onward or return ticket, a booking for somewhere to stay and enough money for the trip. The standard period granted is thirty days. Separately, every traveller must submit the Maldives Traveller Declaration online within 96 hours before arrival and again within 96 hours before departure — this is a requirement of Maldives Immigration, it applies to arrivals and departures alike, and it is the thing visitors most often forget. Confirm the current rules on Maldives Immigration’s own website before you fly.

Do you have to stay in an expensive resort island?

Not since the law changed and guesthouses were allowed on inhabited islands. There are now two quite different Maldives holidays. A resort island is a private island where one hotel occupies the whole thing, all meals and transfers are part of the arrangement and the rules of the country are relaxed indoors. A guesthouse island such as Maafushi or Dhigurah is a working Maldivian village where you stay in a small hotel, eat in local cafes and pay a fraction of the price. The second is cheaper, more interesting and asks you to respect local custom. Plenty of travellers do a week of one and a few nights of the other.

What are the rules about alcohol and swimwear?

The Maldives is a Muslim country and the law is not a formality. Alcohol is not sold on inhabited islands — only at resorts, on liveaboard boats and at the hotel by the airport — and bringing it in with you is prohibited; it will be taken at customs. On an inhabited island, bikinis and swim shorts belong on the stretch of sand marked as a bikini beach and nowhere else, and you cover shoulders and knees walking through the village. On a resort island, which is private, none of this applies. Nobody will be unpleasant about it, but these are the country’s own rules and worth knowing before you pack.

How do you get from the airport to where you are staying?

By speedboat, by domestic flight or by seaplane, and which one you get depends entirely on how far away your island is. The near atolls are a speedboat ride of twenty minutes to an hour. Further out it is a domestic flight to a regional airport and then a boat. Islands with no runway within reach are reached by seaplane. Two things to plan around: seaplanes fly in daylight only, so a late arrival can mean a night near the airport before you travel on, and transfers are a real part of the cost — ask what yours will be before you book the room, not after.

Is the Maldives a good destination for older travellers?

Yes, and in some ways it is the easiest country in Asia for it. You arrive, you transfer once and then you do not move again; there is no touring, no altitude and no long road journeys. What to watch is the boat. A speedboat crossing in the windy months can be a wet, bouncy hour, and a seaplane is a small aircraft with a short ladder and no cabin crew. If either is a concern, choose an island reached by a short transfer in the calm season, and say what you need when you book — the properties are used to arranging it.
