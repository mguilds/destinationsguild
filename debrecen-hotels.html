Debrecen

Where to Stay in Debrecen

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Debrecen is Hungary’s second city, about two hundred thousand people on the flat eastern plain near the Romanian border, and it is nothing like Budapest. It grew rich as a market town on the puszta, became the capital of Hungarian Protestantism — it is still called the Calvinist Rome — and twice in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries served as the country’s emergency capital. For a visitor it is a calm, inexpensive, strikingly spacious city with one long main street, a great yellow church at the top of it and a forest with thermal baths at the far end. Choosing where to sleep here comes down to whether you want the city or the trees.

Where to stay in Debrecen, area by area

Four parts of a long, flat, easy city, and which end of it suits the kind of trip you are taking.

Kossuth tér and Piac utca — the main square and the high street

Most centralEverything on footTrams at the door

Debrecen has one great axis, Piac utca, running north to the Great Reformed Church on Kossuth tér, and the middle of the city is simply the length of it. The church is the largest Protestant church in Hungary and the building the city is known by; the Déri Museum with its enormous Munkácsy canvases is a few minutes behind it, and the shops, cafes and restaurants line the street between. Staying here means walking to everything, with the tram at the kerb for the forest end. The hotels are mostly mid-sized and modern with lifts and parking, and prices are low. It is quiet after about ten in the evening, which suits most visitors and will disappoint anybody hoping for Budapest’s nightlife.

The Nagyerdő — the forest park, the university and the baths

Green and quietBaths on the doorstepTram into town

North of the centre the city runs into the Nagyerdő, the Great Forest: a large wooded park holding the thermal and water-park complex, the university’s grand main building, a stadium and a lake. The tram takes about ten minutes into the middle of town and runs often. This is the pleasantest part of Debrecen to sleep in if you are staying more than a night, and the obvious base if the baths are why you came, since you can be in the water before the crowds. Rooms tend to be larger and better value than the centre. The trade-off is the usual one: not much to walk out to in the evening beyond the park itself and the student bars near the university.

The station end — the southern stretch of Piac utca

Arrivals and departuresCheapest roomsPlain surroundings

The lower end of the main street around the railway station, where the trains from Budapest and the buses to Hortobágy arrive. Nobody comes to Debrecen for this quarter and it earns its place purely on convenience: it is a flat fifteen-minute walk or three tram stops up to the church, the hotels are modern, plain and the cheapest in the city, and an early train is an easy matter. Choose it for a single night between journeys, or if you are touring the east by rail and want to keep the bill down. Do not choose it for atmosphere.

Out towards Hortobágy and the plain

Puszta on the doorstepHorses and birdsCar or tour needed

Debrecen is the gateway to the Hortobágy, the great grass plain to the west that is a national park and a UNESCO site, famous for its grey cattle, horse herds, shepherds’ traditions and autumn crane migration. A small number of guesthouses and inns sit out in the villages near the park, and staying in one puts you on the grassland at dawn and dusk, which is when it is worth seeing. It is an hour or so from the city by road and the buses are infrequent, so this really wants a car or a booked tour. Treat it as a night away from Debrecen rather than a base for it.

Getting around

Debrecen is flat and generously laid out, and walking the length of the centre takes about twenty minutes. The backbone of public transport is the tram, which runs from the station up Piac utca past the Great Church and out into the Nagyerdő in a long loop, every few minutes through the day. Buses and trolleybuses cover the rest of the city. One ticket type covers all of them, bought from machines, kiosks or the app, and validated as you board.

Trains from Budapest Nyugati take roughly two and a half to three hours on the direct intercity services and are comfortable and cheap; the line continues east to the Romanian border. Debrecen also has a small international airport with a handful of European routes, mostly seasonal, which is worth a look if the dates happen to suit.

For Hortobágy, there are trains and buses west to the village, but the park itself is spread out and the wildlife is best at the ends of the day, so most visitors either hire a car or take an organised trip. A car is otherwise unnecessary in Debrecen, where parking in the centre is paid and the tram does the work.

When to come, and what it costs

May, June and September are the best months here, as they are across the plain: warm, green and long, without the hard heat. July and August are hot and the plain gives no shade, though the baths and the forest park make the city itself bearable; the twentieth of August brings the Flower Carnival, Debrecen’s big annual event, when the city fills and rooms are booked well ahead. Autumn is the time for the Hortobágy, when the cranes come through in great numbers. Winter is cold, grey and very cheap, and the indoor thermal pools are the reason to be here.

Debrecen is inexpensive even by Hungarian standards. A room in the centre or by the forest costs a good deal less than the equivalent in Budapest, eating out is cheap, and the trams and museums cost very little. The exceptions are the carnival week in August and the university’s graduation weekends, when the city is genuinely full. Hungary uses the Hungarian forint (HUF, written Ft), not the euro. Hungary has been in the European Union since 2004 but has kept its own currency and has set no date for changing it, so euro notes are not legal tender. A few hotels and tour desks quote in euros and some will take euro cash, almost always at a poor rate. Pay in forint. Cards and phones are taken nearly everywhere, including on buses, trams and at ticket machines, and you need very little cash — a small amount is handy for market stalls, thermal-bath lockers, church donation boxes and rural buses. Prices in shops include tax. In Budapest restaurants a service charge is often printed on the bill; check before adding a tip, and where there is none, ten per cent is plenty. Decline any card machine offering to charge you in dollars and let your own bank convert. Avoid the standalone exchange booths near the main tourist streets and at the airport, which are far worse than a bank machine or an ordinary exchange office in town.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS — the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years — is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today — nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Hungary is a straightforward case. It has been in the European Union since 2004 and in the Schengen area since the end of 2007, so everything above applies here in full, and flying in from Vienna, Munich or Amsterdam involves no passport control at all. What Hungary has not joined is the euro, so the currency is the forint. Its borders with Austria, Slovakia, Slovenia, Croatia and Romania are all internal Schengen crossings now and you will usually drive or ride straight through them. The borders with Serbia and with Ukraine are external Schengen frontiers, where there are proper checks and the queues can be long. None of that affects an ordinary trip to the places in this guide.

Staying in Debrecen — questions

Is Debrecen worth visiting?

It is, provided you want what it offers rather than a second Budapest. Debrecen is calm, spacious, cheap and genuinely Hungarian in a way the capital is not, with a great Protestant church at its heart, a good museum, a forest park with thermal baths and the Hortobagy plain on its doorstep. Two nights is about right. If you are after nightlife, architecture on the scale of the capital or a dense old town, this is not the city for it.

Should I stay in the centre or by the forest?

The centre, on or near Piac utca, if you want to walk to everything and are staying one or two nights. The Nagyerdo forest park if you are staying longer, travelling with family, or have come for the baths, since the rooms are greener, quieter and often larger, and the tram into town takes about ten minutes. The station end is the cheapest and the plainest, and makes sense for a single night between trains.

What is there to do in Debrecen?

The Great Reformed Church on Kossuth ter and the Reformed College behind it; the Deri Museum, whose Munkacsy trilogy is one of the most important sets of paintings in Hungary; the Nagyerdo park with its thermal baths and water park; and the modern art at MODEM. Beyond the city, Hortobagy national park is the main draw, and the Tisza lake is within reach for a day. It is a half-day city with two or three good days around it.

How do I visit Hortobagy?

The village of Hortobagy is about forty kilometres west and reachable by train or bus, which is enough if you only want the famous nine-arched bridge, the herdsmen's museum and a short walk. The park itself is large and flat and its wildlife, especially the autumn crane migration, is best at dawn and dusk, so a car or an organised trip makes a real difference. Several operators run half-day trips from Debrecen in season.

How long is the train from Budapest?

Roughly two and a half to three hours on the direct intercity trains from Budapest Nyugati, which run through the day and are comfortable and cheap. Buy a seat reservation on the intercity services if you are travelling on a Friday, Sunday or a holiday weekend; the rest of the time you can turn up.

Are the Debrecen baths any good?

Yes, and they are the main reason many Hungarians come. The complex in the Nagyerdo has indoor medicinal pools and a large outdoor water park alongside, so it works for a serious soak and for children on the same ticket. It is busiest on summer weekends and in the Hungarian school holidays. Take a costume, flip-flops and a towel, and expect a locker system rather than a cabin.

Is English spoken?

In hotels, at the university and among younger people, generally yes. In shops, on the trams and among older people it thins out quickly, more so than in Budapest. Signs at the station are bilingual and the ticket machines have an English setting, but a translation app is worth having here in a way it is not in the capital.

Do I need a car in Debrecen?

Not in the city, where the tram runs the length of the centre and parking is paid and limited. A car is worth having only for Hortobagy, the Tisza lake and the villages of the plain, where buses are infrequent. If you are touring eastern Hungary by road, keep the car and park it at the hotel; if you are coming by train for the city alone, leave it alone.
