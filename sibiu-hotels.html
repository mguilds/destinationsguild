Where to Stay in Sibiu

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Sibiu is the handsomest town in Romania and it knows it. The chief city of the Transylvanian Saxons for most of eight hundred years, it kept its walls, its guild towers and three linked squares of tall ochre and pale-green houses with the eyebrow-shaped attic vents that give the roofs their watchful look. It was European Capital of Culture in 2007 and the restoration money from that period is still visible in every facade. It is smaller and quieter than Brasov, better preserved, and it sits at the foot of the Fagaras mountains with two of Europe’s great mountain roads starting within an hour of it.

Where to stay in Sibiu, area by area

The parts of Sibiu a visitor actually chooses between, and who each one suits.

Piata Mare and the Upper Town

Most centralPrettiestLevel walking

The Large Square is the centre of Sibiu and one of the finest squares in this part of Europe: the Brukenthal Palace along one side holding the national art collection, the Jesuit church, the baroque town hall and the Council Tower you can climb for the view over the roofs. The streets around it — Strada Nicolae Balcescu running south as the main promenade, and the lanes toward the Huet square and the Evangelical cathedral — are where most of the town’s hotels are. Flat, beautiful and five minutes from everything.

Piata Mica and the Liars’ Bridge

AtmosphericCafesQuieter than the big square

The smaller square just north, ringed by arcaded houses, with the iron Liars’ Bridge crossing the gap down to the Lower Town and the Council Tower joining it to Piata Mare. It holds the pharmacy museum and a run of terraces that catch the evening sun. Rooms here are small hotels and apartments in old merchant houses, often up stairs. It is our favourite part of Sibiu to stay in: central but a shade calmer than the main square, and the nicest view from a breakfast table in the country.

The Lower Town

CharacterfulBetter valueA climb home

Below the walls, reached down the Liars’ Bridge stairs or the Ocna street ramp, lies the old artisans’ quarter: low pastel houses on cobbled lanes along the Cibin, with the surviving stretch of town wall and towers above. It is quieter, noticeably cheaper and genuinely attractive in its own right, and it is where locals live rather than where visitors congregate. The cost is the climb back up to the squares, which is short but real.

The ASTRA museum side and Dumbrava

GreenFamily-friendlyShort bus in

South-west of the centre the town runs out into the Dumbrava woods, where the ASTRA open-air museum spreads several hundred relocated village buildings over a large wooded park with a lake — the best museum of its kind in the country and an easy half day. The streets this side are residential and leafy, with a bus or a twenty-minute walk into the squares. A good choice in summer and for anyone travelling with children.

Out toward the airport and the ring

Best valueParkingOrdinary streets

West of the centre toward the airport and the ring road is where the larger mid-range hotels, the easy parking and the lower prices are. It is unremarkable to look at and perfectly practical: ten minutes by taxi or twenty-five on foot from Piata Mare, and the obvious base if you are driving the Transfagarasan or the Transalpina and want to leave early without negotiating the old town. Getting around Sibiu

On foot

Sibiu is the easiest Romanian town to walk. The three squares and the main streets off them are flat, pedestrianised and close together, and you can see the whole historic centre in a morning without hurrying. The one gradient that matters is between the Upper and Lower Towns, taken by the Liars’ Bridge stairs, the Ocna ramp or the passage under the Council Tower. Cobbles throughout, so bring shoes rather than a wheeled case you care about.

Buses and taxis

A small city bus network reaches the outer districts, the ASTRA museum and the airport side; pay on board or with a card from a kiosk. Taxis are metered, cheap and easy to find at ranks around the centre, and ride-hailing works. Nothing within Sibiu is more than a few euros by taxi.

To and from the airport

Sibiu International is about five kilometres west of the centre, which is unusually convenient, with a handful of European connections and a city bus and taxis at the door. Ten minutes by car in normal traffic. It is a good way into Transylvania if the flight times suit, and it saves the long haul up from Bucharest.

The two mountain roads

Sibiu is the natural base for the Transfagarasan, the DN7C that climbs over the Fagaras range past Balea Lake, and for the Transalpina, the DN67C that runs even higher to the west. Both are summer roads: the high sections are shut by snow for much of the year and typically open from around late June or July until October, with the dates set each year by conditions. Check the current status with the roads authority or locally on the day before you set out, and never take a date off an old blog post.

Trains, and getting out of town

Trains run east to Brasov and on to Bucharest, and north to Cluj, all of them slow by Western European standards; the Brasov leg is the most useful. For the Saxon fortified churches, the Marginimea Sibiului villages along the mountain foot, Sighisoara and Alba Iulia, a car or an organised day is much better than the timetable. Bus departures are from the main bus station beside the railway station.

When to come

Sibiu is at six hundred metres with mountains behind it, and its calendar follows them. Late May to early July and September are the best weeks: warm days, cool nights, the squares full of tables and the high roads open or opening. Mid-July and August are the busiest, with the international theatre festival in June and a run of summer events filling rooms — check the festival calendar before booking, because Sibiu is small and sells out. October is lovely, with the Fagaras turning colour, though the mountain roads start closing. December brings one of the best Christmas markets in the country to Piata Mare and is worth a cold trip; January to March is cold, often snowy, cheap and very quiet.

Getting in: what applies, and what does not

Romania is in the European Union and, since 1 January 2025, a full member of the Schengen area — air and sea borders opened in March 2024 and the land borders followed on New Year’s Day 2025. Arriving from another Schengen country is now an internal journey with no routine passport check, which is a real change from older guidebooks. Romania is not in the euro area: the currency is the Romanian leu.

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen external border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave. Ninety days in any one hundred and eighty is the visa-free tourist allowance for American, Canadian, British and Australian visitors, and it is counted across the whole Schengen area rather than against Romania alone.

Sibiu — questions

Which part of Sibiu should I stay in?

Piata Mica, for our money: central, a little calmer than the Large Square, and the prettiest outlook in town. Piata Mare and the Upper Town streets if you want the big hotels and the flattest walking. The Lower Town for character and better prices, accepting the climb. The ASTRA side for trees and space. The airport and ring side for parking and value, which matters if you are driving the mountain roads.

How long do I need in Sibiu?

A full day covers the town: the three squares, the Brukenthal art museum, the Council Tower, the Evangelical cathedral, the walls and the Lower Town lanes. Two nights is the right length, and the second day is best spent on the ASTRA open-air museum or out among the Marginimea Sibiului villages. If you intend to drive the Transfagarasan or the Transalpina, give yourself a third night, because both are full days.

Sibiu or Brasov, if I can only do one?

Sibiu is the better-preserved and more beautiful town, and the calmer one. Brasov is livelier, has the mountain rising out of the back of it, and is far better placed for day trips — Bran, Rasnov, Peles and the Bucegi are all close. If you want one lovely town to sit in, Sibiu. If you want a base to explore from, Brasov. Most trips with a week in Transylvania take both, two hours apart by road.

What are the eyes on the roofs?

They are attic ventilation openings, built into the tiled roofs of the Saxon houses in a narrow eyebrow shape so that air could move through the lofts where grain and goods were stored. The effect, repeated across hundreds of roofs, is that the town appears to be watching you. It is the single most photographed thing in Sibiu and it is a piece of practical carpentry rather than a decoration.

When is the Transfagarasan open?

The high central section is a summer road and is closed by snow for a large part of the year, usually opening somewhere around late June or July and shutting again in October, with the exact dates set each year by the conditions and announced by the roads authority. The lower ends stay open longer. Do not plan a date around a figure you read on a travel blog; check the current status before you drive, and have a second plan if it has not opened.

Do I need a car?

Not for the town, which is small, flat and pedestrianised, and where old-town parking is awkward. For everything around it — the fortified churches, the Marginimea villages, Alba Iulia, Corvin Castle at Hunedoara, and above all the two mountain roads — a car transforms the trip. Hiring in Sibiu for two or three days is a good arrangement.

Is the ASTRA museum worth half a day?

Yes, and more than most open-air museums are. It holds several hundred genuine buildings — houses, mills, workshops, churches, an inn — moved here from villages all over Romania and set out along paths through woodland round a lake, so it reads as a landscape rather than a row of exhibits. Allow two to three hours minimum, wear walking shoes, and go in the morning in high summer.
