Gjirokaster

Where to Stay in Gjirokaster

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Gjirokaster is built down a mountainside in grey stone and slate, with a long castle along the ridge above it and the Drino valley spread out below — and it is the more dramatic of Albania’s two UNESCO stone towns, listed in 2005 with Berat added in 2008. The old quarters are tall fortified houses with slate roofs and walled courtyards, reached by lanes so steep that the town’s other name is the city of a thousand steps. Ismail Kadare was born here and so was Enver Hoxha, which tells you something about the place. One or two nights, and an hour and a half from Saranda.

The bazaar and the old quarters

The reason to comeBest for eatingSteep cobbles

The old bazaar is a short crossroads of stone-paved streets just below the castle, with the shops, the restaurants and most of the guesthouses that visitors want, and the old quarters of fortified houses — Palorto, Dunavat, Manalat — climbing away from it. Staying here puts you inside the town that is the point of coming and within a few minutes of the castle gate. The cost is gradient: these are the steepest streets in urban Albania, the stone is slippery when wet, and a car often cannot get to the door.

Palorto and the upper quarters

Best viewsQuietestSerious climb

Above the bazaar, Palorto holds the grandest of the old houses, several of them now guesthouses, with views across the Drino valley to the Lunxheria mountains that are the best in the town. It is quiet, it is beautiful and it is high: the walk down to dinner is easy and the walk back is not. Worth it for one or two nights if you are reasonably mobile; a mistake if you are not, and taxis cannot always reach the individual door.

The new town on the flat

Easiest accessLevel walkingBest value

Below the old town, around Rruga 18 Shtatori and the modern centre, the twentieth- and twenty-first-century town is flat, drivable and where most Gjirokastrites actually live. The hotels here are conventional, the prices are the lowest in town, and cars and buses can reach them. The old quarters are a stiff ten- or fifteen-minute walk or a very short taxi above you. This is the sensible base if steps are a problem or you are travelling with luggage and no help.

Along the Drino valley

For a carQuietShort drive in

The valley floor below the town carries the main road south toward Saranda and north toward Tepelene, and a scatter of guesthouses, agritourism places and small hotels sit along it among olive groves and vineyards. They are quiet, usually good value and entirely car-dependent. From here Antigonea’s ruins, the hot springs at Benja and the Blue Eye on the Saranda road are all easy, which the town itself cannot say.

Toward Libohove and the Blue Eye road

For touringCountrysideNeeds a car

East across the valley, Libohove sits under its own castle on the slope of the Lunxheria range, and the road south from the valley floor runs past the Blue Eye spring on the way to Saranda. A handful of small guesthouses out this way suit travellers who are touring by car and want a base between Gjirokaster and the coast rather than a night in either. Not a choice for anyone without a vehicle.

Getting around Gjirokaster

On foot, and the thousand steps

The name is not decoration. Gjirokaster is built down a mountainside and the old quarters are joined by stepped cobbled lanes at gradients that would be closed to the public in most countries. It is all short and none of it is dangerous, but a day in the town is genuinely strenuous, and the polished stone is treacherous in rain. Boots or trainers with grip, not smooth soles.

Getting up to the castle

The castle runs along the ridge above the bazaar and is reached by a walk up through the old streets, which is steep but short, or by taxi, which is cheap. Inside there is a long gun gallery of captured artillery, the remains of the prison, a stage built for the folklore festival, and an American reconnaissance aircraft that came down in 1957 and has been there ever since.

Buses and furgons

Coaches and furgons run from the new town down to Saranda in about an hour and a half, north to Tepelene, Berat and Tirana, and across to Korce. Departures are informal and seasonal rather than timetabled, so ask at your hotel on the day. Travelling on to Greece via Kakavia is also done from here.

Taxis

Cheap, and the thing that makes Gjirokaster manageable. Use one to get up to the castle, up to Palorto with a bag, or out to the Blue Eye and Antigonea. Agree the fare first.

Driving and parking

Driving in the old town is best avoided altogether: the lanes are narrow, stepped in places and often one-way in practice if not in law. Park in the new town or at one of the lots below the bazaar and walk up. If you have booked a guesthouse in the old quarters, ask in advance where to leave the car and how far the walk is.

When to come

Gjirokaster keeps the same calendar as Berat and for the same reason: a stone town on a south-facing slope is hard work in the heat. April to early June and September to October are the right months — warm, clear, and cool enough to climb. July and August are hot and the stone holds it; go up to the castle early and spend the middle of the day in the bazaar’s shade. November to March is mild, wet and very quiet, with snow on the Lunxheria range opposite and the town almost to yourself; the lanes are slippery, so grip matters. The national folklore festival in the castle is held every few years rather than annually and the schedule has not always held, so confirm from an Albanian source before building a trip around it.

Getting in: what applies, and what does not

Albania is not in the European Union and not in the Schengen area, so it runs its own border. United States citizens may enter as tourists without a visa and may stay up to one year without a residence permit; your passport must be valid for at least three months from arrival and carry a blank page for the stamp. Most other Western nationalities get ninety days visa-free — check your own. The United States rates Albania Level 2, exercise increased caution, in an advisory issued on 26 August 2026 on account of crime; read the current text at travel.state.gov, because the level and wording change.

If your trip also crosses into Greece, Italy or anywhere else in the Schengen area, the following applies to that crossing and not to Albania:

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Gjirokaster — questions

Which part of Gjirokaster should I stay in?

Stay in a guesthouse in the bazaar or the old quarters if you want to be inside the town that is the reason for coming, and can manage steep stepped lanes. Stay up in Palorto for the grandest houses and the best views, if a serious climb is no trouble. Stay in the new town on the flat for level ground, car access and the lowest prices. Stay in the Drino valley if you are touring by car and want countryside.

How steep is it, really?

Steeper than almost any town a visitor is likely to walk in Europe. The old quarters climb a mountainside on cobbles and steps, and the gradient between the bazaar and the upper houses is severe rather than merely noticeable. It is all short, it is all walkable for anyone reasonably fit, and taxis cover the worst of it cheaply. If stairs are a difficulty, sleep on the flat in the new town and take a taxi up — you will still see everything.

How long do I need?

One night and the better part of two days covers it: the castle and its gun gallery, the bazaar, one of the great houses — the Skenduli or the Zekate — the cold war tunnel under the town, and an evening meal in the bazaar. A second night lets you add the Blue Eye, Antigonea or the Benja hot springs, all of which are short drives. Gjirokaster also works well as a stop between Saranda and Berat.

Is Gjirokaster better than Berat?

They are a pair, and the UNESCO listing treats them as one site for good reason: see both if you can. Gjirokaster is the more dramatic — grey stone down a mountainside, a castle along the ridge, a real bazaar — and the harder work underfoot. Berat is gentler to walk, has the famous thousand-windows view and the inhabited castle quarter. For a single easier day, Berat; for the more striking town, this one.

Do I need a car?

Not for the town, which is reached easily by coach or furgon from Saranda, Berat and Tirana and walked or taxied once you are there. A car earns its keep for what is around it: the Blue Eye, Antigonea, Libohove, the Benja springs and the drive down the Drino. If you are staying in the valley rather than the town, a car is essential.

What should I not miss?

The castle, for the gun gallery and the view along the ridge; one of the fortified Ottoman houses open to visitors, the Skenduli and the Zekate being the usual two; the bazaar in the evening; and the cold war tunnel built under the town for the party leadership, which is a short guided walk and very strange.

Is the Blue Eye worth the trip?

Yes, and it is easy from here — it lies off the Gjirokaster to Saranda road, with a short walk in from the parking area. It is a karst spring that fills a very deep pool of extraordinary colour, and it is busy in high summer and lovely early in the morning. Access arrangements and any charge have changed more than once, so check locally on the day rather than relying on an older account.
