Fly Fishing British Columbia & Alaska — Steelhead, Salmon and the Fly-In Rivers

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Checked against the Alaska Department of Fish and Game, the National Park Service, the Government of British Columbia and Fisheries and Oceans Canada, 9 October 2026

This is the cold, wild end of North America: rivers that come down out of glaciers and spruce forest to the North Pacific, where every summer and autumn the salmon come home and the steelhead follow them. Some of it you can drive to from an Anchorage hotel. A great deal of it you cannot — you reach it by float plane, land on a lake, and fish water that may not see another angler all week.

Two things set this region apart from anywhere else in our atlas. First, the steelhead — a rainbow trout that has been to sea and come back big, chrome and stubborn, and which British Columbia now protects so carefully that every wild one must be put back. Second, the bears, who were here first and have right of way on the best pools. This page covers the Kenai, the Bristol Bay fly-in rivers, Yakutat’s Situk, the Skeena system, the Dean and Vancouver Island — with every licence, fee and rule read from the government’s own pages and dated. Where we could not confirm something, we say so.

Where these facts come from. Every licence, fee and rule on this page was read on 9 October 2026 from a government source: the Alaska Department of Fish and Game (its licence pages, its 2026 regulation booklets and its 2026 emergency orders); the National Park Service for Katmai and the Alagnak Wild River; the Government of British Columbia (its licence pages and the 2025–2027 Freshwater Fishing Regulations Synopsis); and Fisheries and Oceans Canada. Seasons come from the agencies where they publish them and from the lodges where they do not, and we say which each time. Alaska’s salmon rules are changed by emergency order, sometimes week by week — check the agency before you fish.

The Kenai River — Alaska you can drive to

Two and a half hours south of Anchorage on the Sterling Highway, and the most-fished river in the state.

The upper and middle Kenai, Cooper Landing to Skilak Lake

The Kenai leaves Kenai Lake at Cooper Landing a startling turquoise, the colour of glacial flour, and runs fast and clear through a canyon to Skilak Lake. This is the fly angler’s Kenai: big rainbow trout and Dolly Varden that follow the salmon up and feed on their eggs and, later, on the flesh of spent fish — which is why the local flies are beads and pale strips of “flesh” rather than anything with wings. Most anglers fish it from a guided drift boat; there is bank access at Cooper Landing and at the mouth of the Russian River, where the July sockeye run draws a famous, shoulder-to-shoulder crowd.

Season and what to expect: the stretch below Kenai Lake is closed from 1 May to 10 June to protect spawning rainbows. From mid-June the trout are on; sockeye fill the river in July; and ADF&G says coho (silvers) arrive “in late July, with more fish arriving early to mid-August through early October”. For the trout, the lodges call late August to October the best of it, when the fish are fat on salmon eggs.

Confirmed with the Alaska Department of Fish and Game, 9 October 2026 — the 2026 Kenai booklet for the river below Kenai Lake: “May 1-June 10: Closed to all sport fishing”; “Only one unbaited, single-hook, artificial lure or fly is allowed year-round”; rainbow trout “1 per day … must be less than 16 inches long”. On the Russian River: “June 11-August 20: Only artificial flies are allowed”. In 2026 the Russian River Sanctuary opened early by emergency order. Bag limits for sockeye change in season; check the current emergency orders.

The lower Kenai, Soldotna — and the king salmon that are not there

Below Skilak Lake the river grows wide and powerful as it runs past Soldotna to Cook Inlet. This was the river of the world-record king salmon, and for decades people came for nothing else. Today the Kenai’s kings are in trouble, and we are not going to sell you a king salmon trip here. What the lower river does offer is sockeye in July and silvers from August into the autumn, from the bank or a boat.

Confirmed with the Alaska Department of Fish and Game, 9 October 2026 — Emergency order, “Kenai River Late-Run King Salmon Sport Fishery is Closed”: in “the Kenai River drainage downstream of the outlet of Skilak Lake”, “effective 12:01 a.m. Saturday, June 20 through August 15, 2026 … Fishing for king salmon of any size, including catch-and-release, is closed.” A separate order closed the early run, 1 May to 19 June 2026, “including catch-and-release”. The 2026 booklet: “King salmon fishing is closed in 2026 by emergency order.” Silvers on the lower river from 1 September: “3 per day, 3 in possession”. King rules are set each year by emergency order. Look up the current year’s orders on ADF&G’s emergency order page before you plan around a king.

Bristol Bay and Katmai — the fly-in rivers

No roads. A jet to King Salmon, Iliamna or Dillingham, then a float plane every morning to a different river.

The Naknek, the Alagnak, the Kvichak and the Brooks

Bristol Bay is the largest wild sockeye salmon run on earth, and every one of its rivers carries rainbow trout that grow fat on what the salmon bring. The way most people fish it is the fly-out lodge: a week in a lodge on a lake or river, with a de Havilland Beaver or Otter on floats outside the door, and a different river each day depending on weather, water and where the fish are. The Naknek flows out past King Salmon; the Alagnak is a National Wild River; the Kvichak drains Iliamna Lake, the largest lake in Alaska; and the Brooks, inside Katmai National Park, is the short river where the bears stand at the falls catching sockeye in the photographs everyone has seen.

Season and what to expect: the lodges fish from June to the end of September. Rainbow King Lodge publishes a month-by-month guide — king salmon in June, sockeye from late June into July, silvers in August and September, and its best rainbow fishing from mid-August through September. Royal Wolf Lodge gives its 2027 season as 7 June to 2 October. It is the most expensive fishing on this page and among the most expensive in the world: a week runs to five figures.

Confirmed with the Alaska Department of Fish and Game, 9 October 2026 — the 2026 Bristol Bay booklet: on the Alagnak, “Only unbaited, single-hook, artificial lures or flies may be used” and rainbow trout “June 8-October 31: Catch-and-release fishing only. All rainbow trout caught must be released immediately.” On the Brooks: “June 8-October 31: Only unbaited, single-hook, artificial flies may be used.” On the Kvichak, “Only unbaited, single-hook, artificial lures or flies may be used year-round”, and upstream of Yellow Creek “June 8-September 30: Catch-and-release fishing only.” Kings: “an annual limit of 5 king salmon 20 inches or longer in Bristol Bay salt and fresh waters combined”. Much of the region closes to sport fishing from 10 April to 7 June for spawning rainbows. Each drainage has its own rules — your lodge will know them; read them yourself as well.

Fishing among bears — the Brooks River rules

On the Alaska Peninsula you will fish within sight of brown bears, often within a few yards. The guides manage it every day and it goes well when everyone follows the rules. At Brooks Camp the rules are written down, and they are worth reading even if you are going somewhere else.

Confirmed with the National Park Service, Katmai, 9 October 2026 — “At minimum, keep 50 yards between yourself and all bears.” “No lures can remain in the water when a bear is within 50 yards of you” — be ready to cut or break your line, and never let a bear take a fish from you. “Brooks River is catch and release only, except downstream of the bridge.” Fishing from the bridge is prohibited. Arriving at Brooks Camp, “you must attend a brief bear safety orientation at the visitor center.” On the Alagnak: “Anglers must abide by all State of Alaska fishing regulations.”

Yakutat and Kodiak — steelhead in spring, and a closed king season

Southeast Alaska’s best-known steelhead river, and an island where the kings are resting.

The Situk River, Yakutat

Yakutat is a small town on the Gulf of Alaska, with a jet service, a few lodges and, running through the forest behind it, the Situk — a short, clear river that carries one of the best steelhead runs in Alaska. You float it in a day with a guide, from the road at the top to the sea. It is also a sockeye and silver salmon river later in the year.

Season and what to expect: ADF&G says the steelhead fishery “takes place from April to June, with peak effort occurring in May”, and that “a small number steelhead also enter the Situk in the fall to overwinter.” Yakutat Lodge, which fishes it, calls the steelhead fishery catch-and-release by custom, and says it is “being rewarded with record runs”.

Confirmed with the Alaska Department of Fish and Game, 9 October 2026 — the 2026 Yakutat booklet for the Situk: “Only single hooks are allowed” and “Only unbaited, artificial lures or flies may be used year-round. The use of bait is prohibited.” King salmon: “Closed to king salmon fishing, unless opened by emergency order.” Southeast steelhead may be kept only at 36 inches or longer, with “a 2 fish annual limit, harvest record required.” Catch and release of Situk steelhead is a voluntary custom, not the law. We would follow it. Section dates on the Situk vary; read the Yakutat booklet.

Kodiak — the Karluk and the Ayakulik

Kodiak’s two famous rivers, the Karluk and the Ayakulik, are fly-in rivers on the south-west of the island known for king salmon, sockeye, silvers and steelhead. In 2026 their kings were closed.

Confirmed with the Alaska Department of Fish and Game, 9 October 2026 — emergency order “Ayakulik and Karluk Rivers Closed to King Salmon Fishing”, 1 April to 25 July 2026: “This closure prohibits sport fishing for king salmon including catch-and-release.” We could not verify a Kodiak fly-fishing lodge whose own site we could open and read this time, so we list none. We will look again.

The Skeena system — the steelhead capital of the world

The Bulkley, Morice, Kispiox, Babine and Sustut: the rivers where the whole two-handed steelhead culture grew up.

The Bulkley, the Morice, the Kispiox, the Babine and the Sustut

The Skeena drains a huge piece of northern British Columbia, and its tributaries carry summer steelhead that come in from the sea in summer and are fished as the leaves turn in September and October. The Bulkley, running past Smithers, is the most accessible, with a highway alongside much of it; its upper river is the Morice. The Kispiox, north of Hazelton, has a reputation for very large fish. The Babine and the Sustut are remote, fished mostly from lodges. Most anglers swing flies with a two-handed spey rod — it is the classic way to fish for steelhead.

Season and what to expect: the provincial regulations treat 1 September to 31 October as the peak: on the Bulkley, Morice, Kispiox, Babine and Sustut those are the dates when the river is a classified water and the steelhead stamp is mandatory. The lodges fish from late August into November. Nicholas Dean Outdoors offers Bulkley day trips “from late August through October”. Expect cold mornings, short days, and fish that are counted by the week rather than by the hour.

Confirmed with the Government of British Columbia, 9 October 2026 — the 2025–2027 Synopsis for Region 6 (Skeena): “Single barbless hook: must be used in all streams of Region 6, all year”; a bait ban in the Skeena and its tributaries year-round; and all wild steelhead must be released. On the Bulkley, Morice and Kispiox, “Angling prohibited for non-guided non-resident aliens on Saturdays and Sundays, Sept 1-Oct 31” (the same applies on the Babine below the fish counting weir). No angling from boats on the Morice, and on the Bulkley from the Morice down to Barrett, from 15 August to 31 December; on the Kispiox, “No angling from boats, no powered boats”. The Province’s Skeena steelhead update of 30 July 2026 estimated 16,700 steelhead past the Tyee test fishery and said “The fishery will proceed as normal.” The Province can still close sections in season. Check the in-season changes page before you go.

Worth knowing before you travel — the Province published a notice that the Gitxsan First Nation announced, on 22 May 2026, a fishing ban for the 2026 season across their traditional territory, which includes much of the upper Skeena. The same notice says: “Provincial angling licences for freshwater species remain valid and the fishing opportunities described in the B.C. Freshwater Fishing Synopsis … are open.” Ask your guide or lodge how they are working with it, and be a courteous visitor.

The Dean, the Thompson and Vancouver Island

One legendary river you need a lottery to fish alone, one that is closed, and one easy island.

The Dean River — summer steelhead and a draw

The Dean comes off the Coast Mountains into the sea at Kimsquit, on the central coast, and is reached only by air or boat — most anglers fly into Bella Coola first. Its summer steelhead arrive straight from salt water and are famously hot fish. It is a classified water, and for non-Canadians who want to fish its main section without a guide it is the only river in the province run by a lottery.

Season and what to expect: the draw covers dates between 21 June and 30 September. BC West Lodge, near the mouth, fishes a nine-week season from mid-June to the end of August.

Confirmed with the Government of British Columbia, 9 October 2026 — “Only non-resident alien anglers may apply for the Dean River draw.” It covers “the Class I – Main Section (from Crag Creek to signs 500 m upstream of the canyon) of the Dean River without a guide.” No draw is needed for the lower or upper sections, or for “Any part of the Dean River when using a licensed angling guide.” “Dates must fall between June 21 and September 30”, each choice “8 consecutive days or fewer”; “There is no application fee.” Today the page says: “The Dean River draw is closed for applications.” The page does not say how many licences are drawn or when the next draw opens. Check with the Province.

The Thompson — closed, and why we still mention it

The Thompson’s autumn steelhead were once the most famous in British Columbia. The run has fallen so far that the river below Kamloops Lake is closed through the steelhead season. We mention it so that nobody books a trip on an old article.

Confirmed with the Government of British Columbia, 9 October 2026 — the 2025–2027 Synopsis, Region 3: Thompson River “downstream of signs at Kamloops Lake outlet to the confluence with Fraser River”, “No Fishing Oct 1-May 31”; and in Region 3 “you must release: ALL STEELHEAD”.

Vancouver Island — the Campbell River

The easy way into British Columbia fishing: a ferry or a short flight from Vancouver, a town with hotels, and a river running through it. The Campbell River calls itself the salmon capital of the world, and in late summer and autumn it fills with pink, coho and chinook salmon; its upper section is fly fishing only. The island’s other famous river, the Cowichan, is a winter steelhead and brown trout river near Duncan.

Confirmed with the Government of British Columbia, 9 October 2026 — the 2025–2027 Synopsis, Region 1: on the Campbell, “fly fishing only year-round” from the John Hart Dam power station down to the power line about 200 m upstream of the Quinsam confluence, and “No Fishing between Elk Falls and John Hart Dam Power Station”. Single barbless hooks in all streams of the region, all year. Salmon in the river are managed by Fisheries and Oceans Canada, which sets limits by notice. If you fish the estuary or the sea, you need the federal tidal licence — see below.

When to go — a month-by-month sketch

Built from the agencies’ own dates where they give them, and the lodges’ where they do not. Fish do not read calendars; use it to choose the month, then ask the lodge.

Spring to autumn

April–May: Situk steelhead, peaking in May (ADF&G). Much of Bristol Bay and the upper Kenai are closed for spawning rainbows until early-to-mid June. Winter steelhead on Vancouver Island and the lower Skeena tributaries through to about April (lodges).

June: the Kenai below Kenai Lake reopens on 11 June; most Bristol Bay rainbow waters reopen on 8 June and the fly-out lodges start. The Russian River fly-only season begins 11 June. The Dean draw window opens 21 June.

July: sockeye everywhere — the Kenai and the Russian, and the Bristol Bay rivers. Dean River summer steelhead. Rainbows feeding behind the spawning salmon from late July.

August: silvers arrive on the Kenai and in Bristol Bay. Dean season ends at BC West at month’s end. The Skeena lodges open in late August.

September–October: the best of the year for Alaska rainbows (lodges), late silvers on the Kenai, and the peak of Skeena steelhead — 1 September to 31 October is the regulated season on the classified waters. Bristol Bay lodges close at the end of September or early October.

November: a few Skeena lodges fish on into November for the hardiest; the Kenai’s silver season runs to 30 November on the lower river.

Rods and flies — what the lodges will expect you to bring

This is big water with big fish. Bring more rod than you think, and follow the lodge’s list.

One rod for each job

Rainbows and Dolly Varden: a 7- or 8-weight single-hand rod, floating line and indicator, with plastic or painted beads that imitate a salmon egg pegged above a single hook, and pale “flesh” flies later in the season. In the early season and on the Bristol Bay streams, sculpins, leeches and mouse patterns, which the big rainbows hit on the surface.

Steelhead: a two-handed spey rod, usually 7- or 8-weight and twelve to thirteen and a half feet, with sink-tips and swung flies — Intruders, marabou and the old hair-wing patterns. A single-hand 8-weight works on smaller water.

Silvers and sockeye: an 8-weight with a sinking-tip line; pink and chartreuse streamers for silvers. Kings, where open, want a 10-weight.

Everywhere: single barbless hooks (the law in BC streams, and on most Alaskan fly-only water a single unbaited hook is required), chest waders and layers — this is cold water, even in July — a good rain jacket, and wading boots without felt if your lodge asks. A guide carries bear spray; know where it is.

A note on sources — this tackle advice is ours, written from general practice in the region, not from any agency. Each lodge’s own list is the one to follow.

Licences and the law, plainly

Two countries, two systems. Alaska sells you a licence and, for kings, a stamp. British Columbia adds surcharges and per-day licences for its best rivers.

Alaska — a licence, a king stamp and a harvest record

Every visiting angler aged 16 or over needs an Alaska sport fishing licence. If you fish for king salmon you also need a king salmon stamp, even if you release the fish. Buy both online from ADF&G before you go, print or save them on your phone, and record any king or steelhead you keep on the licence or harvest card straight away.

Confirmed with the Alaska Department of Fish and Game, 9 October 2026 — non-resident sport fishing licence: $15 for 1 day, $30 for 3 days, $45 for 7 days, $75 for 14 days, $100 for a year. Non-resident king salmon stamp: the same prices — $15, $30, $45, $75 and $100. “Non-residents under the age of 16 do not need a sport fishing license.” Buy online at the ADF&G store (adfg.alaska.gov/store). Anglers should “record your catch immediately in the field” and “carry your harvest record card with you while fishing.” The price page does not state which licence year it covers. King salmon limits for non-residents are set by region and often changed by emergency order in season; check the current orders for the water you are fishing.

British Columbia — a freshwater licence, surcharges and classified waters

A visitor from outside Canada — a “non-resident alien” in the Province’s words — buys a BC freshwater licence, then adds the steelhead conservation surcharge to fish for steelhead at all, the salmon conservation surcharge only if keeping salmon in fresh water, and a classified waters licence for each day on a classified river during its classified period. Guided anglers give their guide’s licence number when they buy. Sea fishing is a separate federal licence.

Confirmed with the Government of British Columbia, 9 October 2026 — freshwater licence for non-resident aliens: C$22.86 for one day, C$57.14 for eight days, C$91.44 for a year (the licence year runs 1 April to 31 March). Steelhead surcharge, C$68.57 for non-residents, required to fish for steelhead anywhere in BC “whether you keep or release the fish”. Non-tidal salmon surcharge, C$34.29: “A stamp is not required if you release all salmon caught.” Classified waters, non-residents: Class I C$45.72 a day, Class II C$22.86 a day, each licence for no more than 8 consecutive days; annual classified licences are not available to non-residents. “All fees are subject to change and exclude tax.” Freshwater licence sales moved to the Province’s WILD licensing system for the 2026–2027 season. Canadians from outside BC pay different rates; see the Province’s table.

Salt water in British Columbia — the federal tidal licence

A BC freshwater licence does not cover the sea, the estuaries or tidal river mouths. For those you need a B.C. Tidal Waters Sport Fishing Licence from Fisheries and Oceans Canada, plus its salmon conservation stamp to keep a salmon. Barbless hooks are required for all salmon fishing.

Confirmed with Fisheries and Oceans Canada, 9 October 2026 — “All recreational fishers must have a licence to harvest fish and shellfish in tidal (salt) waters in British Columbia.” “Barbless hooks are required for all salmon and sea-run trout fishing.” “Partially crimped barbs are not allowed; barbs must be crimped flat against the shaft.” DFO’s licence page shows two non-resident fee tables without saying which year each belongs to — a day licence of about C$8 and a year’s of about C$116 to C$121, before tax, plus a salmon stamp of about C$7. Check the price with DFO when you buy.

Where to base yourself

A fly-in week begins and ends in a town. On the Kenai and the Skeena, the town can be the whole trip.

The towns that serve each river

For the Kenai: fly into Anchorage, then drive the Sterling Highway. Cooper Landing is a string of lodges and cabins right on the upper river and the Russian; Soldotna is the full-service town on the lower river, with supermarkets, motels and fly shops.

For Bristol Bay: scheduled flights from Anchorage to King Salmon (the Naknek, Alagnak and Katmai), Iliamna (the Kvichak and Iliamna Lake) and Dillingham (the Nushagak and Wood-Tikchik country). Your lodge’s float plane meets you there. Plan a night in Anchorage either side — weather delays are normal.

For the Situk and Kodiak: Yakutat has a jet service from Anchorage and Juneau; Kodiak has flights from Anchorage and the state ferry.

For the Skeena: fly to Smithers for the Bulkley, Morice and Babine, or Terrace for the lower Skeena and the Kispiox road; both have direct flights from Vancouver. For the Dean, fly to Bella Coola. For the Campbell, Campbell River has its own airport and is a few hours’ drive from the Nanaimo ferry.

Travelling as a group? Cooper Landing, Soldotna and Smithers all have riverside cabins and houses to rent, and a party fishing with day guides often does better in one cabin than in four motel rooms. Our vacation-home guide covers what to ask before you pay.

Questions anglers ask us

Do I need a fishing licence to fly fish in Alaska?

Yes, if you are 16 or older and not an Alaska resident. The Alaska Department of Fish and Game sells non-resident sport fishing licences at 15 dollars for one day, 30 for three days, 45 for seven days, 75 for fourteen days and 100 for a year. If you fish for king salmon, even to release them, you also need a king salmon stamp, sold at the same five prices. Buy both online from the department's store before you travel, and record any king or steelhead you keep on your licence or harvest card straight away.

Can I fish for king salmon on the Kenai River?

Not in 2026. The Alaska Department of Fish and Game closed the Kenai's early run from 1 May to 19 June and its late run from 20 June to 15 August, and both orders closed king salmon fishing of any size, including catch and release. Kodiak's Karluk and Ayakulik were closed to kings as well. King seasons are set each year by emergency order, so check the department's current orders before planning a king trip. Rainbows, Dolly Varden, sockeye and silvers remain open on the Kenai under their own rules.

What licences does a visitor from outside Canada need to fish for steelhead in British Columbia?

Three things, from the Government of British Columbia. A freshwater licence, which for a non-resident alien is 22.86 Canadian dollars for one day, 57.14 for eight days or 91.44 for a year. The steelhead conservation surcharge, 68.57 dollars, which you need to fish for steelhead at all, whether you keep or release the fish. And on a classified water during its classified period, a classified waters licence for each day: 45.72 dollars a day for Class I and 22.86 for Class II. Fees exclude tax and are subject to change. All wild steelhead must be released.

Can I fish the Skeena rivers without a guide?

Yes, on most days. But on the Bulkley, the Morice and the Kispiox, and on the Babine below the fish counting weir, British Columbia's regulations say angling is prohibited for non-guided non-resident aliens on Saturdays and Sundays from 1 September to 31 October. On the Dean River, a non-Canadian who wants to fish the main section without a guide must apply for the Province's draw. Single barbless hooks are required in all streams in the Skeena region, and bait is banned in the Skeena and its tributaries.

Is it safe to fish around bears?

It is, if you follow the rules, and the guides deal with it every day. At Brooks River in Katmai, the National Park Service asks you to keep at least 50 yards from all bears, and says no lures can remain in the water when a bear is within 50 yards of you. Be ready to cut or break your line, and never let a bear take a fish from you. Everyone arriving at Brooks Camp must attend a short bear safety talk. Fish with a guide the first time.

When is the best time to go?

It depends on the fish. For Situk steelhead in Yakutat, the Alaska Department of Fish and Game says the fishery runs April to June and peaks in May. For sockeye, July. For silvers, August and September. For big Alaskan rainbows, the lodges say late August and September, when the trout are fat on salmon eggs. For Skeena steelhead, British Columbia's regulated season on the classified rivers is 1 September to 31 October. Bristol Bay fly-out lodges fish from June to the end of September.

Six honest gaps

Next year’s king salmon. Alaska sets king seasons by emergency order, often just before or during the run. Everything we say about kings is about 2026.

Skeena run timing as biology. No provincial page we read states when the steelhead run; we give the regulated season and the lodges’ months instead.

The Dean draw. The Province does not say how many licences it draws or when it next opens.

Kodiak lodges, and some famous names. We could not open and read a Kodiak fly-fishing lodge’s own site, and several well-known Alaska and Skeena lodges would not open for us. They are left out until we can.

Federal refuges. Parts of the Kenai run through the Kenai National Wildlife Refuge, and the Togiak refuge covers rivers west of Dillingham; we did not check the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service’s rules for them this time.
