Maribor

Where to Stay in Maribor

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Maribor is Slovenia’s second city and the capital of its wine country, sitting on the Drava with a forested mountain at its back and the Austrian border twenty minutes north. It is a working university city rather than a resort, which is the key to understanding it: the sights take a day, the prices are well below Ljubljana’s, and what makes a stay here worthwhile is the country around it — the Štajerska vineyards, the thermal spas, the oldest town in Slovenia half an hour downstream and the Pohorje trails and ski runs reached by cable car from the edge of town. The centre around the castle is where to sleep on a first visit; Lent along the river is the pleasantest in summer; and up on Pohorje is a different holiday altogether.

The centre: Glavni trg, Grajski trg and the castle

Most centralWalk everywhereLarger hotels

Maribor’s centre is a compact grid of squares between the castle and the river, and it is where a first visit should sleep. Glavni trg with its plague column, Grajski trg in front of the castle, the covered market and the cathedral are all within a few minutes of each other, the pedestrian streets between them hold most of the cafes and shops, and the main bus and railway stations are a ten-minute walk east. The hotels here are mid-sized and conventional rather than characterful, which usually means a lift, parking and a proper breakfast. Maribor is a university city rather than a resort, so the centre is busy on weekday evenings in term and noticeably quiet in August.

Lent and the Drava embankment

By the riverSummer festivalRestaurants

Lent is the old raftsmen’s quarter along the north bank of the Drava below the centre, and it is the pleasantest part of Maribor to be in on a warm evening: a wide embankment walk, terraces over the water, the Water Tower and the Judgement Tower at either end, and the Old Vine House with its ancient vine growing up the wall. The Lent festival fills it for a couple of weeks in late June and early July, which is the best and the loudest time to be here. Rooms are mostly apartments and small guesthouses. It is five minutes’ walk up into the centre, so you lose nothing by choosing the river.

Pohorje, up the hill

Skiing and walkingSpa hotelsCable car in

The Pohorje massif rises straight up behind the city, and the hotels on and below it are a different holiday from the ones in town: forest, walking and mountain-biking trails in summer, a modest ski area in winter with the World Cup slalom slope at the bottom of it, and several places with pools and spa facilities. The cable car from the southern edge of the city runs up to the lower station, so you can stay up here and still get into Maribor without a car, though the timetable thins in the evening and off season. This is the choice for a family, a walking week or a ski trip, not for a short sightseeing visit.

Ptuj, half an hour downstream

Oldest townCastleQuieter

Ptuj is the oldest town in Slovenia, about half an hour down the Drava, and it is the prettiest place in this corner of the country: a single steep street of painted houses rising to a castle above the river, Roman and medieval layers underneath it, and a thermal spa complex on the far bank. There are a handful of small hotels and guesthouses in and below the old town and rooms cost less than in Maribor. The Kurentovanje carnival before Lent is a genuine and very old festival and the one time the town is full; check the date for the year you are travelling, because it moves with Easter.

The spa towns and the wine road

Thermal spasVineyardsNeed a car

Eastern Slovenia is thermal-water country, and Rogaška Slatina is the best-known of its spa towns — a formal nineteenth-century resort of colonnades and drinking halls built around a mineral spring, about an hour south-west of Maribor. Around it, and north and east of Maribor through the Štajerska hills, runs the wine road, with cellars, farm guesthouses and osmice where growers sell their own wine and food. These are places to stay if you have a car and want several slow days; without one you will be stuck, because the bus network out here is built for schoolchildren rather than visitors.

Getting around

Maribor is flat, compact and easily walked: the centre, Lent and the embankment are one continuous stroll, and the main stations are ten minutes from Glavni trg on level pavement. City buses cover the suburbs and the foot of Pohorje, and the cable car climbs the mountain from the southern edge. Trains are the good way in and out — Ljubljana is roughly two hours, Vienna and Graz are reachable north over the border, and Zagreb lies south-east — and the station is the one in the centre, not a suburban halt. A car is not needed in the city and parking is metered and a little awkward, but it changes what is possible beyond it: Ptuj, the wine road, the spa towns and the Pohorje forest roads are all much easier with one, and the bus service to the villages is sparse. Maribor has its own small airport with very little scheduled traffic, so almost everyone flies into Ljubljana, Graz, Zagreb or Vienna and comes on by train or road. Slovenian motorways need a vignette; check whether a hire car already carries one.

When to come, and what it costs

Maribor has a continental climate and the pattern of a working city rather than a resort, which means the quietest and cheapest weeks are often the ones a visitor would most enjoy. Late May, June and September are the best: warm, long evenings on the Lent embankment, the hills walkable, everything open. July and August are hot and the city empties as its residents leave, so the centre can feel oddly deserted while the Lent festival at the turn of the month is the busiest fortnight of the year. September and early October are the vintage in the Štajerska vineyards, which is the best reason to come to this part of Slovenia at all, and the farm guesthouses are at their fullest and most interesting then. November is grey and damp. Winter brings skiing on Pohorje, the World Cup races in the first half of the season and cold, often foggy days in the valley, with the lowest room rates of the year in the city itself. Student term dates move midweek prices more than tourism does.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Slovenia is in the European Union, the Schengen area and the euro, so there is no passport control arriving from another Schengen country and a single check arriving from outside it. Maribor sits close to two borders and this is mostly useful news: Austria is about twenty minutes north, with Graz an hour away and Vienna reachable in a long morning, and both crossings are internal Schengen and ordinarily unmanned. Hungary lies east and is also inside Schengen. Croatia is south-east and has been an internal Schengen crossing since the start of 2023, although checks are sometimes reintroduced temporarily at particular crossings, so carry your passport. If you are flying home from Graz or Vienna rather than Ljubljana, which is often cheaper from this corner of the country, allow for the border in your timings even though you will probably not be stopped at it.

Staying in Maribor — questions

Is Maribor worth a stop?

For a first visit to Slovenia of under a week, probably not — Ljubljana, the lakes and the caves are the stronger trip, and Maribor is two hours the other way. For a second visit, or a trip built around wine, thermal spas or walking rather than sights, it is a good base and much better value than the west of the country. The city gives you a comfortable day: the castle and its museum, the market, the Old Vine, the embankment and a good dinner. What justifies several nights is what lies around it.

What is the Old Vine?

A vine growing up the front of a house on Vojasniska street in Lent, generally reckoned to be the oldest vine in the world still producing fruit — it is usually put at more than four hundred years old, and it survived the phylloxera that killed most of Europe’s vineyards. It still yields a small crop each year, bottled in tiny quantities and used mostly for official gifts. The Old Vine House beside it has a tasting room and an exhibition about Štajerska wine, and it is the sensible first stop if the wine country is why you came.

Can I visit the wine road without a car?

Not easily. The cellars and farm guesthouses are scattered through the hills north and east of the city and the rural buses are timed for schools and commuters, not for visitors. The practical answers are an organised tasting tour from Maribor, a taxi arranged for a half day, or picking one village, staying the night and walking between the growers around it. Do not plan to drive yourself between tastings: Slovenia enforces its drink-driving limits firmly and they are lower than many visitors expect.

How good is the skiing on Pohorje?

Modest in scale and very convenient in position. It is a low mountain, so the snow is less reliable than in the Julian Alps or across the Austrian border and the runs are mostly gentle, which makes it genuinely good for learners, children and a family that wants a short ski day rather than a week. Snowmaking covers the main pistes. The cable car from the city means you can sleep in Maribor and ski without a car. If you want serious Alpine skiing, go to Kranjska Gora or over into Austria instead, and check conditions before you travel either way.

Is Ptuj or Celje the better side trip?

Ptuj, for most people: it is smaller, prettier and more intact, half an hour away, and the castle and the one great street can be done in an easy afternoon. Celje, about an hour towards Ljubljana, has the larger castle — the biggest fortress in the country, on a hill above the town — and a good regional museum, and it makes a natural stop if you are driving between Maribor and the capital rather than a destination in itself. With a full day and a car you can see both.
