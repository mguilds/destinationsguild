Saint Lucia

Saint Lucia Travel Guide

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Saint Lucia is a volcanic island in the Windward chain, about 238 square miles of steep green ridges and some 180,000 people, with Martinique a short hop to the north and Saint Vincent to the south. Unlike the flat coral islands further east, almost all of it is mountain: a rainforest interior, a spine running down the middle, and on the south-west coast the two sharp peaks that put the island on every poster — Gros Piton and Petit Piton, rising straight out of the sea either side of the town of Soufrière and protected as a UNESCO World Heritage site since 2004. Independent since February 1979, English-speaking with Kwéyòl spoken everywhere alongside it, and the improbable birthplace of two Nobel laureates — Sir Arthur Lewis in economics and the poet Derek Walcott — it is the island people mean when they say the Caribbean is more than a beach.

What your money is worth in Saint Lucia
US $1 = 2.70 EC$
EC$ 10 = about US $3.70
Saint Lucia uses the Eastern Caribbean dollar, shared with Antigua, Grenada, Dominica, Saint Kitts and Nevis, Saint Vincent and Anguilla, and pegged to the US dollar at EC$2.70 — a rate that has held for many years, so no app is needed: a price of EC$27 is about ten US dollars. US dollars are accepted in most hotels, restaurants and taxis, but change often comes back in Eastern Caribbean dollars and paying in local money is usually very slightly better. Cards are fine in hotels, supermarkets and the larger restaurants; keep cash for minibuses, the market and small bars. A government tax and a service charge are normally added to hotel and restaurant bills, so the final total sits above the headline rate — ask what is included when you book rather than at check-out.

Why visit Saint Lucia

Mountains that come down to the sea, a volcano you can drive into, and an island that is scenery first and beach second.

The Pitons, and they are not oversold

Two volcanic plugs, Gros Piton at about 2,619 feet and Petit Piton at about 2,438 feet, standing straight up out of the water with the town of Soufrière between them. They are the reason the south-west coast is a UNESCO World Heritage site, they look exactly as they do in photographs, and they change character all day as the cloud comes and goes. You can climb the larger one with a guide, sail past them, or simply sit and look at them, which is what most people end up doing.

A drive-in volcano and water that steams

Soufrière sits inside the collapsed caldera of an old volcano, and at the Sulphur Springs you can drive almost to the vents: grey mud, sulphur smell, water boiling out of the ground, and warm mineral pools nearby that people bathe in. Add Diamond Falls and its botanical garden, the Toraille waterfall and a rainforest interior with trails through it, and the island gives you a week of land to look at, not only a sea to lie beside.

Two coasts with two different tempers

The Caribbean west coast is calm, deep and good for swimming and sailing, with bays rather than long strands and sand that runs from pale in the north to dark volcanic grey under the Pitons. The Atlantic east coast is wild, windy and largely undeveloped, with heavy surf and almost no hotels. Nearly everything a visitor does happens on the west, and the east is worth a day for the look of it.

Green figs, cocoa and Friday night

The national dish is green figs and saltfish — green bananas with salted cod — and bananas were the island’s living for most of the twentieth century. Cocoa is the other old estate crop, and several working estates in the Soufrière hills show you the pods, the fermentation and the chocolate at the end of it. Friday night belongs to the street party at Gros Islet, where the road closes, the grills come out and the whole north of the island eats standing up.

Saint Lucia at a glance

Money

The Eastern Caribbean dollar, pegged at EC$2.70 to the US dollar. Saint Lucia is not a budget island — the all-inclusive resorts are priced accordingly — but minibuses, local restaurants and the smaller guesthouses are reasonable, and the shoulder months take a real bite out of a room rate.

Language

English is the official language and is spoken everywhere. Alongside it almost everyone speaks Kwéyòl, a French-based creole, which is why so many place names on the map are French while the road signs are in English.

Getting around

Minibuses on the main routes, taxis with set zone fares, water taxis along the west coast, and hire cars for anyone who wants the south at their own pace. Driving is on the left and visitors need a temporary local permit. The roads are narrow, steep and slow — distances on the map understate the time by a long way.

Flying in

Hewanorra International at Vieux Fort in the south takes the long-haul flights, about ninety minutes’ drive from the northern hotels and about forty-five from Soufrière. George F. L. Charles airport at Castries takes regional flights and is minutes from the north coast hotels. Check which one your ticket says before you book a transfer.

Weather

Warm all year, roughly in the high seventies to high eighties Fahrenheit by day. The dry season runs from about mid-December to May, the wetter months from June to November, and the interior is far wetter than the coast at any time of year. Hurricane season is 1 June to 30 November.

How long to stay

A week is right for one base and a couple of excursions. Ten days lets you split the island between the north and Soufrière, which is the trip we would pick. Three or four nights is enough only if you choose one end and stay there.

North, west and south — which part of the island is which

Saint Lucia is small on the map and slow on the road. Where you sleep decides most of what you see.

The north: Rodney Bay, Gros Islet, Cap Estate

The busiest and easiest corner. Reduit Beach is the island’s best stretch of pale sand, the marina brings yachts and the restaurants that follow them, and Pigeon Island National Landmark sits at the end of a causeway with its old fort and two small beaches. This is where to stay if you want to walk to dinner and have a choice of it.

Castries and the hotel coast

The capital is a working harbour town with a covered market, the cruise berths at Pointe Seraphine and La Place Carenage, and very little designed for visitors — which is part of its appeal. Just north of it the coast road runs through Choc Bay and Labrelotte Bay, where several of the larger resorts sit on their own bays within fifteen minutes of town.

Soufrière and the Pitons

The south-west: the two peaks, the volcanic springs, the waterfalls, the cocoa estates and the dark-sand bays of Anse Chastanet and Anse Mamin. Hotels here are built into hillsides, the views are the best in the Caribbean and the steps are many. Evenings are quiet and dinner usually means your own hotel.

Marigot Bay and the middle coast

A narrow, almost hidden inlet halfway down the west coast, deep enough for yachts and sheltered enough to have been a hurricane refuge for centuries. It is a marina, a handful of places to eat and a very pretty half hour, and it makes a natural stop on the drive between the north and Soufrière.

Vieux Fort and the south

The flat, windy southern tip, with the international airport, the island’s kitesurfing beaches on the Atlantic side and far fewer hotels. Useful for an early flight and for anyone who sails or kites; not where most people spend a week.

The east coast

Open Atlantic, heavy surf, fishing villages and almost no tourism. The drive over from the west is a day out in itself, through the middle of the island or around the north. Bring a swimsuit for later, not for there.

Things to do in Saint Lucia

The island rewards a few proper days out more than a long list of attractions.

The Sulphur Springs and Diamond Falls

Half a day from Soufrière: the steaming vents and grey mud of the old volcano, the warm mineral baths, and a short drive away the Diamond Falls Botanical Gardens, where the mineral water has stained the rock behind the waterfall in bands of ochre and green. Both are ticketed and both are busy with cruise excursions in the middle of the day, so go early or late.

Climb Gros Piton, or walk Tet Paul

Gros Piton is climbed with a guide from the interpretive centre at Fond Gens Libre — several hours, steep, hot, and a real achievement at the top. If that is more than you want, the Tet Paul Nature Trail is a short guided walk on a hillside above the town with the classic view of both peaks, and the Edmund and Barre de l’Isle forest trails give you the rainforest interior.

Pigeon Island and the north

A national landmark joined to the mainland by a causeway, with the ruins of Fort Rodney on top of a hill, a twenty-minute climb, two small beaches and a view north to Martinique on a clear day. Combine it with Reduit Beach and, if it is a Friday, stay for the Gros Islet street party in the evening.

Sail the west coast

The classic day out: a boat from Rodney Bay or Castries down the calm Caribbean side, past Marigot Bay, with the Pitons coming into view ahead, a stop to snorkel and lunch ashore at Soufrière. It is also the least tiring way to see the south, since the road is slow and winding in both directions.

A cocoa estate

Working estates in the hills around Soufrière take visitors through the whole business — the pods on the tree, the fermenting boxes, the drying trays and the chocolate at the end. It is the most interesting hour on the island for anyone who likes to know how things are actually made.

Castries Market

Saturday is the day, and the covered market and the streets around it are the island at its most ordinary and most alive: fruit, root vegetables, spices, baskets, hot food. It is five minutes from the cruise berths and costs nothing to walk through.

When to go

A short version here; the full month-by-month account, with sea temperatures and what a room costs, is in our best time to visit Saint Lucia guide.

Mid-December to May — the dry season

The reliable half of the year: sunshine, lower humidity, a steady breeze and little rain. It is also the busiest and dearest, with February and March at the peak. The Saint Lucia Jazz and Arts Festival in May is the big fixture at the end of it.

June to November — the green season

Hotter, wetter and considerably cheaper, with heavy showers that pass rather than days of rain. Carnival falls in July and Creole Day at the end of October. This is also hurricane season, so take insurance that covers weather disruption.

The weeks we would pick

The first half of December, before the Christmas rates begin and after the worst of the storm season, and late April into May, when the weather is still dry, the sea is warm and the Jazz Festival is on. Both give you dry-season weather without peak prices.

Arriving by ship

Saint Lucia is a port of call on most eastern and southern Caribbean itineraries, and a good one.

Ships berth in Castries, either at Pointe Seraphine on the north side of the harbour, where the duty-free shopping is, or at La Place Carenage on the town side; both are a short walk or a shuttle from the market and the centre. A few smaller ships anchor off Soufrière instead and tender in, which is the lucky draw, since it puts you under the Pitons without the drive.

With a single day, choose one end of the island rather than both. South means Soufrière — the Sulphur Springs, a waterfall and the Pitons — which is about an hour and a quarter each way by road or rather more pleasantly by boat. North means Pigeon Island and a beach at Rodney Bay, twenty-five minutes away. Castries itself fills a couple of easy hours on foot if you would rather not travel at all. Agree the fare with any taxi before you get in, and leave more time to get back to the ship than the driver tells you — the coast road is slow and has no alternative.

Travelling in your later years

An honest assessment, because Saint Lucia is steeper than the brochures admit.

The good part: English is the language, the island is small, medical care in the north is reasonable by regional standards, and taxis and minibuses mean you never need to drive. Hotels are used to older guests and most will arrange airport transfers and excursions for you.

The part to plan around is the terrain. This is a mountainous island and a great many hotels — particularly the famous ones around Soufrière — are built down a hillside in tiers, with steps rather than lifts between the room and the beach and the restaurant. Golf buggies help but do not solve it. Before booking anywhere, ask three plain questions: how many steps are there between the room and the restaurant, is there a lift or a buggy, and how far is it to the sand. The flatter, easier ground is in the north around Rodney Bay and on the Castries hotel coast, and that is where we would send anyone who would rather not climb. The ninety-minute transfer from Hewanorra is also worth thinking about: a night in the south on arrival, or the regional airport at Castries, can turn a tiring arrival into an easy one.

Getting in

Straightforward for most visitors, with one or two things worth doing before you fly.

You need a passport valid for your stay and a return or onward ticket. Visitors from the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom and the European Union do not need a visa for an ordinary holiday, and stays of several weeks are normally granted on arrival; other nationalities should check with a Saint Lucian mission before travelling. Immigration and customs declarations have in recent years moved online, with the form to be completed shortly before arrival, and the arrangements have changed more than once — so check the current requirement with your airline or the Saint Lucian authorities when you book rather than trusting an address printed in a guide, including this one.

Departure tax is normally included in the price of an air ticket. If you intend to drive, you will need a temporary Saint Lucian driving permit, which the car hire companies issue on production of your home licence when you collect the car. Nothing about entering Saint Lucia requires an agent or a service: deal directly with the airline and the authorities.

Fishing here

Saint Lucia is blue-water fishing rather than flats fishing: the shelf drops away steeply off the west coast, and the charter boats out of Rodney Bay and Castries go after blue marlin, sailfish, wahoo, dorado and tuna within a few miles of the shore. What the island does not have is the shallow sand flats that the bonefish islands are built on — for that you want the Bahamas or the Caribbean’s flats proper, which is the subject of our guide to fishing the flats. Questions travellers ask about Saint Lucia

When is the best time to visit Saint Lucia?

Mid-December to May is the dry season: steadier sunshine, lower humidity and a reliable trade wind, and also the dearest half of the year. June to November is hotter and wetter, with heavy afternoon showers rather than all-day rain, and it is the Atlantic hurricane season, which runs from 1 June to 30 November. Our own picks are the two edges of the dry season - the first half of December, and late April into May, which also catches the Jazz and Arts Festival - when the weather is good and rooms cost noticeably less than in February.

How long does it take to get from the airport to the hotels?

Longer than people expect, and it is the one thing to plan for. Almost all long-haul flights land at Hewanorra International at Vieux Fort, on the southern tip of the island, while most of the hotels are in the north around Rodney Bay and Castries. That drive is about an hour and a half on a winding road. Soufriere and the Pitons are closer to Hewanorra, roughly forty-five minutes. Helicopter and boat transfers exist and cost accordingly. If you are flying in from elsewhere in the Caribbean you may land at George F. L. Charles airport in Castries instead, which is minutes from the northern hotels.

Should I stay in the north or near the Pitons?

The north - Rodney Bay, Gros Islet and the Castries coast - has the beaches, the restaurants, the marina, the supermarkets and the shortest taxi rides, and it is where most first visits are spent. Soufriere, in the south-west, has the Pitons on the doorstep, the rainforest, the waterfalls and the volcanic springs, and far less to do after dark. Many people split a week between the two, and that is what we would suggest if the trip is long enough. If you only have a few days and want the famous view, stay near Soufriere; if you want sand, a choice of dinner and company, stay in the north.

Do I need to hire a car in Saint Lucia?

Not necessarily, and plenty of visitors never do. Minibuses run the main routes cheaply, taxis are everywhere, fares are set by zone rather than by meter, and water taxis run along the west coast. Against that, the island is mountainous and the drive between the north and Soufriere is slow and twisting, driving is on the left, and visitors need a temporary local driving permit, which hire companies arrange when you collect the car. Hiring for two or three days to see the south is a sensible middle course; agree every taxi fare before you set off.

What money do they use?

The Eastern Caribbean dollar, shared with several neighbouring islands and pegged to the US dollar at EC$2.70, which makes the arithmetic easy enough: divide the local price by rather less than three. US dollars are accepted in most hotels, restaurants and taxis, though your change may come back in Eastern Caribbean dollars and you will usually do a shade better paying in local money. Cards work in hotels, supermarkets and the larger restaurants; carry cash for minibuses, market stalls and small bars. Expect a government tax and a service charge to be added to hotel and restaurant bills, so ask what is included when you book.

Can you climb the Pitons?

Gros Piton, the larger and further south of the two, has a marked trail and is climbed with a guide from the interpretive centre at Fond Gens Libre. It is a steep, hot scramble of several hours rather than a walk, and it asks for proper shoes, water and a reasonable head for rough ground. Petit Piton is the sharper spike and is not an ordinary hike - it is a near-vertical climb and is not something to attempt casually. For the view without the climb, the Tet Paul Nature Trail above Soufriere is short, shaded in places and gives you both Pitons in one frame.

Does Saint Lucia get hurricanes?

Yes. Unlike Barbados, which sits far out to the east, Saint Lucia is squarely in the Windward Islands and in the hurricane belt, and it has been hit: Hurricane Tomas in 2010 did severe damage to roads, water supplies and the banana crop. Most storms in most years pass without a direct strike, and the island's hotels are used to the season. If you travel between June and November, take travel insurance that covers weather disruption, book a room with some flexibility and watch the forecast in the week before you fly.

Is Saint Lucia a good island for a cruise day ashore?

It is one of the better ones, because the two things it is famous for are close to the harbour. Ships berth at Pointe Seraphine or La Place Carenage in Castries, both within walking distance of the market and the town. With one day, the honest choice is either the south-west - a boat or road trip to Soufriere for the Pitons, the Sulphur Springs and a waterfall - or the north, for Pigeon Island and a beach at Rodney Bay. Trying to do both in a port day is how people end up seeing a minibus window. If your ship is in on a Friday evening, the street party at Gros Islet is the local night out.
