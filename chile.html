Chile — Desert, Vineyards, Lakes & Patagonia

Chile Travel Guide — Where to Stay, Region by Region

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Chile is four countries wearing one flag. The Atacama in the north is the driest desert on earth; the centre holds Santiago, the vineyards and the painted port of Valparaíso; the south is lakes, volcanoes and rain; and the far south is Patagonia, with its glaciers, its wind and the towers of Torres del Paine. They are four thousand kilometres apart end to end, so the first decision is not which hotel but which Chile. Below is each region, where to sleep in it, and the honest word on altitude, season and distance that decides whether a trip works.

Why visit Chile

Chile is the long thin country on the western edge of South America — something over four thousand kilometres from the Peruvian border to Cape Horn, and rarely more than two hundred wide, with the Andes down one side and the Pacific down the other. That shape is the whole point of travelling here. The north is the driest desert on earth, where some weather stations have never recorded rain. The middle is Mediterranean: vineyards, a capital city under snow-capped mountains, and a coast of painted ports. The south is green, wet and volcanic, lake after lake with a cone standing over it. The far south breaks into fjords, glaciers and the granite towers of Patagonia, and keeps going until it runs out at the bottom of the inhabited world.

What this means in practice is that Chile is not a country you “do” in a week. Santiago to Punta Arenas is a three-and-a-half-hour flight; Santiago to the Atacama is two. Most visitors pick two of the four Chiles and fly between them, and they are right to. The country makes that easy: the roads are good, the internal flights are frequent, the water is safe to drink in the cities, and Chile is among the more orderly and better-organised places to travel in South America.

Chile at a glance

Capital

Santiago, in the central valley, home to roughly a third of the country’s people and the hub for nearly every domestic flight. Valparaíso, ninety minutes away on the coast, is the seat of the National Congress, which surprises visitors.

Money

The Chilean peso, written CLP and usually shown with a $ sign — which catches visitors out, since prices look alarming until you do the arithmetic. Cards are taken almost everywhere in the cities and widely in the south; carry cash for small towns, markets and park entry. Decline any card terminal that offers to charge you in your own currency.

The tax that visitors can avoid

Foreign visitors staying in Chilean hotels are normally exempt from the 19% sales tax when the room is invoiced and paid in US dollars with a foreign card or transfer, on production of a passport and the tourist card given on arrival. The hotel administers it, so ask at check-in rather than at check-out — it is a real saving and a surprising number of visitors miss it.

Language

Spanish, and Chilean Spanish has a reputation among other Spanish speakers for being the fastest and most idiomatic in the Americas. English is common in the better hotels, the tour agencies and the eastern districts of Santiago, and much less common elsewhere. A translation app earns its place here.

Getting about

Driving is on the right, the main highways are good and tolled, and hire cars are straightforward. Long-distance buses are comfortable and genuinely good. For the length of the country, fly: the domestic network out of Santiago is frequent and competitively priced, and a bus the length of Chile is a two-day commitment.

Power and plugs

220 volts at 50 Hz, with the round-pin European-style plug and the three-pin Chilean variant. Most modern chargers handle the voltage; bring the adaptor.

Seasons are upside down

Chile is in the southern hemisphere. December to February is high summer and the Chilean school holiday; June to August is winter, which is ski season near Santiago and closing season in much of Patagonia. Plan around that before anything else.

Earthquakes

Chile is one of the most seismically active countries on earth, and small tremors are a normal part of life. It is also, for that reason, one of the best prepared: building codes are strict and well enforced. Read the notice on the back of the hotel door when you arrive, and then put it out of your mind.

The four Chiles

The north — the Atacama

The driest desert on earth, and the clearest skies: salt flats with flamingos, geyser fields at dawn, lagoons the colour of antifreeze, and some of the world’s great observatories. Base yourself at San Pedro de Atacama and respect the altitude — the village is at about 2,400 metres and the best excursions go a good deal higher.

The centre — Santiago, the coast and the wine

Where most Chileans live and most visitors land. Santiago sits in a bowl under the Andes with ski fields an hour away in winter; the Casablanca, Maipo and Colchagua valleys make the wine; and Valparaíso climbs its hills above the Pacific an hour and a half from the capital.

The south — lakes, volcanoes and Chiloé

Green, rainy and German-settled, a chain of big lakes each with a volcano behind it, and the wooden churches and stilt houses of the island of Chiloé. Puerto Varas is the comfortable base; this is also the northern end of Chile’s famous trout water.

The far south — Patagonia

Fjords, icefields, glaciers calving into grey lakes, and the granite towers of Torres del Paine. Reached by air to Punta Arenas and road to Puerto Natales. Short season, extraordinary wind, and the landscape that puts Chile on most travellers’ lists in the first place.

And Rapa Nui, which is none of them

Easter Island lies some 3,500 kilometres out in the Pacific and is Chilean territory, reached by a five-and-a-half-hour flight from Santiago. It is a trip in its own right rather than a side trip, with its own rules on where visitors may go and a requirement to have accommodation arranged before you fly. Things to do in Chile

Stand under the towers

The base-of-the-towers walk in Torres del Paine is the country’s signature day, and an honest eight hours over rough ground. The viewpoints from the road and the catamaran across Lake Pehoe give much of the drama with none of the suffering.

Watch the desert at dawn

The El Tatio geyser field performs at sunrise because the cold makes the steam visible, which is why every tour leaves San Pedro at four in the morning. The Valle de la Luna at the other end of the day is the gentler companion piece.

Look up

Northern Chile has the clearest night skies that are easy to reach, and the observatories and small tour operators around the Elqui Valley and San Pedro run public stargazing. Choose a date away from the full moon — it makes more difference than anything else.

Drink the wine where it is made

Casablanca for white, Maipo and Colchagua for red, all within easy reach of Santiago, and most estates take visitors by appointment. Chile also makes pisco, and whether pisco is Chilean or Peruvian is a question best not settled at the table.

Take a boat into the fjords

From the glacier trips out of Puerto Natales to the long ferry down the coast from Puerto Montt, the water is how the far south was always travelled and still the best way to see it.

Eat at the market

Seafood is the national strength — congrio, machas, centolla from the far south, and a national devotion to the sea urchin. Santiago’s Mercado Central and the fish markets of the coastal towns are where to start.

Best time to visit & getting there

The short version: October to April suits most of the country, and the further south you are going, the more tightly you are tied to December, January and February. The Atacama in the north works all year. Central Chile is at its best in spring and autumn. Patagonia has a real season and a real off-season, and outside it a good deal simply closes. Our full month-by-month guide is the best time to visit Chile.

Nearly everyone arrives through Santiago’s international airport, which has direct flights from North America, Europe, Australasia and across South America, and is the hub for the domestic network. The internal flight to Calama for the Atacama, or to Punta Arenas for Patagonia, is the step most itineraries are built around.

Fishing here

Chile is one of the handful of places serious fly fishers organise a life around. From the spring creeks and lakes of the Araucanía and the Lake District down through Aysén to Patagonia, the rivers hold big brown and rainbow trout in water that is clear, cold and often empty of other anglers, and the southern season runs roughly from spring to autumn — our northern winter. Lodges and guides work out of the small towns rather than the cities; Puerto Varas and Puerto Natales are both sensible bases. Our full guide is fly fishing in Chile, and the whole shelf is in The Guild Fishing Library.

Travelling in your later years

Altitude is the one to plan around

This is the single most important thing on this page for an older traveller. San Pedro de Atacama sits at about 2,400 metres and its best excursions reach 4,300. That is high enough to matter for anyone with a heart or lung condition, and the trips start before dawn in the cold. Take medical advice before booking the high excursions, allow a full day to settle at village height first, and know that a guide will turn back with you without fuss.

The cities are easier than the countryside

Santiago’s metro is modern, with lifts at many though not all stations, and the newer hotels in the eastern districts are built to current accessibility standards. Valparaíso, by contrast, is forty-two hills of staircases and cobbles — stay in Viña del Mar and visit by train if stairs are a problem. Patagonia’s sights are largely reached on foot over rough ground, though the viewpoints, the boat trips and the lodge terraces give a great deal without a walk.

Distances, and how to soften them

The country is very long and the temptation is to see all of it. Two regions on a two-week trip, with flights rather than buses between them and three nights minimum in each, makes a far better holiday than four regions and a succession of early starts.

Health care and medicines

Private clinics in Santiago and the larger cities are good and used to foreign patients, and pharmacies are plentiful. Carry your prescriptions in their original packaging with a doctor’s letter, and take out travel insurance that covers the altitude excursions and any trekking you intend — standard policies sometimes exclude both, and a rescue in Patagonia is not a cheap thing.

Getting in: what to expect at the border

Visitors from North America, the United Kingdom, the European Union, Australia and New Zealand normally enter Chile for tourism without arranging a visa in advance, and are given a tourist card — the Tarjeta Única Migratoria — on arrival. Keep it with your passport: you surrender it when you leave, and you will be asked for it at hotels in order to claim the tax exemption described above. The length of stay granted is set by the immigration authorities and is stamped or recorded on arrival, and the rules change from time to time, so check the current position with Chile’s immigration service or your nearest Chilean consulate before you travel rather than relying on what a guide of any kind, ours included, said last year.

The border check that actually catches visitors out is agricultural. Chile is an island in farming terms — desert to the north, ice to the south, the Andes to the east — and it defends that fiercely. You must declare all food, plant and animal products on the form you are given, including the apple in your bag and the sandwich from the plane. The sniffer dogs at Santiago airport are very good, the fines for an undeclared item are substantial, and declaring something costs you nothing but the time it takes to hand it over. The same inspection applies at the land borders with Argentina and Peru, and between the mainland and Rapa Nui.

Chile — questions

How long do I need in Chile?

Two weeks is the comfortable minimum for two of the four regions — say Santiago and the coast, then Patagonia, or Santiago and the Atacama. Ten days works for one region with the capital attached. Chile is more than four thousand kilometres long, so the mistake visitors regret is trying to see the desert, the lakes and Patagonia in one short trip and spending the holiday in airports.

Is Chile expensive?

By South American standards, yes; by North American or Western European standards, generally no, with Patagonia the sharp exception. Hotels in Torres del Paine in January are priced like a European capital in high season, and the all-inclusive lodges are a different order again. Santiago, the coast and the Lake District are good value. We do not print prices that go stale — the Browse links on these pages open live rates for your dates.

Is Chile safe?

Chile is one of the safer countries in South America and most visits are entirely uneventful. The real risk to a traveller is opportunistic theft rather than violence, and it concentrates in known places: central Santiago, the port and bus terminal areas of Valparaíso, crowded transport, and anywhere an obvious camera is on show. Demonstrations in central Santiago can close streets and the metro at short notice and are best avoided rather than watched. Natural hazards — earthquakes, the odd volcanic alert, and serious wind in the south — are managed well, and local instructions are worth following.

When is the best time to go?

It depends which Chile you mean, which is why we wrote a separate guide. The desert in the north is good all year. Central Chile is loveliest in spring, from September to November, and in autumn from March to May. The Lake District and Patagonia want the southern summer, December to February, with November and March the quieter shoulders. Chilean families holiday in January and February, so book early for those two months anywhere in the south.

Can I drink the water?

Yes, in Santiago and the main cities and towns the tap water is treated and safe. It is heavily mineralised in parts of the north, which some visitors find upsetting to the stomach simply because it is different from home, so bottled water is a reasonable precaution in the Atacama. In remote areas and small settlements, ask.

Do I need Spanish?

You will manage without it in hotels, agencies and the eastern districts of Santiago, and you will struggle without it in taxis, small restaurants, bus stations and anywhere off the visitor track. Chilean Spanish is fast and idiomatic even for fluent speakers. A translation app, a phrasebook and the courtesy of trying will carry you a long way; Chileans are patient with the attempt.
