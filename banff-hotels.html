Banff and Lake Louise

Where to Stay in Banff and Lake Louise

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Banff is the oldest national park in Canada and the busiest, and the part of the country most visitors have actually seen pictures of: glacial lakes the colour of antifreeze, mountains straight out of the valley floor, and a small Victorian resort town in the middle of it. It is also the place where booking badly costs you most. There are only four sensible bases, the whole corridor fills up months ahead for July and August, and the park now runs parts of itself on shuttles and timed entry, so the where and the when are tied together here more than anywhere else in this library.

Banff townsite — the obvious base, and the busiest

Most choiceWalkableDearest in summer

A small town of a few thousand people inside the national park, with one main street of shops, outfitters and restaurants, the Bow River and its falls at the bottom, the hot springs and the gondola up Sulphur Mountain above, and Lake Minnewanka and the Vermilion Lakes a short drive away. It has by far the widest choice of rooms, from hostels to grand hotels, and you can manage here without a car because the local buses reach the main trailheads and viewpoints. The drawbacks are price and crowds in July and August, when the main street is very busy indeed, and the fact that you are paying park rates for everything.

Lake Louise — at the lakes, and nearly empty at night

The lakesEarliest startsBook far ahead

Forty-five minutes north-west of Banff, the hamlet of Lake Louise is a handful of large hotels, a hostel, a small shopping plaza and not much else, close to the two most famous lakes in the country — Lake Louise itself and Moraine Lake in the next valley. The reason to stay is simple and good: you can be at the lakeshore at dawn, before the day visitors and the shuttles, which is when it is at its best. In exchange there is almost nothing to do in the evening, the choice of dinner is small and expensive, and the rooms sell out further ahead than anywhere else in the Rockies.

Canmore — just outside the gate, and noticeably cheaper

Best valueNo park pass to stayTwenty minutes out

Twenty minutes east of Banff and just outside the park boundary, Canmore is a real town rather than a resort: more shops and ordinary restaurants, a proper grocery, the Three Sisters peaks over it, good trails of its own and room rates well below the park. You do not need a park pass simply to stay here, which is a small saving and a simplification, and it is closer to Calgary. The trade-off is that you are commuting: twenty minutes to Banff, over an hour to Lake Louise, with the parking at the famous lakes already difficult by mid-morning.

Jasper and the north end — a separate trip, not a day out

QuietestIcefields ParkwayFour hours away

Jasper is the other great Rockies park, three and a half to four hours north of Banff along the Icefields Parkway, which is itself one of the finest drives anywhere. It is quieter, wilder and cheaper than Banff, with the Columbia Icefield about halfway up, Maligne Lake and a dark-sky preserve. Treat it as a second base with two or three nights of its own rather than a long day from Banff: the drive is slow in the best way, with stops every few kilometres, and it is genuinely unsafe to rush it in poor weather.

Getting around

Two practical things first. Everyone in the park, including people just driving through and stopping, needs a Parks Canada pass — a daily one or an annual one — bought online or at the gates; we do not print the price because it is reviewed each year. And the busiest places are now managed rather than open: personal vehicles are not permitted on the Moraine Lake road, which is reached by Parks Canada shuttle, a commercial bus or a bicycle, and the Lake Louise lakeshore parking fills before dawn in summer. Check what Parks Canada is running for your dates before you set out, because the arrangements are reviewed from season to season.

Within the valley, Roam public transit runs between Canmore, Banff, the hot springs, the gondola, Lake Minnewanka and Lake Louise, frequently in summer and less so in winter, and it is a sensible way to avoid the parking problem altogether. Most visitors arrive from Calgary airport, which is about ninety minutes to two hours away by road with regular scheduled coaches, and hire a car there. There is no passenger rail service to Banff on a scheduled basis.

If you are driving between November and April, take the conditions seriously: winter tyres or approved all-season tyres are required on many mountain highways in the cold months, hire cars in Calgary are not always fitted with them by default, and the Icefields Parkway has long stretches with no fuel, no phone signal and no services. Ask the hire desk explicitly what the car is shod with.

When to come

July and August are warm, long and entirely reliable, with every trail, gondola, boat and shuttle running — and they are also the most crowded and expensive weeks of the year anywhere in Canada. If you come then, book months ahead, start your days early and use the shuttles. Late June gets you most of the programme with slightly fewer people and a little more snow on the high trails.

September is the pick. The weather usually holds, the coach traffic falls away after the Labour Day weekend, the lakes keep their colour, the larches turn gold in the last fortnight of the month in the high valleys, and the wildlife is active. Winter, December to March, is a real season in its own right: three ski areas in the park, the frozen lakes with skating cleared on Lake Louise, and the town at a quarter of its summer pitch. May and the first half of June, and October, are the cheap in-between weeks, with many high trails still or already under snow.

Whatever month you come, treat the wildlife rules as real. Black bears and grizzlies both live here, elk are dangerous in the autumn rut and in spring when they have calves, the trailhead warning boards are current, and bear spray is sold and rented in the town. Getting in

Most visitors from visa-exempt countries arriving in Canada by air need an Electronic Travel Authorization, an eTA, applied for online before they fly and tied to the passport they travel on. Citizens of the United States do not need one but do need proof of citizenship. Nationals of visa-required countries need a visitor visa instead. An eTA is not required if you arrive by land or by sea. We do not print the fees here because they are revised from time to time — read them off the official pages at canada.ca when you book, and apply there rather than through an agent charging a mark-up for the same form.

Two Canada-specific points worth checking well before you travel: past criminal convictions, including an old drink-driving conviction, can make a traveller inadmissible, and there is a formal process for it that is not quick; and taking cannabis across the border in either direction is a federal offence whatever is legal at home. Banff and Lake Louise hotels FAQ

Do I need a park pass?

Yes, if you are inside Banff or Jasper National Park at all, including simply driving the highway and stopping at viewpoints. A daily pass or an annual discovery pass is bought online or at the park gates, and the annual one pays for itself in about a week. The one exception worth knowing is that staying in Canmore, which is outside the boundary, does not by itself need a pass — you buy one on the day you go into the park.

Can I drive to Moraine Lake?

No. Personal vehicles are not permitted on the Moraine Lake road, and have not been for several seasons. You reach it by Parks Canada shuttle, by a commercial bus or shuttle service, by bicycle, or on a guided trip. The shuttles are booked online and the early slots go first. Lake Louise itself can still be driven to, but its car parks fill before sunrise in July and August, so the shuttle is usually the better plan there too.

Banff, Lake Louise or Canmore?

Banff townsite for a first visit: the most choice of rooms and restaurants, walkable, and well served by the local buses. Lake Louise if seeing the lakes at dawn matters more to you than having somewhere to eat, and you have booked a long way ahead. Canmore to save real money, with a car and the willingness to commute twenty minutes each way. Jasper is a separate base rather than an alternative.

How many days do the Rockies need?

Three nights in the Banff and Lake Louise corridor is the minimum that does not feel rushed, and four or five is comfortable. If you want Jasper and the Icefields Parkway as well, add two or three nights at the north end and drive it slowly in one direction rather than there and back in a day.

How careful do I need to be about bears?

Careful, and the rules are simple. Keep a long distance and never approach for a photograph, never leave food, rubbish or scented things in a tent or in the open, make noise on blind corners of trails, carry bear spray where the park advises it and know how to use it, and read the trailhead boards, which are current and sometimes close a trail outright. Elk in the autumn rut and cow elk with calves in spring are statistically the more common problem in the town itself.
