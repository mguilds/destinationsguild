Before you go

This is not an "attraction." Know that, and the rest of the visit takes care of itself.

Most travel websites list Arlington National Cemetery alongside theme parks and shopping streets. We don't. Arlington is an active military cemetery, and funerals with full military honors take place here most weekdays. The people walking beside you on the path may be on their way to bury a husband, a daughter or a friend.

That is exactly why it is worth the trip. More than 400,000 service members, veterans and their families rest on these hills above the Potomac River, from every war the United States has fought. Established in 1864 on the grounds of the Arlington estate, the cemetery holds presidents, astronauts, Medal of Honor recipients and thousands of men and women whose names only their families remember. Walking the rows of white headstones is one of the most moving things you can do in the Washington area.

Give yourself at least half a day. The grounds are large and hilly, the walk from the Welcome Center to the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier alone takes the better part of half an hour on foot, and you will not want to rush.

The Changing of the Guard at the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier

The ceremony most visitors come to see — and the one that stays with them.

The Tomb of the Unknown Soldier has been guarded every minute of every day, in every kind of weather, since 1937. The sentinels are volunteers from the 3rd U.S. Infantry Regiment, known as The Old Guard, the oldest active-duty infantry unit in the Army. Between ceremonies a single sentinel walks the mat in front of the Tomb: 21 steps, a pause of 21 seconds, and back again.

Changing of the Guard times: from October 1 through March 31 the guard is changed every hour, on the hour. From April 1 through September 30 it is changed every half hour. The guard continues through the night after the cemetery closes to visitors, out of public view.

The relief commander steps onto the plaza, announces the ceremony, and carefully inspects the incoming sentinel's rifle before the guard is passed. Visitors are asked to stand and remain silent for the entire ceremony. Arrive 15 minutes early in spring and summer to find a place on the steps of the Memorial Amphitheater plaza.

The Tomb itself holds unknown service members from World War I, World War II and the Korean War. The Vietnam crypt has stood empty since 1998, when DNA testing identified the serviceman buried there; it now honors all who remain missing from the Vietnam War. The Tomb was first dedicated on November 11, 1921, and it remains the focus of the national observances held here each Memorial Day and Veterans Day.

What to see at Arlington National Cemetery

Six places to build your visit around.

The heart of the cemetery

Tomb of the Unknown Soldier

The guarded Tomb and the Changing of the Guard. Plan the rest of the visit around the ceremony times above.

Presidential gravesite

John F. Kennedy Gravesite & Eternal Flame

President Kennedy rests beside Jacqueline Kennedy Onassis beneath the Eternal Flame, with Robert F. Kennedy and Edward Kennedy nearby. Quiet, simple and deeply affecting.

History on the hilltop

Arlington House

The columned mansion above the Kennedy gravesite, managed by the National Park Service, with one of the finest views over the Potomac to the Lincoln Memorial and the Capitol.

The recent wars

Section 60

Where many who fell in Iraq and Afghanistan are buried. You will often find families visiting here. Walk quietly and give them room.

Ceremonies & observances

Memorial Amphitheater

The marble amphitheater beside the Tomb, home to the national Memorial Day and Veterans Day ceremonies, with a small display room of artifacts.

Memorials across the grounds

Challenger, Columbia & USS Maine

Memorials to the crews of the space shuttles Challenger and Columbia, and the mast of the USS Maine, stand within easy reach of the Amphitheater.

Looking for someone in particular? Download the free ANC Explorer app before you go, or use the kiosks in the Welcome Center. It finds a gravesite by name, shows a photo of the headstone, and gives walking directions.

How to get to Arlington National Cemetery

By Metro (easiest)

Take the Blue Line to Arlington Cemetery station. The main entrance at 1 Memorial Avenue is about a 5-minute walk. From Rosslyn station it is about 15 minutes on foot to the Ord and Weitzel Gate.

No REAL ID is needed if you arrive on foot or by Metro — you are screened at the Welcome Center.

By car

Visitors park in the paid garage at the Welcome Center. Accessible parking spaces are on the garage's top level. Private cars are generally not allowed to drive through the cemetery, except for funeral attendees and holders of a gravesite-visit pass.

Adults entering by vehicle need REAL ID-compliant identification.

Arlington National Cemetery accessibility

The grounds are steep in places. Here is the help that is available.

Free tram for visitors with disabilities. Visitors with a valid disability placard or proof of disability may ride the interpretive tram at no cost, with one companion.

Free gravesite shuttle. Families visiting a particular grave can ask at the Welcome Center ticket window for the free shuttle to that gravesite.

Wheelchairs. The cemetery does not rent wheelchairs, so bring your own or rent one in the Washington area before your visit, and come with someone who can help on the hills.

Other help. Sign language interpretation is available with at least three days' notice, and assisted listening devices are available on request. The cemetery's accessibility office can be reached at (703) 692-6202.

How to visit respectfully

Silence at the Tomb

Stand and stay silent throughout the Changing of the Guard. Sentinels will correct visitors who don't.

Give way to funerals

If you come upon a funeral or procession, stop, keep your distance and wait. Do not photograph mourners.

Stay on the paths

Walk on roads and paths, and never sit, lean or stand on headstones or monuments.

Photography

Personal photos are welcome. Never photograph a funeral or grieving family up close, and keep the camera still and silent during the Changing of the Guard.

Dress and voice

Dress as you would for a memorial service, keep voices low, and wear good walking shoes.

Arlington National Cemetery FAQ

Is Arlington National Cemetery free to visit?

Yes. There is no charge to enter Arlington National Cemetery. Only the optional narrated tour tram costs money, and it is free for visitors with a disability and one companion.

What are Arlington National Cemetery's hours?

The cemetery is open every day of the year from 8 a.m. to 5 p.m. Check the official website before you travel, as hours can change for holidays and special events.

What time is the Changing of the Guard at Arlington?

From October 1 through March 31 the guard at the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier is changed every hour on the hour. From April 1 through September 30 it is changed every half hour.

How do I get to Arlington National Cemetery?

The easiest way is the Metro Blue Line to Arlington Cemetery station, about a 5-minute walk from the main entrance. Drivers park in the paid garage at the Welcome Center.

Do I need ID to visit Arlington National Cemetery?

If you arrive on foot, by Metro or rideshare, or park in the Welcome Center garage, you go through standard screening and do not need REAL ID. Adults over 18 entering by vehicle must show REAL ID-compliant identification.

Can you take photos at Arlington National Cemetery?

Yes, personal photography is welcome. Please do not photograph funerals or mourners up close, and stay silent and still during the Changing of the Guard. Commercial photography needs the cemetery's permission.

How long should I spend at Arlington National Cemetery?

Allow at least half a day to see the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier, the Kennedy gravesite and Arlington House without rushing. The grounds are large and hilly.

How do I find a specific grave at Arlington?

Use the free ANC Explorer app or the kiosks in the Welcome Center. They find a gravesite by name, show the headstone and give directions.
