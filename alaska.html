A short history

Alaska has been home to Indigenous peoples — Iñupiat, Yupik, Aleut, Tlingit, Haida and others — for many thousands of years, with cultures deeply adapted to sea, ice and forest. Russian fur traders arrived in the 1700s, founding settlements before the United States purchased the territory from Russia in 1867 for about two cents an acre, a deal once mocked as 'Seward's Folly.'

Gold rushes in the late 1800s and early 1900s, including the Klondike rush that also transformed Canada's Yukon, drew prospectors north and built towns like Skagway and Nome. Alaska became the 49th U.S. state in 1959, and the 1968 discovery of North Slope oil, followed by the Trans-Alaska Pipeline, reshaped its economy and infrastructure.

Today fishing, oil, tourism and the military anchor the economy. Alaska Native corporations play an outsized economic and cultural role, and cruise-based tourism through the Inside Passage has made glaciers, fjords and wildlife-watching central to how the world experiences the state.

When to go

June–August for long days and open roads; May and September are quieter and cheaper.

Winter (Dec–Feb)

Dark, cold and snowy, with prime aurora viewing away from coastal cloud cover.

Spring (Mar–May)

Snow lingers in the mountains as days lengthen quickly; a quieter, cheaper shoulder season.

Summer (Jun–Aug)

Long daylight, open roads and peak wildlife and cruise season, especially July.

Autumn (Sep–Nov)

Fall colour arrives fast, crowds thin, and early aurora and snow return by October.
