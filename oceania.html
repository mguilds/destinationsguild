The Guild's chapter on Oceania

Oceania is the long haul: the trip that costs you a day getting there and repays it with three easy, English-speaking weeks at the far end. Nobody goes to Australia for a long weekend, which is rather the point — it is a destination for travellers with time, and often the trip people take when they finally have it. The Guild's chapter on Oceania is a single book today, Australia, and we will not pretend to more; New Zealand and the Pacific islands are still to be written. What is here is the country most American travellers begin with anyway, covered properly.

The countries on the shelf

One country book so far — Australia — with its city guides and a best-time-to-go page.

AustraliaBOOK 1

Australia is roughly the size of the lower forty-eight with about the population of Texas, which is worth holding in mind before anyone plans to ‘see it all’ in ten days. Sydney and Melbourne make the natural first pair — harbour, beaches and the Opera House in one; laneways, coffee and galleries in the other — with about ninety minutes of flying between them. The seasons are reversed, so our winter is their summer, and every visitor needs an Electronic Travel Authority or a visa approved before boarding.

When to go

Australia is wide enough to hold several climates at once, so the honest answer to ‘when should I go’ depends on whether you are heading for the southern cities or the tropical north. Spring and autumn suit almost everybody.

September to November — spring

The Guild's first choice for a general trip. Sydney and Melbourne are warm without being hot, the jacarandas are out in Sydney in November, the Red Centre is comfortable for walking at Uluru, and the tropical north is drying out after the wet. Airfares from the United States sit below the December peak.

December to February — the southern summer

Beach season in the south, and the busiest and most expensive weeks of the year around Christmas and January school holidays. The tropical north — Cairns, Darwin, the Kimberley — is in its wet season then, which brings heat, humidity, monsoon rain and the risk of cyclones. Box jellyfish make unprotected swimming inadvisable on tropical Queensland beaches through these months.

March to May — autumn

The other sweet spot, and often the better value one. The southern cities keep their warmth into April, the crowds have thinned, and by May the north has switched over to its dry season. Melbourne is particularly good in this stretch, and the Great Ocean Road is at its most pleasant to drive.

June to August — the dry winter

Cool and often grey in Sydney and Melbourne, and genuinely cold in Tasmania and the alpine country, where the ski fields open. This is, however, the ideal season for everything north of the tropic: the Great Barrier Reef, Kakadu, Darwin and the Kimberley are dry, clear and comfortable, and it is the best window for the Red Centre too.

When is the best time to visit Australia?

September to November and March to May are the two sweet spots for a general trip: mild weather in Sydney and Melbourne, comfortable conditions in the Red Centre, and fewer crowds than the summer peak. If the Great Barrier Reef, Darwin or the Kimberley are your priority, go between May and October, when the tropical north is in its dry season. Avoid the far north from December to March, which is the wet season and brings monsoon rain and cyclone risk.

Do Americans need a visa for Australia?

Yes, in effect. The State Department is clear that you must get a visa or an approved Electronic Travel Authority before you arrive in Australia. For short tourist or business trips the ETA is the simple route: it is applied for electronically through the Australian government's app or an approved agent for a small fee, and it must be granted before you board. Confirm current requirements with the Australian Department of Home Affairs before you book.

How long is the flight, and how do you handle the jet lag?

Los Angeles to Sydney is about fifteen hours nonstop; from the East Coast, budget twenty to twenty-four hours in total with a connection. Crossing the International Date Line means you lose a day going out and gain it coming back. The Guild's advice for anyone over sixty is to build in a soft landing: fly out in the evening, book a hotel room from the morning you arrive rather than the afternoon, and give yourself two nights in Sydney before doing anything that requires an early start.

How many days do you need in Australia?

Two weeks is the sensible floor, and three is better. Sydney and Melbourne plus one more region — the Reef, Uluru or Tasmania — is a realistic fortnight. Trying to add all three to the same trip means spending your holiday in airports; the distances are American in scale, and domestic flights of three or four hours are normal.
