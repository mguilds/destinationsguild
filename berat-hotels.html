Berat

Where to Stay in Berat

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Berat is called the city of a thousand windows, and the name explains itself the moment you see it: a single steep slope of white Ottoman houses, all of them glazed on the same face, stacked below a castle on a rock above the Osum. The historic quarters have been on the UNESCO World Heritage List since 2008, when Berat was added to the Gjirokaster listing of 2005. The castle on the top is not a ruin but a walled quarter with families still living inside it, which is the thing that surprises most visitors. It is about two hours from Tirana, and one or two nights is the right length.

Mangalem: the Ottoman slope

The reason to comeWalk to everythingCobbles and steps

The quarter directly below the castle, on the right bank, is the face of Berat you have seen in photographs: tall white houses with rows of windows, stepped cobbled lanes between them, the Lead Mosque and the old bachelors’ mosque at the bottom. A number of the houses are now guesthouses, and sleeping in one of them with the valley below the window is the best thing the town offers. Two honest warnings: the lanes are steep, stepped and cobbled, and cars mostly cannot reach the door, so expect to carry a bag the last part of the way.

The castle quarter

Most unusualQuietestSteepest access

Inside the walls on the summit is a living neighbourhood of houses, lanes, ruined and surviving churches, the Onufri icon museum in the cathedral of the Dormition, and the views out over both banks of the river. A handful of guesthouses operate up here. Staying inside a working fortress is a genuine experience and the quietest night in the town, and the cost is the climb — a long steep cobbled approach that you will do at least twice a day unless you drive, and parking up there is limited.

Gorica: the other bank

Best views of MangalemCalmerOver the bridge

Across the Osum by the old stone footbridge, Gorica faces Mangalem and therefore has the view of it — which is why a number of visitors sleep on this side and look at the famous slope rather than standing on it. It is a quieter, less visited quarter of older houses with its own church and a monastery above, five or ten minutes’ walk from the centre over the bridge. Prices tend to be a little below Mangalem for the same sort of room.

The modern town and the boulevard

Easiest accessLevel walkingBest value

Below and north-west of the old quarters, the twentieth-century town runs along a boulevard with the shops, the bus departures, the ordinary restaurants and most of the conventional hotels. It is flat, cars can reach it, and rooms are the cheapest and most practical in Berat. It is also where the town actually lives. Ten to fifteen minutes on foot gets you to the foot of Mangalem. The right choice if stairs or luggage are a consideration.

Out of town and the Osum valley

For a carQuietShort drive in

Beyond the town the Osum valley runs up into hill country, with the Bogove waterfall and the Osum canyon upstream and vineyards and olive groves on the slopes. A scatter of guesthouses and small agritourism places out here are peaceful, often very good value, and entirely dependent on having a car. Worth considering for a second night if you want countryside rather than more cobbles.

Getting around Berat

On foot, and the climb

Everything a visitor comes to Berat for is within about twenty minutes’ walk, but the walk is up. From the boulevard to the castle gate is a long steady climb on cobbles, and Mangalem’s lanes are stepped. Good shoes matter more here than a map, and in wet weather the cobbles are slippery. Plan one ascent a day rather than three.

Getting up to the castle

You can walk the cobbled road up from the town, which is the usual way and takes twenty to thirty minutes at a steady pace, or take a taxi to the gate, which is cheap and the sensible choice if the climb does not appeal or the day is hot. Cars can drive up, with limited parking inside the walls.

Buses and furgons

Coaches and furgon minibuses connect Berat with Tirana in about two hours and with Vlore, Gjirokaster and Korce on the inland routes. Departures are from the newer part of town and run informally rather than to a published timetable, so ask locally on the day. There is no passenger railway worth a visitor’s attention.

Taxis

Cheap and easy within the town, and the obvious way to save yourself the climb to the castle or get to a guesthouse in Mangalem with a bag. Agree the fare before you set off.

Driving and parking

Berat is comfortable to drive to and awkward to drive in: the old quarters are narrow, stepped and in places simply closed to cars. If you are arriving by car, ask your guesthouse in advance exactly where you can park and how far the last stretch is on foot — the answer is often two hundred metres of steps.

When to come

Berat is at its best in the shoulder seasons, and genuinely hard work in high summer. April to early June and September to October are the months to come: warm, clear, green in spring and gold in autumn, and comfortable for a town whose main activity is climbing. July and August are hot on this inland plain, often in the mid-thirties and above, which makes the castle climb a morning or evening job rather than a midday one. November to March is mild, wet, very quiet and very cheap, and Berat in low cloud with the windows lit is a fine sight — but the cobbles are slick, so bring grip.

Getting in: what applies, and what does not

Albania is not in the European Union and not in the Schengen area, so it runs its own border. United States citizens may enter as tourists without a visa and may stay up to one year without a residence permit; your passport must be valid for at least three months from arrival and carry a blank page for the stamp. Most other Western nationalities get ninety days visa-free — check your own. The United States rates Albania Level 2, exercise increased caution, in an advisory issued on 26 August 2026 on account of crime; read the current text at travel.state.gov, because the level and wording change.

If your trip also crosses into Greece, Italy or anywhere else in the Schengen area, the following applies to that crossing and not to Albania:

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Berat — questions

Which part of Berat should I stay in?

Stay in a Mangalem guesthouse if you want the experience of the place and can manage steps and cobbles with a bag. Stay inside the castle walls for the quietest and most unusual night, if the long climb does not put you off. Stay in Gorica across the bridge to look at Mangalem rather than stand in it, a little cheaper and calmer. Stay in the modern town along the boulevard for level ground, car access and the best prices.

How long do I need in Berat?

One night is enough to see it; two is better and lets you take the castle slowly, spend proper time in the Onufri museum, walk Gorica and the bridge, and eat without hurrying. Many people treat Berat as a day trip from Tirana, which works — it is about two hours each way — but means seeing the famous slope in the flat light of the middle of the day and missing it in the evening, which is when it is at its best.

Is Berat hard going on foot?

Moderately, and it is worth being straight about it. The old quarters are built up a slope on cobbles, Mangalem’s lanes are stepped, and the castle is a long steady climb from the town. None of it is dangerous and all of it is short, but a day here involves real up and down. Taxis to the castle gate are cheap and remove the hardest part. If stairs are a problem, sleep in the modern town rather than in Mangalem.

What is actually inside the castle?

A lived-in quarter rather than a monument: lanes, houses with families in them, the remains of a dozen churches and a mosque, cisterns, and the cathedral of the Dormition, which holds the Onufri museum of sixteenth-century icons. There are also the views over Mangalem and Gorica, which are the best in the town. Allow two hours inside the walls, more if the museum interests you.

Is Berat better than Gjirokaster?

They are a pair and the honest answer is to see both, which the UNESCO listing more or less says too. Berat is the gentler of the two to stay in and walk, has the more striking single view, and has the living castle quarter. Gjirokaster is steeper, greyer in its stone, more dramatic in its setting and has the better bazaar. If you only have time for one and want an easier day, take Berat.

Do I need a car?

No. Berat is easy to reach by coach or furgon from Tirana and the inland towns, and everything in the town is walkable or a cheap taxi. A car only becomes useful if you want the Osum valley, the canyon and the Bogove waterfall upstream, or if you are joining Berat to Gjirokaster and the south in one trip.

What should I not miss?

The walk up through Mangalem and into the castle; the Onufri icon museum in the cathedral there; the view back at Mangalem from the Gorica side of the old stone bridge, best in the late afternoon; and the ethnographic museum on the slope, which is an Ottoman merchant’s house kept as it was.
