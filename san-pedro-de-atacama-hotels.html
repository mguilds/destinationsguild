San Pedro de Atacama

Where to Stay in San Pedro de Atacama

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

San Pedro is an adobe village of a few thousand people on the edge of the driest desert on earth, and almost everyone who stays here does so to go somewhere else at dawn — the geysers, the salt flat, the lagoons, the Valley of the Moon. The choice comes down to two: a hotel in the village, where the agencies and the restaurants are a walk away, or one of the desert lodges outside, which sell the whole trip in one price. Below are the places we would actually book, and a plain word about the altitude, which is the one thing visitors underestimate.

In the village

San Pedro is small: a grid of adobe walls and dirt lanes around a white church, with one commercial street and the tour agencies on it. Almost everything in the village is within a ten-minute walk, which is the whole appeal — you book a sunrise excursion, walk back to your room, and walk out again for dinner. The lodges outside the village are quieter and darker for the stars, at the cost of needing a lift for everything.

The desert lodges

A handful of all-inclusive lodges sit outside the village on the old haciendas, selling the desert as a complete package: room, meals, guides and excursions in one price. They are expensive and they are also, for many visitors, the right answer — the guiding is the best in the region and the night sky away from the village lights is extraordinary.

Note that it is out of the village: there is nothing to walk to.

What to know before you book — the altitude

The village is high, the excursions are much higher

San Pedro sits at roughly 2,400 metres, which most people feel only as mild breathlessness on the first day. The famous excursions go far higher: the El Tatio geysers are at about 4,300 metres and the altiplanic lagoons are higher still. That is genuine altitude, reached before dawn, in the cold. Altitude sickness is common on these trips and does not care how fit you are.

Give yourself the first day

The standard advice from local operators, and it is sound: arrive, do nothing much, drink a great deal of water, and leave the high excursions until your second or third day. Anyone with a heart or lung condition, or who is pregnant, should take medical advice before booking a 4,000-metre sunrise tour at all. Ask your hotel which excursions are the high ones; they will tell you plainly.

It is a desert, and it is cold at night

This is the driest desert on earth and the temperature swing between afternoon and dawn is severe. Visitors routinely under-pack: you want sun protection and a hat for the day and a genuinely warm coat, hat and gloves for a pre-dawn start, in every month of the year. Many hotel rooms have no air conditioning and do not need it; a few have no heating either, which matters more.

Nothing here is close

Calama airport is about an hour and a half away by road and the transfer is usually booked with the hotel or the tour. Distances between the sights are long and the roads are rough. Hiring a car is possible but most visitors use the village agencies, which is cheaper and, for the high-altitude sites, safer.

Where to stay in San Pedro — questions

How many nights do I need in San Pedro?

Three is the sensible minimum and four is better. The excursions are spread around the compass and most take half a day or more: the Valle de la Luna at sunset, the El Tatio geysers before dawn, the altiplanic lagoons and the salt flat each a long day. Add to that the first day, which you should spend gently while you get used to the height, and three nights fills itself without hurrying.

Will I get altitude sickness?

Some visitors do. The village itself, at about 2,400 metres, troubles few people beyond a headache or a poor first night’s sleep. The high excursions are a different matter: at the geysers you are around 4,300 metres, and nausea, headache and shortness of breath are common there even among fit travellers. Arrive a day early, drink much more water than feels necessary, go easy on alcohol for the first day or two, and tell your guide at once if you feel unwell — they carry oxygen and they are used to it. Anyone with a heart or lung condition should ask a doctor before booking the high trips.

Is it worth staying out at a desert lodge?

It depends what you want from the trip. The all-inclusive lodges give you expert guiding, excellent food and a dark, silent sky, with nothing left to arrange — and they cost several times what a village hotel costs. A village hotel puts you within walking distance of the agencies, two dozen restaurants and the church square, and lets you choose excursions day by day. Neither is wrong; they are different holidays.

What is there to do that is not an excursion?

Less than you might hope, and that is worth knowing in advance. The village has a handful of streets, some craft shops, the small church and a good many restaurants. The reason to be in San Pedro is what lies outside it — the salt flat, the lagoons, the geysers, the valleys and the night sky. A traveller who does not intend to take excursions will run out of San Pedro in a day.
