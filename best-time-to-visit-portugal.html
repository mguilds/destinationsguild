Best Time to Visit Portugal

Best Time to Visit Portugal — Month by Month

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated August 2026

Portugal has a kinder, simpler calendar than most of Europe: a long warm season, a short sharp wet one, and an Atlantic island that ignores both. But timing still makes or breaks a trip — the Algarve in August is a different proposition from the Algarve in September, and the Douro in July is no place to walk a vineyard. Here is the whole year, plainly, with the crowds, the prices and the honest weather for Lisbon, Porto, the Algarve, the Douro and Madeira.

The short answer

For most travellers the answer is late April to mid-June or mid-September through October. Both give you warm, bright days, a sea that is swimmable, everything open, and crowds and prices well below the peak. July and August are hot, expensive and crowded — and in the Algarve genuinely saturated. November to March is mild but wet on the mainland, cheap and quiet, and excellent for the cities; Madeira alone stays comfortable every month. If you are travelling at an unhurried pace and dislike heat and crowds, aim squarely at the second half of September, when even the Atlantic is at its warmest.

Season by season

Spring — April to early June

The countryside is at its greenest and the light is extraordinary. April is variable and can bring rain, with the sea still bracing; by mid-May the days are reliably warm and the Algarve hotels are all open. Early June is close to perfect — hot enough to swim, cool enough to walk a city at noon, and the crowds not yet arrived. Watch for Easter week, when Portugal travels and the towns fill, and for the Lisbon and Porto saint’s-day festivals in the second half of June, which are wonderful and boisterous in equal measure.

High summer — July and August

Hot, bright, expensive and crowded, and the season we steer most readers away from. Lisbon bakes in the low eighties with the heat trapped in its hill streets, the Algarve is at capacity with beach parking a lost cause, and a good share of the best neighbourhood restaurants close while their owners take their own holidays. The Douro interior can pass 100°F. The compensation is a warm sea and long evenings. If you must come now, book everything months ahead and do your sightseeing before eleven in the morning.

Autumn — September to October

The best-kept secret of the Portuguese year. September still feels like summer, and because the Atlantic lags the air by two months the Algarve sea is at its warmest just as the crowds thin from mid-month and rates fall sharply. It is harvest time in the Douro, the single loveliest moment to ride the valley railway. October is gentler still — warm days, cool evenings, thin crowds — though the rain returns to Lisbon and the north by the end of the month. For unhurried travellers, this is the season we recommend above all others.

Winter — November to March

A different country, and a rewarding one if you know what you are getting. The mainland is mild but wet: Lisbon and Porto rarely turn truly cold, and the cities are cheap, quiet and rewarding, though rain can settle in for days. The Algarve is peaceful, with roughly half its resorts open, and shoulder-season golf and walking at their best. And Madeira comes into its own — subtropical, green and mild all winter, culminating in one of the world’s great New Year fireworks displays over Funchal bay.

Month by month

Temperatures are rough daytime highs; the Douro and the northern interior run hotter in summer and colder in winter than the coast.

January — Lisbon about 59°F, Faro 61°F

The quietest, cheapest month on the mainland: mild, often bright, and wet in spells, with the cities almost empty and museums on shorter winter hours. The Algarve is calm with about half its hotels open. Madeira is at its best value and still comfortably warm. A fine time for a city-and-history trip if you do not need a beach.

February — Lisbon about 61°F, Faro 63°F

Much like January but with the first hint of spring by month’s end and almond blossom in the Algarve and the Douro. Carnival brings parades in several towns. Still very quiet, still very cheap, and still a shade early for the coast — but a lovely, uncrowded time for Lisbon, Porto and Sintra.

March — Lisbon about 65°F, Faro 66°F

Spring begins in earnest. Wild flowers appear, the days lengthen, and Lisbon and Porto are delightful for walking between showers. The Algarve is pleasant on land though the sea is still cold. Prices remain low outside Easter, which sometimes falls late in the month and fills the towns.

April — Lisbon about 68°F, Faro 70°F

One of the loveliest months, with green hills and extraordinary light, though rain is still quite possible. The Algarve hotels are opening and the resorts waking up, but the Atlantic is bracing. If Easter falls in April, expect beautiful ceremonies, full hotels and heavy domestic travel — book ahead.

May — Lisbon about 73°F, Faro 73°F

One of the two best months. Reliably warm, everything open, the crowds still modest and the rates still fair. Ideal for combining Lisbon with Sintra and the coast, for the Douro before the summer heat, and for the Algarve before the schools break up. Only five rainy days on average in Lisbon.

June — Lisbon about 79°F, Faro 81°F

Early June is close to perfect: hot, settled and not yet crowded, with a comfortably warming sea. It is also peak festival month — Santo António in Lisbon around the 12th and 13th, São João in Porto on the 23rd and 24th — which are marvellous but pack the cities. Algarve rates stay below their peak until the very end of the month.

July — Lisbon about 82°F, Faro 84°F

Hot and busy. Beach weather is guaranteed and everything is running, but midday sightseeing becomes hard work and the Algarve fills. The Douro interior is fierce. This is when rates climb and the crowds settle in for the summer. Manageable with an early start and a coastal base, but not the connoisseur’s choice.

August — Lisbon about 83°F, Faro 84°F

The peak, and the month we would steer most readers away from. Highest temperatures, highest prices, fullest hotels and heaviest crowds, with many Lisbon shops and restaurants closed for the owners’ own holidays. The Algarve is at saturation. The sea is at its warmest, which is the compensation. Madeira, cooler and oceanic, is the pleasant exception, and peaks now for swimming.

September — Lisbon about 79°F, Faro 79°F

The best month of the year, in our view. The first half still feels like summer; from mid-month the crowds thin, rates fall and the light softens. The Algarve sea is at its warmest of the whole year, the Douro harvest is in, and everything remains open. If you have any flexibility at all, spend it here.

October — Lisbon about 73°F, Faro 73°F

Gentle, beautiful and good value. Warm days, cool evenings, thin crowds and a sea still swimmable in the first half. The rain returns to Lisbon and Porto as the month goes on — October is one of Lisbon’s wettest — but the Algarve stays drier and the cities are a pleasure. Excellent for Lisbon, Sintra and the south.

November — Lisbon about 64°F, Faro 68°F

The mainland season winds down: mild but wet, quiet and inexpensive, with Lisbon at its rainiest. The cities remain rewarding under an umbrella, the Algarve is peaceful, and Madeira stays warm and green. Good value for a city break, poor odds for a beach holiday.

December — Lisbon about 59°F, Faro 63°F

Cool, sometimes wet and very quiet on the mainland, though the cities do Christmas beautifully and without queues. The exception is Madeira, where Funchal fills for one of the world’s great New Year fireworks displays — book that week many months ahead. A good choice for a cheap, unhurried city trip elsewhere.

What is open when

The most useful thing to understand about timing a Portuguese holiday.

The Algarve runs a season, though a lengthening one

Traditionally the Algarve resorts ran from Easter to October and half of them shuttered for the winter. That is changing: an increasing number of hotels now stay open all year, the lower winter tourist-tax band applies, and golf and walking are at their best from October to April. Even so, in December and January you will find fewer restaurants open and a quieter, sleepier resort than the summer brochures suggest — which for many older travellers is precisely the appeal.

The cities are open all year

Lisbon, Porto, Sintra, Coimbra and Évora work in every month, and a winter visit to any of them is a real pleasure: no queues, museums to yourself and hotel rates a fraction of summer. The only planning point is the weather — November through February is the wet season on the mainland — and slightly shorter winter opening hours at some sites. Pack an umbrella and good grippy shoes and the cities are yours.

Madeira is the true year-round island

Funchal keeps its hotels, restaurants and gardens open in every month, and the climate barely varies. It is the one part of Portugal we would happily send someone to in January, and the obvious choice for winter sun without a long-haul flight. The Flower Festival in late spring and the New Year fireworks are its two calendar peaks; otherwise any month serves. Remember only that the mountains and the north coast are far wetter than sheltered Funchal.

The festivals move the crowds

Three dates fill the mainland and are worth planning around. Easter travels every year and packs the towns for its week. Lisbon’s Santo António on the 12th and 13th of June and Porto’s São João on the 23rd and 24th turn both cities into all-night street parties — glorious to witness, but book six months ahead. And the Douro grape harvest, or vindima, in September is the loveliest time in the valley and the busiest for its hotels.

Heat, sea and crowds — plainly

Heat, and where it bites hardest

Coastal Portugal is rarely oppressive — the Atlantic keeps Lisbon, Porto and the Algarve beaches breezy even in August. The heat that catches travellers out is inland: the Douro valley and the Alentejo plain regularly pass 100°F on summer afternoons, which makes vineyard visits and walled-town sightseeing genuinely uncomfortable. If you are visiting the wine country or Évora, come in May, June or September rather than at the height of summer, and keep the middle of the day for lunch and shade.

The Atlantic is cooler than you expect

Portugal’s sea is Atlantic, not Mediterranean, and it is noticeably cooler. Even at the Algarve’s warmest, in September, the water reaches only about 72°F; in June it is a bracing mid-sixties, and the surf coast of the centre and the west is colder still. Many older visitors do their real swimming in a heated hotel pool and use the sea for paddling and long, flat walks at low tide. Madeira’s water is milder and swimmable for more of the year.

Crowds, and how to sidestep them

The crowd map is simple. July and August fill the Algarve and pack Lisbon’s viewpoints and trams; the June festivals fill the two big cities for a few days each; and the Douro fills for the September harvest. Everywhere, the shoulder months of May, June, September and October give you the same country with a third or more knocked off both the queues and the room rates. For the Algarve specifically, September is worth a special effort: warm sea, thin crowds and prices down by something like forty percent from August.

Rain, honestly

Portugal has a sharp wet-and-dry split rather than year-round showers. From June through August it barely rains anywhere. The rain concentrates from October to March, and it is heaviest in the north-west — Porto is one of the wetter cities in southern Europe — and lightest in the Algarve, the driest region in the country. So a winter city trip means packing for rain, while a summer beach holiday almost never does. Madeira can be showery on its mountains any month, so carry a layer for the heights.

Common questions about timing a Portugal trip

What is the single best time to visit Portugal?

If we had to name one window, it would be the second half of September into early October. The Algarve sea is at its warmest of the whole year, because the Atlantic lags the air by about two months; Lisbon and Porto are still warm and dry; the Douro harvest is under way; and the summer crowds have gone home, taking the peak room rates with them. Late April into June is very nearly as good, with wild flowers and long light, though the sea is still bracing. Both beat July and August comfortably for anyone who dislikes heat and crowds.

When is the Algarve at its best, and when should I avoid it?

Avoid mid-July through August unless you are locked to school holidays. Albufeira, Vilamoura and Praia da Rocha reach genuine saturation, beach parking becomes impossible, restaurants need booking and rates roughly double. June and mid-September to mid-October are strictly better: the same warm days in the high seventies and low eighties, far fewer people, and much lower prices. September has the warmest sea of the year. May and October are cheaper still, with warm days and the occasional shower. Winter is mild and quiet, and an increasing number of hotels now stay open through it.

Is Madeira really a year-round destination?

More than anywhere else in Portugal, yes. Funchal sits in a narrow band from the high sixties to around eighty every month and never has a genuinely cold one, which is why it is marketed as the island of eternal spring and why it is the strongest Portuguese choice for a November-to-March trip. Late spring brings the Flower Festival, and New Year’s Eve brings a world-famous fireworks display over the bay — the one week when Funchal prices spike and rooms must be booked many months ahead. One caution: the north coast and the mountains are far wetter and cloudier than the sheltered south, whatever the seafront forecast says.

What is the weather like in Lisbon and the Algarve through the year?

Lisbon runs from daytime highs around 59°F in winter to the low eighties in July and August, with almost no rain from June through August and the wettest spell in November. Faro and the Algarve are a shade warmer and markedly drier — only about five hundred millimetres of rain a year, effectively none in high summer. Porto, in the north-west, is cooler and much wetter, around 1,255 millimetres a year, with frequent rain from November to February. And the Douro interior is continental: colder winters and summer afternoons that can pass 100°F.

Should I take the train between cities, and when do fares open?

In Portugal, yes — this is the one country in southern Europe where we would tell you to leave the car behind for the main routes. The Alfa Pendular links Lisbon and Porto in under three hours, centre to centre, and Lisbon to Faro in about three. Reservations open sixty days ahead on cp.pt and the cheapest fares go first, so book as soon as your dates are fixed. Do check the operator’s notices before a Douro-valley rail day, as storm damage and engineering works closed parts of the network for stretches of early 2026.

What about the tourist tax and entry rules for 2026?

Several cities charge a small tourist tax per person per night, paid at the hotel: four euros in Lisbon, three in Porto, two in Funchal, and two in Albufeira from April to October. Children under thirteen are exempt. On entry, the EU’s new Entry/Exit System has been fully live since April 2026, so on your first arrival your photograph and fingerprints are recorded and your passport is no longer stamped — allow extra time at the border. The separate ETIAS travel authorisation has been delayed again and is not required for a 2026 trip. Always confirm the current rules with official EU guidance before you book.
