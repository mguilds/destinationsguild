Best Time to Visit the Netherlands

Best Time to Visit the Netherlands

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated August 2026

The short answer is September, or the second half of May into mid-June. Both give you mild weather, long or generous daylight, gardens and terraces in full use, thinner crowds and hotel rates below the summer peak — and September in particular is the wisest month for anyone who would rather not queue in the heat. The longer answer matters, though, because two things shape a Dutch trip more than the temperature does: tulip season, which is both the most beautiful and the most crowded six weeks of the year, and King’s Day on 27 April. Here is the whole year, plainly.

Why September wins

It is the month with the fewest compromises for this readership: soft warm light, the summer crowds gone home, gardens and terraces still in use, reasonable weather and hotel rates well below the July and August peak. The students are back so the cities feel alive rather than emptied out, and the museums and canal boats are bookable at short notice. Mid-September in particular is, for comfort, arguably the best fortnight of the whole Dutch year.

Why late May and early June is the close second

These are the longest days of the year — light until nearly eleven at night — with the meadows and gardens in flower, mild temperatures and crowds that have not yet peaked. Everything is open and running, the terraces are busy in the best way, and the evenings are long enough to fit a canal walk in after dinner. It edges just behind September only because it is a touch busier and the weather a shade less settled.

The tulips are glorious and very crowded

Tulip season, roughly late March to mid-May with a mid-April peak, is the most beautiful six weeks in the Netherlands and also the most crowded and expensive. If the flowers are the reason you are coming, come then and accept the trade — but book far ahead, expect coaches in convoy at Keukenhof, and consider sleeping in Haarlem or Leiden rather than Amsterdam. Confirm Keukenhof’s exact dates on its own site before booking flights.

Winter is cheap, and it shows

November through February is damp, grey and dark by mid-afternoon — not bitterly cold, but short on light and hard on the flat landscape. It is the cheapest time to come and genuinely atmospheric for museums, cosy brown cafes and Christmas markets, and the cities are calm. But if this is your one Netherlands trip, the shoulder seasons will serve you far better.

Tulip season, and King’s Day

The two dates that shape a spring trip — one wonderful, one to decide about deliberately.

Keukenhof and the bulb fields

The great spring garden at Keukenhof and the surrounding bulb fields run roughly from the third week of March to the middle of May, with the peak display usually around mid-April. The exact bloom depends on the winter, and Keukenhof sets its own opening dates each year, so check keukenhof.nl before you commit to flights. The gardens are step-free and easy underfoot, which makes them a good choice for older visitors — but go on the first entry of the day or late in the afternoon to miss the coach crowds.

Seeing the fields, not just the garden

The flower fields themselves are farmland, not attractions, and they change from year to year, so do not chase a specific field you saw in a photograph. The reliable approach is to base yourself near the bulb region — Haarlem, Leiden or Lisse — and either take a guided tour, rent a bike for the flat lanes, or simply take the train between towns and watch the colour go past the window. The bloom is brief; a week either side of mid-April is the safest bet.

King’s Day, 27 April

The national holiday, moved to the 26th when the 27th is a Sunday. The whole country turns orange, a free nationwide flea market fills the pavements, and Amsterdam becomes one enormous canal-side street party. It is a real spectacle and real fun if that is what you want — and it is physically demanding, shoulder-to-shoulder, with hotels booked out months ahead and rates to match. Decide about it on purpose: build your trip around it, or step out of the cities for the day.

The overtourism backdrop

Worth knowing because it shapes a spring trip: Amsterdam is actively trying to have fewer visitors. It has frozen its hotel-bed count, cut short-term rentals in the centre to fifteen nights a year, and levies a 12.5 per cent tourist tax it intends to raise further. In tulip season especially, that makes basing outside Amsterdam — in Haarlem, Leiden or Utrecht — both cheaper and calmer, and it is more or less what the city is asking visitors to do.

Month by month

Weather, light, crowds and what is worth coming for. Temperatures are typical daytime highs, and Dutch rain is spread across the whole year, so pack a light rain jacket whenever you come.

January

Cold, grey and quiet, with highs in the low forties Fahrenheit and the light gone before five. The cheapest month, atmospheric for museums and brown cafes, and calm in the cities. A poor month for the flat outdoors, a fine one for a low-key city break with the galleries to yourself.

February

Still winter — damp, raw and short on daylight — but the museums are uncrowded and rates stay low. Carnival lights up the Catholic south, Maastricht above all, for the days before Lent: loud, colourful and not a restful time to be there, so come for it deliberately or avoid it.

March

The turn of the season. Cold early, milder by month’s end, the days lengthening noticeably, and Keukenhof and the bulb fields usually opening in the third week. Early March is quiet and cheap; the last week begins the spring build-up. A good-value time if you catch the very start of the tulips.

April

Tulips and crowds together. The bulb fields peak around mid-month, the weather is unreliable but often lovely, and this is the busiest and most expensive stretch of the year — sharpened by King’s Day on the 27th, when the whole country parties and hotels sell out. Wonderful if the flowers are your aim; book far ahead and brace for the crush.

May

Arguably the loveliest month. The late tulips run into early May, the meadows green up, temperatures climb into the comfortable sixties, and the days grow long. Late May, once the school holidays and the tulip peak have passed, is the sweet spot — everything open, terraces busy, crowds thinner and rates easing.

June

Early to mid June is the runner-up to September, and by some measures its equal: the longest days of the year with light until nearly eleven, warm but rarely hot, gardens in full flower and the summer crowds not yet arrived. One of the two best windows in the Dutch calendar for an unhurried, comfortable trip.

July

High summer — warm, long-lit and lively, with everything open and the terraces full. It is also peak crowds and peak prices, and it now carries a second problem: the new border checks have pushed arrival queues at Schiphol to one and two hours. Heat waves into the nineties happen, and many older hotels have no air conditioning, so confirm it when you book.

August

As busy and expensive as July, easing only in the last week as European schools go back. Warm, sometimes genuinely hot, with the same air-conditioning caution and the same Schiphol queues. Fine if your dates are fixed — base somewhere with a fan or air conditioning, start sightseeing early, and keep the hot afternoons for shaded canals and cool museums.

September

Our pick. Soft warm light, the crowds gone home, gardens and terraces still in use, comfortable temperatures and hotel rates well below the summer peak — and everything still open. The students are back so the cities feel lived-in rather than emptied. For comfort and value together, mid-September is the finest fortnight of the Dutch year.

October

Quiet, cool and often beautiful, with autumn colour in the parks and canals and highs in the mid-fifties. The light softens and shortens, and the first half is far nicer than the second as the weather turns. Good value, calm museums, and a lovely time for the cities if not for the beach.

November

The month to avoid unless you want cities and early Christmas markets. Grey, wet, often foggy and dark by mid-afternoon, with highs in the mid-forties. It is the cheapest month and it earns the price honestly — fine for galleries and cosy cafes, poor for everything outdoors and flat.

December

Short, dark and cold, but the Christmas markets and the lights lift the cities from late November, and Amsterdam’s canals look their best under winter light. The week between Christmas and New Year is crowded and dear; the rest of the month is quiet. A charming time for a city-only trip, wrapped up warm.

Best time to visit the Netherlands — common questions

What is the best month to visit the Netherlands?

For most travellers, and especially for anyone wanting to avoid both crowds and heat, September into early October, with late May into mid-June a close second. Both give you mild weather, long or reasonable daylight, gardens and canal-side terraces in full use, and far thinner crowds than the tulip rush or high summer, and hotel rates below the peak. If you must have the tulips, come in April and accept the crowds and prices as the price of the flowers.

When is tulip season, and when does Keukenhof open?

The bulb fields and the Keukenhof gardens run roughly from the third week of March to the middle of May, with the peak tulip display usually around mid-April — but the exact bloom shifts with the weather and Keukenhof sets its own opening dates each year, so confirm them on keukenhof.nl before you book flights. Understand what you are buying: this is simultaneously the most beautiful and the most crowded six weeks of the Dutch year, with the highest hotel prices and coaches arriving in convoy.

What is King's Day and should I plan around it?

King's Day (Koningsdag) is the national holiday on 27 April, moved to the 26th when the 27th falls on a Sunday. The whole country dresses in orange, a free nationwide flea market takes over the pavements, and Amsterdam becomes a dense, cheerful, heavily drinking canal-side street party. It is a genuine spectacle and it is also physically hard going, with crowds that do not thin until late and hotels booked out months ahead. Commit to it deliberately or avoid it entirely; there is no comfortable middle ground.

Which months should I avoid?

November through February if grey, wet and dark afternoons would spoil the trip — the Dutch winter is damp and raw rather than severely cold, but the light is short, going dark before five, and the weather does the flat landscape few favours. These months are cheap and atmospheric, and fine for museums and Christmas markets, but a hard sell if this is your one trip. April is worth a note too: it brings the tulips but also the heaviest crowds and highest prices of the year.

How hot does it get, and do hotels have air conditioning?

Summer highs are typically in the low-to-mid seventies Fahrenheit, which is pleasant, but heat waves into the nineties now happen most summers — and this matters more than the numbers suggest, because Dutch buildings were never designed for heat and a great many older hotels, canal-house B&Bs and even some museums have no air conditioning. A hot week in a top-floor canal-house room with a single window is a real and underestimated discomfort for an older visitor. If you travel in July or August, confirm air conditioning when you book, and ask for a lower, cooler floor.

Is the weather good enough in the shoulder seasons?

Yes, and the shoulder seasons are the sweet spot precisely because they trade a little weather risk for far fewer people and lower prices. Late May into mid-June gives you the longest days of the year, with light until nearly eleven at night, mild temperatures and gardens in full flower. September stays warm enough, with softer light and the summer crush gone. Bring a light rain jacket in any season — Dutch rain is spread across all twelve months rather than saved for a wet season — and you will rarely be rained out for a whole day.
