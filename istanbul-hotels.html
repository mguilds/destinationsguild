Istanbul — Where to Stay on Two Continents

Where to Stay in Istanbul — Best Areas & Hotels

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Istanbul is a city where the district you choose changes the holiday more than the hotel does. Sultanahmet puts you inside the old city, within walking distance of almost every monument on your list, and empties after dark. Beyoğlu, Galata and Karaköy, across the Golden Horn, are where the city eats, drinks and stays up, two tram stops from the sights. Beşiktaş and the Bosphorus are the grand, expensive, water-facing addresses, further from the monuments and worth it for the view. Below is each one plainly, with hotels we can stand behind in each.

Sultanahmet and Fatih — inside the old city

For a first visit, and for anyone who wants to walk out of the hotel door into the monuments. Hagia Sophia, the Blue Mosque, Topkapı Palace, the Basilica Cistern and the Grand Bazaar are all within about twenty minutes’ walk of one another here.

The trade is that Sultanahmet empties in the evening. It is a sightseeing district rather than a living one: the restaurants on the main streets are aimed squarely at visitors, and the good eating is a tram ride away. The ground is cobbled and the streets tilt; many of the small hotels are converted wooden houses, which is their charm and also why you should ask about stairs and lifts before you book. Ask for a room at the back — the call to prayer from two great mosques at once is wonderful and it is also at dawn.

Hotel Ibrahim Pasha

$$ Mid-rangeSultanahmetRoof terraceSmall and quiet

A small, long-established hotel on a quiet lane behind the Hippodrome, a few minutes’ walk from the Blue Mosque and Hagia Sophia. The roof terrace looks straight at the minarets and is the reason most guests remember it. Rooms are modest in size, in the way of converted old houses — this is a place to sleep and have breakfast rather than to spend the afternoon, and the location is the point. Ask about stairs when you book.

Beyoğlu, Galata and Karaköy — the evening city

Across the Golden Horn and up the hill: the nineteenth-century European quarter, the Galata Tower, İstiklal Caddesi and the lanes below it where Istanbul actually goes out at night.

This is where we would stay on a second visit, and where many people wish they had stayed on the first. You are two tram stops and a short walk from the old city — close enough to be at Hagia Sophia at opening time, far enough that dinner is a choice rather than a compromise. Karaköy on the water has become the design-hotel and coffee end of it; Galata just above is the most walkable; İstiklal itself is loud until late, so a street off it sleeps better. The hill is a real hill: there is a funicular and a historic underground railway up it for a reason.

Beşiktaş and the Bosphorus — the grand addresses

North along the European shore, where the palaces are and where the water is close enough to hear. This is the expensive Istanbul, and on the right morning it is worth every lira of it.

The Bosphorus hotels are a different proposition from the old city: resort-scale buildings on the water, with pools, proper spas and ferry piers on the doorstep. You are twenty to thirty minutes from Sultanahmet by tram or taxi, so sightseeing takes planning rather than a walk. Beşiktaş itself is a lively neighbourhood with a market and a football stadium; the villages further up — Ortaköy, Bebek, Arnavutköy — are prettier and further out again.

What to know before you book

Which side of the water

Almost everything in this guide is on the European side. The Asian side — Kadıköy and Moda especially — is a fine place to eat and spend a day and a cheaper place to sleep, but it puts a ferry between you and the monuments every morning.

Hills, cobbles and lifts

Istanbul is built on hills and much of the old city is cobbled. Converted-house hotels often have narrow stairs and no lift. If stairs are a problem, say so when you book and prefer the modern hotels in Beşiktaş or along the tram line.

Getting from the airport

Check which airport your ticket says. Istanbul Airport (IST) is well to the northwest; Sabiha Gökçen (SAW) is on the Asian side. Both are a long way out — allow an hour and a half in traffic, and more at rush hour.

Getting around

Buy a rechargeable travel card at any station. The T1 tram runs through Sultanahmet and over the Galata Bridge; the metro, the funiculars and the commuter ferries cover most of the rest. You do not need a car and will not enjoy having one.

When it is busiest

April to June and September to October are the pleasant months and also the full ones. July and August are hot and humid. Winter is cold, grey, sometimes snowy, much quieter and often the best value of the year.

Mosque hours

The working mosques close to visitors around prayer times, which move through the year. Mornings are usually the safest bet, and the dress rules are simple: shoes off, shoulders and knees covered, hair covered for women.

Questions

Which district should I stay in for a first visit to Istanbul?

Sultanahmet, if the monuments are why you are coming — Hagia Sophia, the Blue Mosque, Topkapı Palace and the Basilica Cistern are all within a short walk of one another and being able to reach them before the queues build is worth a great deal. The cost is that the district is quiet and tourist-facing in the evening. If you would rather have the city's night life at the door and accept a tram ride to the sights, stay in Galata or Karaköy instead.

Is the Asian side worth staying on?

Worth spending a day on, yes; worth sleeping on, usually not for a short first visit. Kadıköy and Moda have the best ordinary eating in the city and much better value rooms, but they put a ferry crossing between you and the monuments every morning. On a longer stay, or a repeat visit, it is a genuinely nice place to be based.

How far are the airports from the city?

Both are a long way out. Istanbul Airport (IST) is to the northwest of the European side and Sabiha Gökçen (SAW) is on the Asian side, so check which one your ticket names — they are not interchangeable. Allow about an hour and a half to the centre from either, and more in rush-hour traffic. There are airport buses and a metro connection as well as taxis.

Do I need a car in Istanbul?

No, and we would avoid one. The T1 tram runs through Sultanahmet and across the Golden Horn, the metro and funiculars handle the hills, and the commuter ferries are both transport and the best cheap sightseeing in the city. Buy a rechargeable travel card at any station. Traffic is heavy and parking in the old city is genuinely difficult.

When is the best time to visit Istanbul?

April to June and September to October: warm but not hot, and comfortable for a city you will walk a great deal of. July and August are hot and humid and the sights are at their busiest. Winter is cold and grey, occasionally snowy, much quieter, and often the best value of the year — and Hagia Sophia in January with nobody in it is a memory worth having.
