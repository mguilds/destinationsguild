Malta

Malta Travel Guide

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Malta is a very small country that has been fought over for three thousand years, and almost everything about a visit follows from that. Inside an area smaller than most American counties you have neolithic temples older than the pyramids, a capital city built from nothing by crusader knights and listed by UNESCO in its entirety, Arab field walls, Caravaggio’s largest painting, a hundred and fifty years of British naval history and a working Mediterranean fishing fleet. It is also warm later in the year than almost anywhere else in Europe, and both Maltese and English are official languages, which makes it an unusually forgiving first trip. This guide covers the harbour towns, the north and Gozo town by town, with an honest account of the hills, the heat and when to come.

Why visit Malta

Seven thousand years of building, in one small place

Temples at Ggantija and Hagar Qim older than the pyramids and Stonehenge, a Roman town, Arab field walls, a sixteenth-century city built by crusader knights, and British barracks - all inside an area smaller than most American counties.

English is an official language

Maltese and English are both official, and effectively everyone in a hotel, a restaurant or on a bus speaks English fluently. For a first trip to Europe that removes the single biggest source of friction before you land.

The sea is the point, and it is warm late

Clear, deep water off rock shelves, two sandy bays worth the trip, the Blue Lagoon on Comino, and swimming that is comfortable from roughly June into November - a month longer than most of the Mediterranean.

It is small enough to see properly

You can cross the main island in under an hour. One base will do for the whole country, which means unpacking once and spending the week looking at things rather than moving luggage between hotels.

Malta at a glance

Capital

Valletta, built from nothing by the Knights of St John after 1565 and a UNESCO World Heritage site in its entirety. About a thousand yards long, and walkable end to end in twenty minutes.

Money

The euro, since 2008. Cards and phones are taken almost everywhere and contactless is normal, though some village shops and stalls are still cash. Decline any card machine offering to charge you in dollars.

Language

Maltese and English, both official. Maltese is a Semitic language written in the Latin alphabet - the only one in the world - and Italian is widely understood as well. No visitor needs any Maltese.

Borders

In the European Union since 2004, the Schengen area since 2007 and the euro since 2008. There is no land border. The ferry from Sicily is an internal Schengen crossing; the one from Tunisia is not.

Getting about

Buses reach everywhere and all routes radiate from Valletta, so a cross-island trip often means changing there. Ferries run Valletta to Sliema, Valletta to the Three Cities and Cirkewwa to Gozo. Driving is on the left.

Shape of it

Three inhabited islands - Malta, Gozo and Comino - about 316 square kilometres in all, low, treeless and built of pale limestone. No rivers, no lakes and no mountains; the highest point is a cliff edge.

The regions

Five parts of a very small country. Nothing in this guide is more than about an hour and a quarter from Valletta, the Gozo ferry included.

Valletta and the Grand Harbour

The capital on its ridge and the three older towns facing it across the water - Birgu, Senglea and Cospicua, where the Knights were based during the siege of 1565. This is the historic heart of the country and almost all of its art, fortification and museums, and the two sides are a three-minute ferry apart.

Sliema, St Julian’s and the harbour towns

The modern hotel coast, north-west of Valletta across Marsamxett Harbour: promenades, rock lidos, the best restaurant choice in the country around Spinola Bay, and the only real nightlife district on the islands at Paceville. Where most first visits are based, and rightly so.

The north and the sandy bays

Above St Paul’s Bay the island opens out into ridges and valleys. Mellieha sits on one of them above Ghadira, the largest beach in Malta; Bugibba and Qawra on the next bay south are the cheaper, busier alternative; and the Gozo ferry leaves from the top of it all at Cirkewwa.

The centre and the south

Inland and down the other coast: Mdina, the walled old capital on its hill, silent after the day visitors leave, with Rabat and the catacombs below it; the neolithic temples at Hagar Qim and Mnajdra on the southern cliffs; and Marsaxlokk, still a working fishing harbour full of painted luzzus, with a fish market on Sunday mornings.

Gozo and Comino

The second island is greener, slower and about a third the size, with one small town at Victoria under its citadel, cliffs and small bays around the edge, the Ggantija temples at Xaghra and the red sand of Ramla Bay. Comino, between the two, is nearly empty apart from the Blue Lagoon, which in July and August is anything but.

Things to do in Malta

St John’s Co-Cathedral, Valletta

Plain outside and overwhelming within - a floor of inlaid marble tombstones, every surface carved and gilded, and in the oratory Caravaggio’s Beheading of St John the Baptist, the largest canvas he painted and the only one he signed. Take a timed ticket in the busy months.

The Hypogeum of Hal Saflieni

A neolithic burial complex cut three storeys down into the rock at Paola, about five thousand years old. Visitor numbers are strictly limited to protect it and tickets go weeks or months in advance, so book this before you book anything else on the trip.

Ggantija and the temple sites

The temples at Xaghra on Gozo, and Hagar Qim and Mnajdra on Malta’s southern cliffs, are among the oldest free-standing stone buildings anywhere - older than the pyramids. The cliff-top setting at Mnajdra is as much of the experience as the stones.

Mdina after the buses leave

The walled old capital holds a few hundred residents and almost no traffic, and by early evening the day visitors have gone and the lanes are genuinely silent. Walk the bastion wall at dusk for the view across the whole island to the sea.

A village festa

Every parish holds one through the summer, and they are not laid on for visitors: band marches, statues carried through decorated streets, and fireworks that Malta takes more seriously than almost anywhere. Ask at your hotel which village is holding one that week.

The Grand Harbour from the water

The fortifications, the creeks and the Three Cities make more sense from a boat than from any lookout. A harbour cruise from Sliema takes about ninety minutes; the three-minute public ferry from Valletta to Birgu does much of the same job for the price of a coffee.

Best time to visit & getting there

April, May, late September and October are the months the Guild would pick: warm but not punishing, the sea swimmable at the back end of the year, and the sites workable at midday. July and August are hot - regularly in the mid thirties Celsius with heavy humidity - and busy, and the Blue Lagoon and the sandy bays are crowded from mid morning. Winter is mild at around fifteen degrees and much the cheapest time to come, with the caveat that it is also the wet season and that the north and Gozo close a good deal of their seasonal trade from November to March. There is a fuller month-by-month account on the best time page.

Everything arrives at Malta International Airport at Luqa, in the middle of the island and about twenty to thirty minutes from the harbour towns. There are no scheduled non-stop flights from North America, so you will change in Europe - most often London, Frankfurt, Munich, Rome, Zurich, Paris, Istanbul or Madrid - and the second leg is two to three hours. A ferry links Valletta with Sicily in season. Buses to Valletta, Sliema and St Julian’s meet arrivals, and the taxi desks in the terminal sell fixed-price fares by zone, which is the simplest way to arrive with luggage after a long day.

Fishing here

Malta is a saltwater country and nothing else - there is not a single natural river or lake on the islands, so there is no trout fishing and no coarse fishing to speak of. What there is instead is a serious Mediterranean sea fishery with centuries behind it: the painted luzzu boats at Marsaxlokk still go out for lampuki, the dorado that runs from late August into November and is the one fish the whole country waits for, and the deeper water off the islands holds amberjack, dentex, tuna and swordfish. Shore fishing off the rocks is a normal evening pastime and needs no licence for personal use; charter boats work out of Marsaxlokk, Sliema and Mgarr on Gozo. The rules change with the species and the season - bluefin tuna in particular is tightly quota-managed - so take the skipper’s word on what may be kept. We have no Maltese fishing book on the shelf yet; the whole collection is in The Guild Fishing Library.

Travelling in your later years

The hills are the thing to plan around

Valletta is a grid laid across a ridge and half its cross streets are staircases; Mellieha village sits a steep twenty minutes above its own beach; Mdina and the Cittadella are both on hilltops. None of it is far, but almost all of it is up. Choose the level street rather than the pretty one, and use the two public lifts in Valletta.

Flat where it matters

Against that, the promenades at Sliema and St Julian’s run level along the sea for miles, the ferries are step-on and step-off, Tigne Point was built new with wide flat pavements and lifts, and the distances everywhere are short. A seafront base in Sliema is about as easy as a Mediterranean trip gets.

Heat, not terrain, is what shortens a day

July and August are genuinely hard going in the middle of the day, with little shade on limestone and high humidity. Build a summer day around an early start, a long indoor middle and an evening outside. April, May, October and November ask much less of anybody.

Buses are good, pavements are variable

The bus network is cheap, air-conditioned and goes everywhere, and drivers are used to helping. The pavements are the weak point: narrow, uneven and often interrupted in the older towns, with steps rather than ramps. Taxis and ride apps are plentiful and cheap for short hops, which is worth budgeting for rather than resisting.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Malta is as straightforward as Europe gets for an American or Canadian visitor. It has been in the European Union since 2004, in the Schengen area since 2007 and on the euro since 2008, so an arrival from another Schengen country has no passport control at all, and an arrival from outside it is a single check at Malta International Airport. There is no land border to cross. The ferry between Malta and Sicily is an internal Schengen crossing; the one to Tunisia is not, and is checked.

Malta — questions

How many days do you need in Malta?

Five to seven nights is the right length for a first visit, and the common mistake is three. Two days covers Valletta and the Three Cities properly, one takes in Mdina, Rabat and the temples, one is a beach or boat day, and Gozo wants a day at the very least and is far better with a night or two on it. Because the country is small you can do all of that from a single hotel, so you are not spending the week moving between them.

Which island or town should I base myself in?

For a first trip, Sliema or St Julian’s: the most rooms, the best restaurant choice, the sea-front promenade and a five-minute ferry to Valletta. For history and quiet evenings, Valletta itself, mostly in small townhouse hotels without pools. For a beach holiday or a trip built around Gozo, Mellieha in the north. For the slow version of Malta, a farmhouse on Gozo with a car. Many visitors split a week between the harbour towns and Gozo, which works well.

Do I need to rent a car?

On Malta, generally no, and often it is a hindrance: parking in the harbour towns is very difficult, the roads are narrow and busy, driving is on the left and local driving is assertive. The buses go everywhere, and taxis and ride apps are cheap for short hops. On Gozo, and in the far north around Marfa Ridge, a car genuinely earns its keep - roads are quiet and parking is easy - and it is usually cheaper to hire on Gozo than to take a car across on the ferry.

Is Malta expensive?

It is one of the better-value countries in Western Europe, though less so than it was. Eating out, buses, museum entry and wine are all noticeably cheaper than France, Italy or Spain; hotel rooms in July and August are not, because demand is concentrated into a few weeks and a few towns. The honest advice is to shift the dates rather than the standard: the same room in May or October often costs half what it does in August, and the weather is better for sightseeing.

What about the heat, and when is the sea warm?

July and August regularly sit in the mid thirties Celsius with high humidity and very little shade, which is harder work than the number suggests on bare limestone. The sea, on the other hand, warms late and holds it: comfortable swimming runs from roughly June into November, and October water is often warmer than June water. That is the argument for the autumn shoulder - you get the sea without the heat or the crowds.

Is Malta a good place for a first trip to Europe?

It is one of the best, for three reasons: English is an official language, so nothing is lost at a reception desk or a bus stop; the country is small enough to see from one hotel; and it packs an unusual amount of genuinely first-rank history into a short trip. The trade-off is that it is not a big-city European capital - there is no grand boulevard, no major opera house and relatively little shopping - so if that is the picture in your head, pair it with a few days somewhere else.
