Slovenia

Slovenia Travel Guide

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Slovenia is a small country that behaves like a much larger one. In an area smaller than Wales it fits the eastern end of the Julian Alps, a national park around a 2,864-metre mountain, the limestone Karst that gave its name to the landform and holds some of the finest show caves anywhere, a forty-six-kilometre strip of Venetian Adriatic coast, and a whole half-country of vineyards and thermal springs in the east that most visitors never reach. Nothing is more than about two hours from anything else, which means a week here needs one hotel change or none. This guide covers where to sleep in the places people actually stay, what each season really does to the country, and the practical matters that catch travellers out: the motorway vignette, the car-free city centres, and how much the coast costs in August.

Why visit Slovenia

Three countries in one, two hours apart

Alps, Mediterranean coast and the Pannonian plain inside an area smaller than Wales. You can swim in the Adriatic in the morning and be walking under three-thousand-foot limestone walls by the afternoon, which is not a boast many countries can make honestly.

The caves are world class and nobody mentions them

The Karst region gave its name to the landform, and Postojna and the UNESCO-listed Škocjan caves are among the great show caves anywhere — vast, cool and more impressive than their modest fame suggests. They are open all year and are the reliable answer to a hot day or a wet one.

It is green, and it means it

More than half the country is forest, there is a national park around its highest mountain, and bears, lynx and wolves live in the southern woods. Tap water comes off the mountains and is excellent everywhere. For a traveller who wants the outdoors without an expedition, this is the easy version.

Small, safe and easy to drive

Nothing is more than about two hours from anything else, the roads are good, crime against visitors is rare and English is widely spoken by anyone under fifty. A week here needs one hotel change, or none at all, which is unusually restful for a country with this much in it.

Slovenia at a glance

Capital

Ljubljana, a river city of fewer than three hundred thousand people with a castle on a wooded hill in the middle and the Alps visible at the end of the streets. Small enough to cross on foot in half an hour, and largely closed to cars.

Money

The euro, since 2007. Cards are taken in hotels, restaurants and shops almost everywhere, but carry some cash for mountain huts, farm stalls, markets and rural buses. Decline any card machine that offers to charge you in dollars.

Language

Slovene, which is Slavic and related to Croatian and Serbian but distinct from both. English is spoken fluently by most younger Slovenes and widely in the tourist trade; German and Italian are useful near those borders. No visitor needs any Slovene.

Borders

In the European Union since 2004, the Schengen area since the end of 2007 and the euro since the start of it. Land borders with Italy, Austria, Hungary and Croatia, all now internal Schengen crossings, though checks towards Croatia are sometimes reintroduced temporarily.

Getting about

Driving is the practical choice and is on the right; motorways need an electronic vignette, so check whether a hire car has one. Trains link Ljubljana with Maribor, Koper and the border cities; buses reach Bled, Bohinj and the villages the trains miss.

Shape of it

About twenty thousand square kilometres and two million people. The Julian Alps and Triglav, at 2,864 metres, in the north-west; the limestone Karst and a forty-six-kilometre coast in the south-west; rolling wine hills and thermal springs in the east.

The regions

Slovenia is small enough that these are an hour or two apart rather than a day — but they are genuinely different countries to be in.

Ljubljana and the centre

The capital sits in a basin in the middle of the country with almost everything else within a two-hour drive, which is why so many visitors never move hotel. South and west of it lies the Karst, with the great caves and the Lipica stud farm; east and south-east are the castle towns and the Krka valley.

The coast and the Karst

Slovenia’s share of the Adriatic is only forty-six kilometres long, which makes it crowded in August and delightful in June and September. Piran is the Venetian set piece; the rest of the strip runs from a working fishing town to a container port by way of the spa resorts.

Things to do in Slovenia

Go down into Postojna or Škocjan

Postojna is the famous one, with a little underground railway carrying you into chambers the size of cathedrals and the blind olm — the “human fish” — in a tank at the end. Škocjan, on the UNESCO list, is wilder and has a river thundering through it. Both hold about ten degrees Celsius whatever the season, so take a jacket in August.

Walk the ring path at Lake Bled before breakfast

Six flat kilometres, mostly away from traffic, round a lake with an island church in it and a castle on a cliff above. At seven in the morning in summer you will have it nearly to yourself, which is a different place from the same path at two in the afternoon. Then take a pletna across to the island.

Drive the Vršič pass into the Soča valley

Fifty numbered hairpins over the highest road pass in the country, built by prisoners of war during the First World War, with a small Russian chapel on the way up commemorating those who died doing it. The far side drops into the Soča valley and Bovec and Kobarid. Closed by snow in winter — check before you set out.

Stand on the Isonzo front at Kobarid

The mountains above Kobarid were the Italian Front, where more than a million men became casualties and where a young Hemingway set part of A Farewell to Arms. The museum in the village is one of the best small war museums in Europe, and the Walk of Peace takes you out to the trenches and the ossuary above the town.

Eat and drink in the Štajerska hills

The wine road north and east of Maribor is cellars, farm guesthouses and osmice where growers sell their own bottles with bread, ham and cheese. September and October are the vintage. Arrange a driver or stay the night in the village, because the drink-driving limits here are low and firmly enforced.

Take the thermal waters, the way Slovenes do

The east of the country runs on thermal springs, and the spa resorts are ordinary family destinations rather than luxury retreats — pools, saunas, treatment rooms and half-board, busy in exactly the grey months when there is nothing else to do out there. Rogaška Slatina is the grandest of them.

Best time to visit & getting there

June and September are the two best months for almost any Slovenian trip: warm but not hot, lakes and sea swimmable, every cave and castle open, mountain paths clear and prices below the peak. July and August are hot and busy, especially on the coast and at Bled, though the high mountains are at their best then and the rivers at their liveliest. February is the month for snow, with the caveat that Slovenian resorts are low by Alpine standards and cover varies. May and October are cool, quiet and lovely at middle altitudes while the tops are shut. November is the month we would tell a first-time visitor to avoid — fog in the Ljubljana basin, the colour gone, the snow not yet arrived and much of the seasonal trade closed. There is a fuller month-by-month account on the best time to visit page.

Ljubljana’s airport at Brnik, about twenty-six kilometres north of the city, is the obvious way in, but it is a small airport and routes change with the season, so check what actually flies rather than assuming. A great many visitors arrive instead through Venice, Trieste, Klagenfurt, Graz, Vienna or Zagreb and come on by road or rail — all are inside the Schengen area and between about one and four hours away, and the fares are often much better. Trains link Ljubljana with Maribor in roughly two hours, with Koper on the coast, and over the borders to Austria, Italy and Croatia; buses reach Bled, Bohinj and the mountain villages that the railway misses. Once here, a hire car is what unlocks the Soča valley and the wine country.

Fishing here

Slovenia is a serious fly-fishing country and barely known as one. The Soča and its tributaries in the west hold the marble trout, a large native fish found in only a handful of Adriatic drainages, and the river itself — clear, cold and an improbable green — is among the most beautiful trout water in Europe; the Idrijca, Bača and Unica are all spoken of in the same breath. In the east the Krka and the lowland rivers are gentler water, and the lakes at Bled and Bohinj are fished too. The rules are strict and taken seriously: fishing is managed by local angling clubs, you buy a day permit for the specific beat you intend to fish, much of the best water is fly-only and catch-and-release, and a warden will ask to see your permit. Arrange it through a club or a local guide rather than turning up with a rod and hoping. The Guild has no Slovenian guide on the shelf yet; the general introduction is our fly fishing pages, and the whole shelf is in The Guild Fishing Library.

Travelling in your later years

The old towns are the obstacle, not the country

Slovenia is modern and well organised, and its new buildings, buses and trains are built to be used by everybody. The difficulty is specific: Piran’s old town is steep stairs and cobbles with no cars allowed, Bled’s upper lanes climb hard, and Ljubljana’s old-town buildings often have no lift. Ask about lifts and the slope of the approach before booking, not after.

Ljubljana has free carts, and you should use them

The electric Kavalir carts that potter around Ljubljana’s pedestrian zone are free, and they exist precisely for people who cannot manage the distance. Wave one down without embarrassment. The funicular to the castle saves the only real climb in the city, and the centre is otherwise flat.

Caves mean stairs, and cold

The great show caves are the one thing almost every visitor wants and they involve a good deal of walking on wet, uneven ground, with steps and gradients; Postojna’s railway covers part of the distance but not all of it. They sit at about ten degrees Celsius all year. Ask about the accessible route when you book, and take a warm layer whatever the month.

Heat in August, fog in winter

The lowlands regularly pass thirty degrees in July and August and a room without air conditioning is a real mistake then. In December and January the Ljubljana basin sits under fog for days at a time, which is damp and dispiriting rather than dangerous — the thermal spas in the east are the local answer, and a good one.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Slovenia itself is straightforward. It has been in the European Union since 2004, in the Schengen area since the end of 2007 and on the euro since the beginning of that year, so an arrival from another Schengen country has no passport control at all and an arrival from outside it is a single check at the airport you land in. The land borders with Italy, Austria and Hungary are internal Schengen crossings and are often barely marked. The Croatian border has been an internal crossing since Croatia joined at the start of 2023, although checks are sometimes reintroduced temporarily at particular crossings and the summer queues towards the Croatian coast can be long, so carry your passport and allow extra time. One practical rather than legal point: Slovenian motorways require a vignette, now bought electronically, and a hire car may or may not already have one — ask at the desk rather than assume, because the fines are substantial.

Slovenia — questions

How long do I need in Slovenia?

Five to seven nights sees the country properly. A good shape is two or three nights in Ljubljana, with the Karst caves and Lipica as day trips, two at Bled or Bohinj for the lakes and mountains, and two in Piran if the Adriatic appeals. With four nights, do Ljubljana and the lakes and leave the coast for another time. With ten days, add the Soča valley and the eastern wine country. Because the country is small, many visitors base themselves in Ljubljana and drive out each day, which works perfectly well.

Do I need to hire a car?

For Ljubljana, Bled and the caves, no — buses and trains cover them and a car in Ljubljana is a liability, since the centre is closed to traffic and parking is metered and scarce. For the Soča valley, the wine road, the spa country and the villages around Bohinj, a car changes the trip completely, because the rural bus network is built for schoolchildren rather than visitors. If you do drive, remember the motorway vignette and check whether the hire car already has one.

Is Slovenia expensive?

Cheaper than Austria or Italy, dearer than it was a decade ago, and much cheaper in the east than in the north-west. The good value is in eating and drinking: the weekday set lunch, the dnevno kosilo, is how the country itself eats and costs a fraction of dinner from the same kitchen. The expensive parts of a Slovenian trip are lakeside rooms at Bled in July and August, the coast in the same weeks, and the ski resorts over the holidays. Tipping is not expected beyond rounding up.

Is it safe, and what about the bears?

It is one of the safer countries in Europe for a visitor; the usual sense about valuables in crowded places is enough. The brown bears are real — several hundred of them in the forests of the south — but they avoid people, encounters on marked trails are rare and attacks rarer still. Make normal noise when walking in the southern woods, do not leave food out at a campsite, and never approach a cub. The mountains themselves are the greater hazard: weather changes fast and the high paths have exposed sections and fixed cables.

Can I climb Triglav?

Not as a walk. Triglav is a two-day mountain route with exposed scrambling and fixed cables, done from a hut, and it needs a head for heights, proper footwear and either experience or a guide; people are injured on it every season. Mountain huts need booking in the summer, the season runs roughly from late June to late September, and snow lingers on the high paths well into the summer. If you want the scenery without the exposure, the valley and middle-altitude walking in the national park is superb and far less demanding.

What should I eat?

Slovenian food is Alpine, Italian and Balkan at once, depending on where you are standing. Look for žlikrofi and štruklji in the centre and north, fresh fish and truffle on the coast, jota and cured ham in the Karst, and prekmurska gibanica — a layered cake of poppy seed, walnut, apple and curd — in the far east. Potica, the rolled nut cake, is the national baking. And the wine is genuinely good and barely exported, which is the best argument for drinking it here.
