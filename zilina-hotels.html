Zilina and the Mala Fatra

Where to Stay in Zilina

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Zilina is the capital of the north-west, a town of about 80,000 at the junction of the Vah and the Kysuca, and it has the most agreeable small square in Slovakia: Marianske namestie, arcaded on all four sides, with cafe tables under the arches and a church on the rise above it. The town is not the reason to come, though it is a pleasant two nights. The reason is what stands fifteen minutes to the east: the Mala Fatra, a ridge of limestone and dolomite towers above a river gorge, which is the best walking country in Slovakia outside the High Tatras and much gentler to get into. From the same base you can reach Orava Castle on its rock in the north, the painted village at Cicmany, and Vlkolinec, the UNESCO-listed hamlet of log houses above Ruzomberok that is still lived in. It is also the main railway junction between Bratislava and Kosice, which makes it easy to reach and easy to leave.

Marianske namestie and the old centre

Most centralArcadesWalk everywhere

The arcaded square and the few streets around it, with the Church of the Holy Trinity and the Burian Tower on the slope above, the Budatin park across the river, and the whole of the walkable town within five minutes. This is where the small hotels and guesthouses in old burgher houses are. It is compact, mostly pedestrian and partly cobbled, it is quiet at night by the standards of any capital, and it is the obvious choice for a short stay. Prices here are low even by Slovak standards, which is one of the pleasant surprises of the north-west.

The station and the New Market quarter

TrainsBusesCheapest

Zilina’s railway and bus stations sit together about ten minutes north of the square, and the modern quarter around them has the larger, plainer hotels. It is the practical choice if you are moving on by train — this is the junction where the Bratislava to Kosice main line meets the lines north to Poland and the Vah valley branch — or if you are catching an early bus up to Terchova. It is a flat, unremarkable ten-minute walk to the square. Cheapest beds in town are here.

Terchova and the Vratna valley

MountainsWalkingSki in winter

The village at the mouth of the Vratna valley, about half an hour east of Zilina by bus or car, and the place to sleep if the Mala Fatra is why you came. Terchova is the birthplace of Juraj Janosik, the eighteenth-century outlaw who is Slovakia’s Robin Hood, and it sits between the Janosik gorges — a ladder-and-walkway route up a narrow limestone cleft — and the Vratna valley itself, which has a cable car up towards the Chleb ridge and a small ski area in winter. Pensions and mountain hotels rather than town hotels, cheap, and genuinely in the mountains rather than looking at them.

Ruzomberok, for Vlkolinec and the Low Tatras

UNESCO villageThermal poolsCar useful

Half an hour east along the Vah, Ruzomberok is a working mill town with two good reasons to stop. Above it on a hillside is Vlkolinec, forty-five log houses on the UNESCO World Heritage list, still inhabited, and the clearest surviving picture of what a central European mountain village actually looked like. Below and around it are the thermal pools at Besenova and the western end of the Low Tatras. It is cheaper than Zilina again, it is on the main railway, and it makes a good middle base if you are working your way east towards the Tatras.

Rajecke Teplice and Cicmany, south of the town

SpaFolk architectureQuiet

South of Zilina a side valley runs up towards two quite different things. Rajecke Teplice is a small, well-kept thermal spa with a lake and a park, the quiet alternative to the grander Piestany, and a good place to do nothing for two days. Further up the same road is Cicmany, a village of dark log houses painted all over with white geometric patterns — it began as lime-wash protection for the timber and became the best-known folk motif in the country. Cicmany has very little accommodation of its own; the spa is the place to sleep and the village is the morning out.

Getting around Zilina

Buses to the mountains

Zilina’s bus station is the key to the whole area. Services run up the Vah to Terchova and into the Vratna valley through the day, roughly hourly in season and thinner out of it, taking about forty minutes to Terchova. Buses also run south to Rajecke Teplice and Cicmany and north to the Kysuce valleys. Check the timetable the evening before rather than assuming: the last bus back down from the valley in the shoulder months can be early, and missing it in Vratna is an expensive taxi.

Trains

Zilina is the main railway junction of northern Slovakia. The Bratislava to Kosice InterCity line runs through it — about two and a half hours to Bratislava, three to Kosice — and the Vah valley line carries on east to Ruzomberok, Poprad and the Tatras. Lines also run north towards the Polish border and into the Kysuce. Trains are cheap, reliable and the best way in and out of the town; for the mountains themselves you want the bus or a car.

The Mala Fatra: lifts, gorges and the ridge

The Vratna valley has a cable car that climbs towards the main ridge near Chleb, which is what makes the Mala Fatra so much easier to sample than the High Tatras — one ride and you are on a grassy ridge at around 1,500 metres with a path along it and views both ways. The Janosik gorges at Terchova are the other signature walk: a one-way route up a narrow limestone cleft on ladders, rungs and plank walkways with the stream underneath, a smaller and friendlier version of the Slovak Paradise routes. Waymarking in the Mala Fatra is good and the walking is genuinely moderate rather than alpine, but the ridge is exposed to weather and the gorge ladders are slippery after rain.

Driving, and what the car unlocks

The D1 motorway passes Zilina, so the town is easy to reach by road from either direction, and a vignette is required. The car is what makes the north-west worth several days rather than one: Orava Castle in the far north, Cicmany, Vlkolinec above Ruzomberok, the Kysuce open-air museum, the Terchova valleys and the Oravice thermal pools are all road trips of between twenty minutes and an hour and a half. Winter tyres are compulsory in winter conditions and the side valleys hold snow.

Day trips worth the drive

Orava Castle, about an hour and a quarter north, is a tiered fortress on a rock directly above the Orava river, 154 steps from the gate to the top, and a good deal of the 1922 film Nosferatu was shot there. Bojnice, about an hour south, is the fairy-tale castle and the most visited in the country. Trencin, with its Roman inscription, is an hour and a quarter south-west. Poprad and the High Tatras are about two hours east, and the Polish border and the Zakopane side of the Tatras about two hours north-east.

When to come

The Mala Fatra has a longer usable season than the High Tatras, which is one of the reasons to come here. Late May to October is the walking year, and unlike the Tatra National Park there is no blanket legal closure of the high trails — though snow lies on the ridge into May and the cable car and the gorge routes take maintenance breaks in spring and autumn, so check before you build a day around either. June and September are the best months: flowers and long days in June, settled weather and empty paths in September. July and August are warm and busy, with Czech and Polish holidaymakers filling Terchova and the valley. January to March is the ski season at Vratna, Donovaly and the smaller areas, and Zilina itself is a cheap, grey, perfectly workable winter town. November is the dead month everywhere in Slovakia and this is no exception.

One note on the gorges: the Janosik routes at Terchova are ladders and walkways over running water, one-way, and they are not suitable for anyone unsteady on a ladder or afraid of heights. They close in heavy rain and in winter. If that rules them out, the cable car in the Vratna valley gives you the ridge without any of it.

Slovakia has used the euro since 1 January 2009 — it was the first of the central European countries to adopt it and the second post-communist state after Slovenia, so there is no Slovak koruna to change and anything quoting one is describing the country before 2009. Cards are taken almost everywhere a visitor goes and contactless is normal on city transport; keep a little cash for a mountain hut, a village pub, a chairlift ticket office in the smaller valleys and a church collection box.

Getting in: what applies, and what does not

Slovakia is in the European Union, in the Schengen area since 21 December 2007 and in the euro since 1 January 2009, so arriving from Austria, Czechia, Poland or Hungary is an internal journey with no routine passport check and nothing to change. The rules below apply when you first enter the Schengen area from outside it.

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen external border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave. United States, Canadian, British and Australian citizens may visit as tourists without a visa for up to ninety days in any one hundred and eighty, counted across the whole Schengen area rather than against Slovakia alone.

We could not read the United States travel advisory for Slovakia on the day this page was written — the State Department’s site would not answer us — so we are not printing a level here rather than quoting one from memory. Read the current advisory yourself at travel.state.gov before you go; the level and the wording change, and it is a two-minute check.

Zilina — questions

Is Zilina worth a stop, or just a base?

Mostly a base, and an unusually pleasant one. Marianske namestie, arcaded on all four sides, is the best small square in the country and worth an evening; the Budatin castle and park across the river and the Burian Tower above the square fill a morning. Beyond that the town is ordinary, and the reason to be here is the Mala Fatra fifteen minutes east, Orava Castle to the north and Vlkolinec to the east. Two nights in the town with a day in the mountains is the right shape; a week wants a car.

Should I stay in Zilina or up at Terchova?

Terchova if the walking is the point — you wake up at the mouth of the valley, the gorge route and the cable car are on your doorstep and the evening is a mountain pension rather than a town. Zilina if you want restaurants, a square, a railway station and cheaper, more varied rooms, and are content with a forty-minute bus each way. Many people split it: a night or two in town, then two up the valley.

How does the Mala Fatra compare with the High Tatras?

Gentler, greener, lower and much less crowded. The Mala Fatra tops out around 1,700 metres against the Tatras’ 2,655, and its character is limestone towers, beech and spruce forest and grassy ridges rather than bare granite and glacial tarns. The practical differences matter more than the scenery: the season is longer, there is no legal closure of the high trails, the cable car puts you on the main ridge in one ride, and you will meet a fraction of the people. If you only have time for one range and you are a walker rather than a peak-bagger, this is arguably the better choice.

What is Vlkolinec and is it worth the trip?

A hillside hamlet of forty-five log houses above Ruzomberok, on the UNESCO World Heritage list since 1993, and still lived in rather than preserved as a museum — which is the whole point of it. There is a wooden belfry, a log well, a small church and a dirt lane, and it takes perhaps an hour to walk round. It is worth the half-hour drive from Zilina, and it pairs well with the thermal pools at Besenova twenty minutes further on. It is a steep site on rough ground, so it is not an easy visit for anyone unsteady.

Is Cicmany worth seeing?

If you have a car and an interest in folk architecture, yes — it is one of the most photographed villages in Slovakia and the white geometric patterns painted over the dark log walls are unlike anything else in Europe. It is about three-quarters of an hour south of Zilina. Manage your expectations on scale: it is a small village and a serious fire in 1921 destroyed much of it, so a good part of what you see is rebuilt. Combine it with the spa at Rajecke Teplice on the same road and it is a comfortable half day.

What about bears here?

The Mala Fatra and the Velka Fatra next to it are genuine bear country — more so than the high rock of the Tatras — and there have been serious incidents in the central Slovak ranges, including a fatal attack in central Slovakia in April 2025 after which the government declared an emergency in several districts. Slovakia put its brown bear population above 1,300 animals in 2025. This should not stop you walking here; it should change how you do it. Stay on marked trails, make noise in the beech forest and in berry scrub, walk in company rather than alone at dawn or dusk, leave no food at a hut or in a car park, never go near a cub, and call 112 if you need to. Guided walking is a reasonable option if this worries you.

Do I need a car?

Not for the town and not for Terchova and the Vratna valley, which the bus serves through the day. Yes for Orava Castle, Cicmany, Vlkolinec and the Kysuce museums, all of which are awkward-to-impossible on the bus timetable within a day. A sensible compromise in the north-west is to arrive by train, use the bus for the mountains, and hire a car for one or two days of castles and villages.

Do I need cash?

A little more than in Bratislava. Slovakia has used the euro since 2009 and cards work in the town, the supermarkets and the larger pensions without trouble, but the mountain huts, the small valley pubs, the Vratna cable car ticket office in the quieter months and the village shops are all happier with cash. Draw some in Zilina before you go up the valley.
