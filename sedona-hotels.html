Where to Stay in Sedona — Uptown, West Sedona & Oak Creek

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Sedona is a small town in a very large landscape. About 11,000 people live here, at roughly 4,500 feet, among red sandstone buttes that turn a different colour every hour of the day. It is about two hours’ drive north of Phoenix, and almost everything a visitor wants — the trailheads, the galleries, the jeep tours, the restaurants with a view — sits within a few miles of the two highways that cross town. Where you sleep matters more here than in most places, because the traffic on those two roads decides how your days go. This page sorts the town into its four real choices and names the hotels in each that we could confirm today.

Choosing Your Area in Sedona

Four parts of town, four quite different trips.

Uptown Sedona, for walking everywhere

Uptown is the old commercial heart on State Route 89A, where the shops, tour desks and restaurants are packed close together and the red cliffs stand right behind the rooftops. If you want to park the car on arrival and do your evenings on foot, this is the place. Just south, along State Route 179, the Tlaquepaque arts village and Gallery Row are an easy walk or a short hop.

The trade-off is the crowds: Uptown is where the day-trippers come, and the main street is busy from mid-morning. Book a room that sits a block or two off the highway, or on the creek side, and you get the convenience without the noise.

West Sedona, for value and the western trailheads

West Sedona is the long stretch of 89A heading towards Cottonwood. It is where most people who live in Sedona actually shop and eat, and it is generally the better-value end of town. Dry Creek Road and the trailheads on the western side of town are close, and so is Airport Mesa, reached by Airport Road, for wide views over the red rocks.

You will drive to Uptown rather than walk, but you also avoid most of the Uptown traffic.

Village of Oak Creek, for Bell Rock and a quieter base

South of town on State Route 179, the Village of Oak Creek sits under Bell Rock. It is the first part of Sedona you reach coming up from Phoenix, and it suits travellers who want to be on a trail early — the easy Bell Rock Pathway is close by — and who do not need to be in town every evening.

It is calmer than Uptown, with golf courses close by and its own small run of shops and restaurants.

The canyons, for a resort or a cabin

Two very different hideaways lie just outside town. To the northeast, Oak Creek Canyon runs up 89A towards Flagstaff, with cabins and lodges strung along the creek under the trees. To the west, Boynton Canyon is a quiet box canyon at the end of a long road, home to one of Sedona’s large resorts and some of its best-known hiking.

Both feel properly away from it all. Both also mean driving for most meals, and the canyon road is the one most affected when the weather turns — see the note on the November 2026 flooding below.

Getting There & When to Go

The handful of Sedona details that change how a trip goes.

Getting to Sedona

Most visitors fly into Phoenix and drive. The Sedona Tourism Bureau puts the trip at about 113 miles and roughly two hours: north on Interstate 17, then west on State Route 179 through the Village of Oak Creek into town. Airport shuttle companies also run between Phoenix and Sedona; the visitors bureau lists them on its website.

If you are coming from the north, State Route 89A down through Oak Creek Canyon is one of Arizona’s designated scenic byways — when it is open (see below).

The November 2026 flooding — check the roads

Heavy rain on 1 November 2026 flooded Oak Creek and closed roads across northern Arizona. In its update of 3 November 2026 the Sedona Tourism Bureau reported that State Route 89A through Oak Creek Canyon, between Sedona and Kachina Village, remained closed with no reopening date, that State Route 179 had reopened, that most evacuation orders had been lifted, and that visitors should stay out of the creek. It also said most businesses in the area were unaffected and welcoming visitors.

Before you travel, check the current road conditions at az511.gov and the latest notice on visitsedona.com, and confirm directly with any creekside or canyon hotel.

When to go

Spring and autumn are the comfortable seasons. The visitors bureau gives average highs of about 76°F in spring and 74°F in autumn, against about 95°F in summer and 60°F in winter, with most of the year’s rain falling in the summer monsoon. The autumn colour in the canyons usually arrives in late October higher up and early November around town. Those are the busiest and dearest months for rooms, so book early; winter is quieter and often bright, with the occasional dusting of snow on the red rock.

The Red Rock Pass

You do not need a pass to visit the town, the shops or the galleries. To park at many trailheads on the national forest around Sedona you need a Red Rock Pass (or an America the Beautiful pass) displayed in the car; not every trailhead requires one. Passes can be bought online through recreation.gov.

Sedona Hotels FAQ

The questions travellers ask most before booking a room here.

Where is the best place to stay in Sedona?

It depends on the trip. Uptown if you want to walk to restaurants and shops and do not mind crowds. West Sedona for better value and the western trailheads. The Village of Oak Creek for a quieter base beside Bell Rock. Boynton Canyon or Oak Creek Canyon if you want a resort or a cabin and are happy to drive to dinner.

How far is Sedona from Phoenix airport?

The Sedona Tourism Bureau gives the drive from Phoenix as about 113 miles and roughly two hours, by Interstate 17 north and then State Route 179 into town. Allow longer at weekends and in the busy spring and autumn seasons.

Is Sedona open after the November 2026 floods?

Yes, with exceptions. The Sedona Tourism Bureau reported on 3 November 2026 that most businesses were unaffected and welcoming visitors, but that State Route 89A through Oak Creek Canyon was still closed and some creekside recreation areas were restricted. Check az511.gov and visitsedona.com before you travel, and confirm directly with your hotel.

When is the best time to visit Sedona?

Spring and autumn, when average highs sit in the mid-70s Fahrenheit by the visitors bureau’s figures. Summer is hot with afternoon monsoon storms, and winter is cool and quieter. Spring and autumn are also the busiest seasons, so book rooms early.

Do I need a Red Rock Pass in Sedona?

Only to park at many of the national forest trailheads and day-use sites around Sedona; not every trailhead requires one. You do not need a pass to visit the town, shops, galleries or restaurants. An America the Beautiful interagency pass is also accepted.
