Dominica

Dominica Travel Guide

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

First, the thing everyone gets wrong: this is the Commonwealth of Dominica, not the Dominican Republic. They are two different countries about five hundred miles apart. Dominica is a small, mountainous, English-speaking island in the eastern Caribbean between Guadeloupe and Martinique — 750 square kilometres, around 290 square miles, with something over seventy thousand people, Roseau as its capital and the East Caribbean dollar as its money. It has been independent since 3 November 1978. What makes it worth the journey is that it was never developed: there is no resort coast and no strip, the mountains come down to the sea, and UNESCO inscribed nearly a tenth of the entire country as the Morne Trois Pitons World Heritage site in 1997 — fifty fumaroles, five volcanoes, three freshwater lakes and a boiling one. Sperm whales live offshore all year. Hurricane Maria struck it as a Category 5 in September 2017 and the island rebuilt itself, which belongs in any honest guide to it.

Why visit Dominica

Six reasons that hold up after the brochure is closed.

A World Heritage site in the middle of it

UNESCO inscribed Morne Trois Pitons National Park in 1997 under two criteria — one for its geology, one for its forests. It covers 6,857 hectares, which UNESCO puts at roughly nine per cent of the whole country’s land area, and it was designated in 1975 as Dominica’s first national park. Few countries have given up a tenth of themselves to a park.

The Boiling Lake

UNESCO describes it as a massive hot spring with a water temperature of about 95 degrees Celsius, surrounded by steep cliffs, and one of the largest of its kind in the world. You reach it on foot through the Valley of Desolation, which the same files call the Grand Soufriere and describe as barren in sharp contrast to the lush forest around it. It is a hard day’s walk and it is the thing people remember.

Sperm whales, all year

A group of sperm whales lives in the waters off Dominica year-round. That is unusual anywhere in the world and it is the island’s real distinction for anyone interested in large marine animals. We are deliberately printing no season, sighting rate or swim-with rule here, because we could not source one — ask the operator.

Fifty fumaroles and five volcanoes

UNESCO’s own listing counts 50 fumaroles, three freshwater lakes, a boiling lake and five volcanoes inside that one park, with Morne Trois Pitons itself standing 1,342 metres above the sea. Boeri Lake and Freshwater Lake are named as the country’s largest lakes. This is a working volcanic island, not a scenic one.

It was never developed

No resort strip, no casino coast, no fleet of jet skis. Dominica is mountains that come down to the water, and almost everything to stay in is small — cottages, seven-room hotels, villas up a track. If you want nine restaurants and a water park, this is the wrong island, and that is most of its appeal.

It rebuilt itself

Hurricane Maria made a direct Category 5 landfall here on 18 September 2017 with 165 mile-an-hour winds, the first Category 5 on record to strike the country; 65 people died and early estimates suggested ninety per cent of buildings were damaged or destroyed. The island came back. That is part of the story and belongs in any honest guide to it.

Dominica at a glance

First, which Dominica

This is the Commonwealth of Dominica — not the Dominican Republic. They are two different countries, 500 miles apart, and the confusion is constant. Dominica is a small, English-speaking, mountainous island in the eastern Caribbean with Roseau as its capital and the East Caribbean dollar as its currency. The Dominican Republic is Spanish-speaking, shares Hispaniola with Haiti, and uses the peso. If your flight says Santo Domingo or Punta Cana, you are going to the other one.

Where it is and how big

The eastern Caribbean, in the Windward chain between Guadeloupe to the north and Martinique to the south. 750 square kilometres, or 290 square miles — small enough to drive the length of in a day, mountainous enough that you would not want to. The highest point is Morne Diablotins, which the sources we read put at 1,448 metres, about 4,751 feet.

Getting there

Douglas–Charles Airport (DOM) is the one with the jets: a single 6,352-foot runway, now taking mainline aircraft. United became the first airline to fly a mainline jet here on a scheduled basis in February 2025, with Boeing 737-700s from Newark, and American Airlines is scheduled to follow with seasonal Airbus A319 flights from Miami from 17 December 2026. Canefield (DCF), three miles north of Roseau, is a 3,130-foot strip handling turboprops in daylight hours only.

Language and money

English is the official language, with Dominican Creole French widely spoken alongside it and Kokoy in some communities. The currency is the East Caribbean dollar, shared with seven neighbouring territories — the Eastern Caribbean Central Bank lists its members and the Commonwealth of Dominica is one of them. The EC dollar is pegged to the US dollar at EC$2.70, a fixed rate the bank’s own site marks the fiftieth anniversary of.

Driving

Visitors need a temporary Dominican driving licence: the UK Foreign Office says you must show your own licence to get one and that hire companies will usually help. It also says to arrange car hire before you arrive, that roads are sometimes narrow and in poor condition with few road signs, and — specifically — that the main highway from Douglas–Charles Airport is prone to landslides after bad weather with sheer drops where the road has fallen away, and not to drive it at night. Taxis are not metered; agree the fare.

Independence and hurricane season

Dominica became independent on 3 November 1978, and 3 November is Independence Day. The Atlantic hurricane season runs 1 June to 30 November; September is its statistical peak and the month Maria struck in 2017. Our best time to visit page goes through the risk and the value month by month.

The island, and which part is which

Roseau and the south-west

The capital, on the sheltered Caribbean coast about a third of the way up the island: a small working town of narrow streets and a harbour front, with the mountains behind. The most practical base for a first visit, because everything else is a drive from it. Not a beach holiday.

Portsmouth and the north-west

The second town, on a broad west-facing bay, with Cabrits National Park on the headland above it. This is where the island’s small high-end places are, and where the diving coast is — a scuba centre opened inside the park itself in 2018. Quieter and more private than the capital.

The north-east coast

Calibishie, Marigot and the Atlantic side, in the parish of Saint Andrew. The quietest corner in this guide, with cottages and small estates rather than hotels, and the island’s jet airport on the same side. Two or three nights of real quiet rather than a base.

The interior

Morne Trois Pitons National Park and the mountain spine: 6,857 hectares of rainforest, crater lakes, fumaroles and five volcanic centres, with at least five forest types including rare elfin or cloud forest on the highest ground. This is why people come, and almost none of it is somewhere you sleep.

Soufrière and the south

Below Roseau the coast road runs on past the sulphur springs that give Soufrière its name — the Ministry of Tourism lists them among the island’s places to visit — towards the island’s tapering southern tip at Scott’s Head. The island’s best-known wellness resort is up here, well away from town.

What there is not

There is no resort coast, no strip and no second city beyond Portsmouth. There is also, we have to report, no description of Dominica’s cruise berths that we were able to source — see the section below. Dominica is a country of three or four places to stay and one very large park.

Things to do in Dominica

The Boiling Lake

The island’s set-piece walk, through the Valley of Desolation to a hot spring UNESCO puts at about 95 degrees Celsius and calls one of the largest of its kind in the world. It is a long, hard, hot day on volcanic ground. We could not read the Forestry Division’s own page on fees, hours or whether a guide is compulsory, so take a guide and ask locally about the site pass — both are sensible regardless.

The rest of the World Heritage park

Boeri Lake and Freshwater Lake, which UNESCO names as the country’s largest; the fumarole fields; and the forest itself. The Ministry of Tourism’s own list of places to visit names Boeri Lake, Freshwater Lake, Trafalgar Falls, the Soufriere Sulphur Springs, the Boiling Lake and the Syndicate Nature Trail — we are naming them from that list without placing each one inside the park, because UNESCO’s own documents do not name them all and we are not going to assume.

Cabrits and Fort Shirley

The best half-day in the north. The park was established in 1986 and covers 1,313 acres; the British began Fort Shirley in 1765 under Governor Thomas Shirley, the French extended it during their occupation from 1778 to 1784, and it was abandoned in 1854. Dr Lennox Honychurch began restoring it in 1982, and today several buildings are fully restored while the ruins of the rest lie scattered through the peninsula.

The Waitukubuli National Trail

Fourteen segments, 115 miles or 185 kilometres, running the length of the island from Scott’s Head in the south to Cabrits National Park in the north — the longest hiking trail in the Caribbean. It opened partially in 2011 and officially in 2013, and the Forestry Division manages it; visitors have needed a paid trail pass since July 2013. One caution we will not skip: we could not confirm from any page we could read which segments are open today after the 2017 hurricane. Check with the Forestry Division before you plan a through-hike.

Diving and whales

The west coast shelf drops away close in, which is why the dive operations work from this side and why a scuba centre opened inside Cabrits National Park in 2018. The sperm whales that live off the island year-round are the other draw. One practical note for divers: the UK Foreign Office’s health page for Dominica does not mention a decompression chamber on the island, and we could not confirm one anywhere else — ask your operator directly where the nearest chamber is before you dive deep.

A chocolate estate

At Pointe Baptiste, on the north-east coast at Calibishie, there is a working chocolate factory on an estate you can tour: its own site prices the tour at 10 EC dollars a head, says it ends with a tasting, and that the estate is open daily from 9.30 to 5. Small, real and a complete change of pace from a mountain.

When to go

Dominica has two seasons. The dry season, about January to May, is the settled and dearer half: more sun, lower humidity, a steady trade wind, and the firmest footing on trails — which matters on an island where the best days out are walks. March is our pick of the twelve. The green season, about June to November, is hotter, wetter, far cheaper and extraordinarily green, with the waterfalls at their fullest, and it carries the Atlantic hurricane season from 1 June to 30 November. September is both the cheapest month and the statistical peak of that season, and the month Maria struck in 2017. The best value with good weather sits on the two edges of the dry season: the first half of December, before the Christmas rates, and late April into May. On festivals, be careful: the tourism authority publishes the Mas Domnik 2027 carnival season as 9 January to 10 February but not the parade dates, and no 2027 date for the World Creole Music Festival had been published anywhere we could read. Our best time to visit Dominica page has all twelve months, the price ladder and a full account of which dates are real and which nobody has announced.

Arriving by ship

We are going to be straight with you, because this is a cruise desk and you would rather have an honest gap than a confident guess. Dominica is a port of call on eastern and southern Caribbean itineraries, and for anyone who prefers landscape to shopping it is one of the more rewarding days ashore in the region — the island’s great sights are close to the coast and the drives are short, if slow. What we cannot give you is a sourced description of where your ship ties up. The Dominica Air and Sea Ports Authority runs the island’s seaports, and its website instructs automated readers not to fetch its pages, so the Guild has no verified account of the berth arrangements, their size, how many ships they take or what is walkable from them. The tourism authority’s own cruise and travel-information pages would not load for us either. Your cruise line’s own port information is the version that counts, and it is the one to plan from. What we can tell you is what is worth your day: one direction, done properly. The mountains and the World Heritage park inland, or the north to Cabrits and Fort Shirley, or the water — diving, or the sperm whales that live here year-round. Trying to combine two is how people spend a Caribbean day looking through a minibus window. Travelling in your later years

Dominica rewards older travellers who plan honestly and punishes those who plan optimistically, and the difference is almost entirely about terrain. Stay in Roseau: it is central, the waterfront is flat and walkable, the market and the Botanic Gardens are close, and you unpack once. Travel in the dry season, February to April, when the footing is firm and the humidity is lowest. Then be realistic about the walks. The famous ones — the Boiling Lake above all — are long, hard days on volcanic ground, and there is no shame whatever in enjoying this island from a car, a boat and a short level path instead; Cabrits and Fort Shirley, for instance, is largely level walking once you are up on the headland, shaded, and genuinely interesting. Hire a driver rather than a car for the airport run and the mountain days: the Foreign Office is blunt that the roads are narrow and sometimes poor with few signs, that the airport highway is prone to landslides with sheer drops where it has fallen away, and that it should not be driven at night. On health, the same source says the government hospital in Roseau, the Dominica China Friendship Hospital, can cope with many types of treatment but that serious cases may require emergency evacuation, and that private treatment is expensive; the emergency number is 999. Take insurance that covers medical evacuation as well as weather disruption, carry your medicines in your hand luggage, and when you book a small property ask the one question that matters most here — how many steps are there between the car and the bed.

Getting in

There is no electronic travel authorisation for Dominica, and that is worth saying plainly because several of its neighbours have introduced one and travellers now assume they all have. Neither the UK Foreign Office nor the US State Department mentions any eTA, pre-arrival form or online immigration form for Dominica. For British passport holders the Foreign Office states that you do not need a visa to visit, that the border authorities will grant a stay of six months on arrival, and — this one is a hard requirement — that your passport must have an expiry date at least six months after the date you arrive. It adds that you may also need to show a return or onward ticket at border control. For US citizens the State Department states that no visa is required for stays of less than six months provided you have an onward or return ticket, confirmation of accommodation and can produce evidence of your ability to maintain yourself; it asks for one blank page for the entry stamp and says the passport must be valid at the time of entry. Note that the two governments differ on passport validity, so take the stricter of the two and travel with six months left on yours. The departure tax catches people out: both governments give it as 86 East Caribbean dollars or 33 US dollars; the Foreign Office says it is usually included in the cost of an air ticket but that you also pay it when leaving by ferry, and the State Department notes children under twelve are exempt. A yellow fever certificate is required if you are arriving from a country listed as a transmission risk, and cash or travellers cheques of EC$10,000 or more must be declared. One honest caveat on dates: the State Department page we read was last updated in October 2023, while the Foreign Office page had been updated this month, so where they differ the newer one is the better bet — and travellers on other passports should check their own government’s current advice, which is the only version that counts at the desk.

Fishing here

Dominica is blue-water fishing, not flats fishing, and the island’s shape tells you why: the volcanic shelf falls away very close to shore, especially on the sheltered west coast, so deep water is minutes from the harbour rather than an hour offshore. That is the same geography that brings the sperm whales in close and makes the west coast a diving coast. What Dominica does not have is the broad, shallow, hard-bottomed sand flats that bonefishing is built on — for that you want the Bahamas and the Caribbean’s flats proper, which is the subject of our guide to fishing the flats. The Guild Fishing Library holds the whole shelf: lakes, rivers and salt. If you are coming to Dominica to fish, book the boat before the room, and ask the skipper what the season does to the run — the green season weather that fills the island’s waterfalls also cancels days at sea.

Questions travellers ask about Dominica

Is Dominica the same as the Dominican Republic?

No, and this is the single most common mistake made about this island. The Commonwealth of Dominica is a small, mountainous, English-speaking country in the eastern Caribbean, between Guadeloupe and Martinique, with Roseau as its capital and the East Caribbean dollar as its currency; it became independent on 3 November 1978. The Dominican Republic is a much larger Spanish-speaking country about 500 miles away that shares the island of Hispaniola with Haiti and uses the peso. If your booking says Santo Domingo, Punta Cana or Puerto Plata, you are going to the other one. Check before you pay.

When is the best time to visit Dominica?

February to April is the most reliable, and March is our pick. The dry season runs from about January to May: steadier sunshine, lower humidity, a dependable trade wind, the firmest footing on the mountain trails and the highest room rates. June to November is the green season - hotter, wetter, much cheaper, with the waterfalls at their fullest - and it carries the Atlantic hurricane season, 1 June to 30 November. The best value with good weather is the first half of December or late April into May. September is both the cheapest month and the peak of the hurricane season, and the month Hurricane Maria struck in 2017.

Do I need a visa or an eTA for Dominica?

No eTA at all, which is worth knowing because several neighbouring islands have introduced one. Neither the UK Foreign Office nor the US State Department mentions any electronic travel authorisation, pre-arrival form or online immigration form for Dominica. British passport holders need no visa and are granted six months on arrival, but their passport must have an expiry date at least six months after the date they arrive. US citizens need no visa for stays under six months provided they have an onward or return ticket, confirmation of accommodation and evidence of their ability to maintain themselves, plus one blank page for the stamp. There is a departure tax of EC$86 or US$33, usually included in an air ticket but payable separately if you leave by ferry, with children under twelve exempt.

What is the Boiling Lake, and can anyone walk to it?

It is a flooded fumarole, and UNESCO describes it as a massive hot spring with a water temperature of about 95 degrees Celsius, surrounded by steep cliffs, and one of the largest of its kind in the world. You reach it on foot through the Valley of Desolation, which UNESCO calls the Grand Soufriere and describes as barren in sharp contrast to the forest around it. It is a long, hard, hot day on unstable volcanic ground and it is not a casual walk. We could not read the Forestry Division's own page on fees, opening hours or whether a guide is compulsory, so we cannot quote you a rule - but take a guide and ask locally about the site pass, because both are sensible whatever the regulations say.

Where should I stay on Dominica?

Roseau for a first visit. The capital sits on the sheltered south-west coast about a third of the way up the island, which means the World Heritage park inland, the dive sites offshore, the sulphur springs to the south and the road north are all day trips from one bed. Portsmouth and the north-west is the choice if you want the island's small, private, high-end places or if diving is the point. Calibishie on the north-east coast is for two or three nights of genuine quiet rather than as a base. What none of them is, is a beach holiday in the Barbados sense - Dominica's draw is the mountains and the water.

Is Dominica good for a cruise day ashore?

For anyone who would rather see landscape than shop, it is one of the better eastern Caribbean port days, because the island's great sights are close to the coast. But we have to be honest about a gap: the Dominica Air and Sea Ports Authority's own website refuses automated readers, so the Guild has no sourced description of where cruise ships berth on this island, how many it takes or what is walkable from them, and the tourism authority's cruise pages would not load either. We are not going to describe a terminal we could not read about. Your cruise line's own port information is the version that counts. Our advice for the day itself is firmer: pick one direction - the mountains, the north and Cabrits, or the water - and do it properly.

Can you really see whales off Dominica?

A group of sperm whales lives in the waters off Dominica year-round, which is genuinely unusual and is the island's real distinction for anyone interested in large marine animals. That is the extent of what we were able to source, and so it is the extent of what we will tell you: we are not printing a season, a sighting rate, a trip price or any swim-with rule, because we could not read an official page that states them. Ask the operator directly, ask what happens if a trip is cancelled for weather, and treat any guarantee of a sighting with the suspicion it deserves.

What about Hurricane Maria - has the island recovered?

Maria made a direct Category 5 landfall on Dominica on 18 September 2017 with maximum sustained winds of 165 miles an hour, the first Category 5 hurricane on record to strike the country. Sixty-five people died there, early estimates suggested ninety per cent of the island's buildings were damaged or destroyed, and the loss was put at around 226 per cent of the country's annual economic output - published damage figures differ between about US$930 million and US$1.37 billion depending on the source. The island has rebuilt and is open, and the hotels on our city pages are trading. Two honest residues of it remain in this guide: we have left one east-coast eco-resort off our Roseau page because the only status notice on its own website is a closure notice from 2018, and we could not confirm which segments of the island-long Waitukubuli National Trail are open today.
