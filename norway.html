Norway

Norway Travel Guide

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Norway is a country of about five and a half million people stretched along one of the most broken-up coastlines on earth — so long that Oslo sits nearer to Rome than to its own northern tip, and so folded that its mainland shore, counting every fjord and island, runs to tens of thousands of miles. That shape explains almost everything about travelling here: why the train and the boat beat the car, why distances cost more time than the map suggests, and why the country behaves like three separate holidays — the capital, the western fjords, and the Arctic north.

Why visit Norway

A country of five and a half million people and an almost absurd amount of coastline, where the landscape does the work.

The fjords are not overrated

They are the real thing: sea water running fifty, eighty, a hundred miles inland between walls of rock a thousand metres high, with farms on ledges and waterfalls coming off the top. Geirangerfjord and the Naeroyfjord are on the UNESCO World Heritage list and deserve to be.

It is wild and it is easy

Very few countries let you stand somewhere that looks genuinely remote and still have a punctual train, a clean lavatory and a card reader within half an hour. Norway does this better than anywhere.

The light is the attraction

Midnight sun in the north from late May to late July; the aurora in the dark months; and in the south a long blue summer dusk that goes on for hours. You are travelling for a quality of light as much as for a place.

You are allowed to be there

The right to roam means the mountains, forests and shorelines are genuinely open to walk and camp on. Nothing is fenced off and sold back to you.

Norway at a glance

Capital

Oslo, at the head of a long fjord in the south-east, and home to about a seventh of the country.

Money

The Norwegian krone (NOK, kr). Not the euro. Cards and phones everywhere; cash is nearly obsolete.

Language

Norwegian, in two written forms. English is spoken fluently almost universally.

Borders

Not in the European Union; in Schengen and the EEA. Land borders with Sweden, Finland and Russia.

Getting about

Trains south and inland, ships and ferries along the coast, short flights to the north. All of it reliable.

Shape of it

Very long. Oslo to the North Cape is further than Oslo to Rome. Plan fewer places than you want to.

The regions

Four Norways, and most trips sensibly take one or two of them.

Oslo & the south-east

The capital, the long Oslofjord and the farm country behind it. Museums, modern architecture, the easiest arrival and the mildest weather in the country.

Things to do in Norway

The Bergen railway

Oslo to Bergen across the Hardangervidda plateau, about seven hours, and one of the finest train rides in Europe. No hiking required.

A fjord by boat

The Naeroyfjord from Flam, or the Geirangerfjord from Geiranger. Two hours on the water tells you more than a week of photographs.

The coastal ship

The working ferry up the coast from Bergen calls at three dozen small places over several days. You can take one leg of it as a day sail.

Preikestolen

The Pulpit Rock above the Lysefjord: a four-hour return walk on a well-made path, and the most photographed view in the country.

Chasing the light

Aurora hunting from Tromsø between late September and late March, or the midnight sun in June. Give either several nights.

Oslo’s museums

The Viking ships, the Fram polar vessel, the Munch collection and the Kon-Tiki rafts — a city that keeps its best stories indoors.

Best time to visit & getting there

Norway has two good seasons and they are nothing like each other. Summer, June through August, is when the country is fully open: every fjord ferry runs, the high mountain roads are clear, the hiking is on, and from late May to late July the sun does not set north of the Arctic Circle. It is also the busiest and dearest time, and Bergen and Geiranger take a good deal of cruise traffic.

The shoulders, May and September, are to our mind the connoisseur’s choice. Daylight is still long, the air is often clearer, prices step down noticeably and the famous places are calm. The price you pay is that some high roads and mountain lodges have not opened or have already shut, so you build the trip around the railway, the coast and the lower fjords rather than the plateau.

Winter is a real season, not a closed one, but it is two different things depending on latitude. In the south it means short days, Christmas markets and cross-country skiing within reach of Oslo. In the north it means the polar night, the aurora, dog sleds and whale watching — and a genuine need for proper clothing. Our month-by-month guide to the best time to visit Norway goes through it season by season.

Getting there is straightforward. Oslo takes long-haul and connecting flights from across Europe and some seasonal nonstops from North America; Bergen, Stavanger and Tromsø take European flights directly, and most North American travellers connect through Copenhagen, Amsterdam, Frankfurt, Reykjavik or London. If the fjords are the point, consider flying into Oslo and out of Bergen, or the reverse, rather than doubling back — airlines will usually price it as an open-jaw without penalty, and it saves you a day.

Norway uses the Norwegian krone (NOK, written kr), not the euro. Norway is not a member of the European Union, and euros are not accepted in shops. It is also one of the most nearly cashless countries in the world: cards and phones are taken everywhere, including on buses, in mountain huts and at market stalls, and many places no longer handle notes at all. You do not need to carry krone in cash. Wine and spirits are sold only through the state shops, Vinmonopolet, which close early and are shut on Sundays; beer is in supermarkets but stops being sold at a set hour in the evening. Tipping is not expected and never chased — rounding up a restaurant bill is plenty. Decline any card machine that offers to charge you in dollars; pay in krone and let your own bank convert.

Fishing here

Norway is one of the great fishing countries, and it comes in two halves. The salmon rivers of the west and north — the Gaula, the Namsen, the Alta — are fly water with a long and very formal tradition, where beats are let by the week and often years in advance. The sea is the other half, and it is wide open: cod, coalfish and halibut off almost any quay in the north, from a hired boat or a pier, with no licence needed for saltwater angling. Inland you need a national fishing fee for salmon, sea trout and migratory char, plus the local permit for the water you are standing in; for ordinary lake and river fishing for brown trout and perch, the local permit alone is usually enough. Our nearest guide on the subject is fly fishing in Iceland and Scandinavia, and the whole shelf is in The Guild Fishing Library.

Travelling in your later years

Norway is one of the easier countries in Europe to travel in slowly, and one of the harder ones to travel in cheaply.

The ground is kind

Lifts, ramps, level boarding and accessible lavatories are the rule rather than the exception, in trains, on the coastal ships and in most hotels built in the last forty years. Oslo’s airport express and its metro are step-free.

The boats do the walking

The single best way to see the fjords involves no walking at all. A fjord cruise, the Bergen railway and the coastal ferry give you the famous views from a seat, with a lavatory and a cup of coffee within reach.

Bergen’s old quarter is the exception

Bryggen’s wooden alleys are cobbled, sloping and uneven, and the funicular station involves steps. Lovely, and worth an hour; not somewhere to be hurried.

Daylight is the thing to plan around

In December, Oslo gets about six hours of light and Tromsø gets none at all. In June the sun barely sets. Neither is a problem if you choose it on purpose, and both are a shock if you do not.

Pharmacies and care are excellent

Standards are high and English is universal among medical staff. Bring your prescriptions in their original packaging with a copy of the prescription, as you would anywhere.

Budget honestly

Norway is expensive and no amount of planning makes it cheap. Where it does reward care is in eating: a hotel breakfast is usually generous and included, bakeries are good and cheap, and a supermarket lunch outdoors is a perfectly respectable Norwegian habit.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS — the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years — is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today — nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Norway is the case readers get wrong most often. It is not in the European Union, but it is in the Schengen area, so everything above applies here in full — the border checks, the Entry/Exit System, and ETIAS when it comes. Flying Oslo to Copenhagen or Stockholm is an internal Schengen hop with no passport control. What Norway’s position outside the EU does change is customs: there are duty limits on what you may bring in, and the allowance for alcohol and tobacco is modest and genuinely enforced.

Norway travel FAQ

When is the best time to visit Norway?

It depends which Norway you want. For the fjords, the hiking and the long light, June to August is the season: everything is open, the mountain roads are clear of snow, and north of the Arctic Circle the sun does not set. May and September are quieter, cheaper and often clearer, with the trade-off that some high roads and mountain lodges are shut. For the northern lights you want the dark half of the year, roughly late September to late March, and you want to be in the north. Winter in the south is not a closed season, but it is a short-daylight one: Oslo in late December gets about six hours of light.

Is Norway as expensive as people say?

Yes. It is one of the most expensive countries in Europe and visitors are regularly caught out by it, particularly on restaurant meals, alcohol and taxis. Wine and spirits are sold only in the state Vinmonopolet shops and are taxed heavily. What takes the sting out of it is that a great deal of the best of Norway is free: the walking, the right to roam, the views from the train and the ferry. Hotel breakfasts are usually included and substantial enough to carry you to the evening, bakeries are inexpensive, and eating one main meal a day out rather than two makes a real difference.

Do I need a car in Norway?

Less often than you would think. The Oslo to Bergen railway, the branch down to Flam, the coastal ships and the fjord ferries between them cover the classic trip without a car, and parking in the cities is expensive and awkward. Where a car earns its keep is the inland valleys, the Atlantic Road and the far north, where buses are sparse and the pleasure is stopping where you like. If you do drive, note that many tunnels, bridges and ferries are tolled automatically by number plate and billed to the hire company afterwards.

Is Norway in the European Union?

No, and this surprises people every year. Norway is not an EU member. It is, however, in the Schengen area and in the European Economic Area, so border formalities are the same as for the EU countries: the Entry/Exit System applies, there is no passport check flying in from Copenhagen or Amsterdam, and ETIAS will apply here too when it starts. The practical differences are the currency, which is the krone and not the euro, and customs allowances, which are modest and genuinely enforced.

What is the right to roam?

Allemannsretten, the right of all men, is a genuine legal right to walk, ski and camp on uncultivated land, including private land, as long as you keep clear of houses, close gates behind you, light no fires in the dry season and leave nothing at all. You may pitch a tent for up to two nights in one spot without asking, if you are more than 150 metres from the nearest inhabited house. It is one of the great pleasures of the country and it is taken seriously in both directions: the access is real, and so is the expectation that you behave.

How do I see the fjords if I only have a few days?

The honest answer is the Bergen railway and one fjord. Take the train from Oslo to Myrdal, the branch line down to Flam, a boat up the Naeroyfjord, and the bus and train back up to the main line. It is a long day, it is almost all sitting down, and it shows you the two things people come for: the mountain plateau and a deep, narrow, UNESCO-listed arm of water. Trying to add Geiranger and the Lofoten islands to the same four days is how people end up seeing Norway through a coach window.

Will I see the northern lights?

Possibly, if you go north in the dark months and give it time. The aurora needs three things at once: darkness, solar activity and a clear sky. Nobody can promise you all three on a given night, and anyone who does is selling something. Give yourself at least three or four nights in Tromso, Alta or the Lofoten islands between late September and late March, get away from town lights, and treat a sighting as a gift rather than an itinerary item. The same trip in daylight gives you snow, sea and mountains that are worth the fare on their own.

How many days do you need?

Seven days is the comfortable minimum for Oslo, the Bergen railway and one fjord. Ten lets you add Stavanger and the Lysefjord, or slow the whole thing down to a sensible pace. Two weeks gets you the north as well, which is a different country again. Norway is long, longer than most maps suggest, and the single commonest mistake is treating Oslo and Tromso as a comfortable day apart when the flight alone is about two hours.
