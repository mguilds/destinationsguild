Jurmala

Where to Stay in Jurmala

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Jurmala is a twenty-kilometre strip of white sand, dune and pine forest on the Gulf of Riga, half an hour west of the capital by train, and it has been Latvia’s seaside since the nineteenth century. It is not one town but a string of them along a single railway line, with wooden villas and old sanatoria scattered through the trees behind the beach and the Lielupe river running parallel a few hundred metres inland. The sea is shallow, calm and bracing, the sand is firm enough to walk for hours, and the whole place is flat. Which station you get off at decides what sort of holiday you have.

Where to stay in Jurmala, area by area

The parts of Jurmala a visitor actually chooses between, and who each one suits.

Majori: the busy middle

Most centralMost to eatBusiest in summer

The heart of the resort and the station most visitors get off at. Jomas iela, the long pedestrian street a block behind the dunes, has the restaurants, the cafes, the ice cream and the summer crowds, and the beach at the end of it is the widest and most used in Jurmala. Most of the hotels and apartments are here or within a few streets. If you want to be able to walk to dinner and not think about transport, stay in Majori; if you want quiet in July, do not.

Dzintari: the concert hall and the forest park

Best balancePine woodsWalk to Majori

Immediately east of Majori and a great deal calmer, with the open-air Dzintari Concert Hall, which has been the summer music venue of the Latvian coast for generations, and the Dzintari Forest Park behind it — boardwalks through the pines and a wooden observation tower you can climb for the view over the trees to the sea. The beach here is as good as Majori’s and emptier, and Jomas iela is a ten-minute walk away when you want it. This is the area we would choose.

Bulduri and Lielupe: the Riga end

Closest to RigaGrand old villasQuietest beach

The first stretch you reach coming out from the city, where the strip is at its widest and the old wooden villas are at their grandest — carved verandas, towers and fretwork under the pines, some restored and some quietly rotting. It is residential, quiet and green, the beach is almost empty on a weekday, and the train into Riga takes a little over twenty minutes. There are fewer places to eat than in Majori and that is rather the point.

Dubulti and Jaundubulti: the narrow waist

Sea and riverVery quietFew restaurants

Where the spit narrows to a few hundred metres between the Gulf of Riga on one side and the Lielupe river on the other, so you can walk from surf to river bank in ten minutes. Dubulti has the extraordinary 1970s concrete church on the hill and a station of the same vintage. It is the quietest of the central districts, with apartments rather than hotels, and it suits a longer stay or anybody who wants the water on both sides and nothing much else.

Kemeri: the bog, the springs and the ruin

Nature reserveAlmost nothing to bookDay trip

The far western end of the line, and a different place altogether: Kemeri National Park, a raised bog with an hour-long boardwalk loop across it, sulphur springs that smell exactly as advertised, and the vast abandoned Kemeri sanatorium standing empty in the woods. It is the best half day in Jurmala. What it is not is a place to sleep — our partner holds almost nothing out here, and we would rather say so than point a booking button at an empty list. Come out on the train from Majori and go back the same way.

Getting around Jurmala

The train from Riga

The reason Jurmala works. Trains run from Riga Central out along the coast several times an hour at peak times, taking about half an hour to Majori, and every district in this guide has its own station on the one line. It is cheap, frequent and far easier than driving.

On foot and by bicycle

The resort is twenty kilometres long and a few hundred metres wide, so it is all one promenade: a cycle path runs most of the length of it behind the dunes, and the beach itself is firm, flat sand you can walk for hours. Bicycles are easy to hire in Majori in season. Nothing in Jurmala is hilly.

Driving, and the entry charge

Jurmala charges a daily fee to bring a car into the resort during the season, paid online or at machines, and it is enforced. If you are driving, look it up before you arrive rather than after; if you are not, the train avoids the question entirely. Parking in Majori in July is its own small misery.

Getting to the airport

Riga’s airport is on the same side of the city as Jurmala, so the journey is shorter than the map suggests — a taxi or ride-hailing car takes well under an hour from the central districts. By public transport you change in Riga, which makes it a good deal longer.

When to come

Jurmala has two quite different lives. July and August are the season: the Baltic is finally warm enough to swim in, Jomas iela is full, the concert programme at Dzintari is running and the hotels are at their fullest and dearest. Book ahead for those weeks, especially around midsummer. May, June and September are our own preference — the pine woods and the long empty beach without the crowds, the light at its best, and rooms a good deal cheaper, with the water too cold for anything but a brave dip. October to April is the resort out of season, and it is genuinely lovely in its own way: wind, grey sea, almost nobody on twenty kilometres of sand, and the spa hotels running at winter rates. The sea ices at the edges in a hard winter.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Latvia is in the European Union, the Schengen area and the euro, so arriving from anywhere else in Schengen there is no check at all. Month-by-month weather and the dates worth planning around are in Best Time to Visit Latvia, and the country guide is The Latvia Travel Guide.

Jurmala — questions

Is Jurmala a day trip or a place to stay?

Both work, and it depends on the season. In July and August it is a proper resort worth two or three nights, with the beach, the pine parks and the concert hall giving you enough to do. Out of season it makes an excellent half day from Riga: take the train to Majori, walk the beach to Dzintari and the forest tower, and go back for dinner in the city. Many visitors do it the other way round in summer, basing themselves on the coast and treating Riga as the day trip, which is also sensible.

How far is it from Riga, and how do I get there?

About twenty-five kilometres west of the city centre, and the train is the answer: services run from Riga Central several times an hour at busy times and take roughly half an hour to Majori, the main stop. Every district in this guide has its own station on the same line, so you can get off wherever your hotel is. Driving is possible but the resort charges a daily entry fee for cars in season and parking in the middle is difficult in high summer.

Which part of Jurmala is best?

Dzintari, for most people: it has the concert hall and the forest park, the beach is as good as the busy one next door, and Majori’s restaurants are a ten-minute walk away. Stay in Majori itself if you want everything on your doorstep and do not mind the crowds, in Bulduri if you want old villas and an empty beach close to Riga, and in Dubulti if you want the narrow strip with water on both sides and very little else.

Is the water warm enough to swim in?

In July and August, yes, and the Gulf of Riga is noticeably warmer than the open Baltic because it is sheltered and extremely shallow a long way out — you can wade for a hundred metres and still be at your waist, which makes it good for children and dull for anybody who wants to swim properly. In June it is bracing. In May it is for paddling. The sand is white, firm and clean, and the beach has long held blue-flag status in the main districts.

Is there anything to do if it rains?

A fair amount. Several of the hotels run full spa and treatment programmes, which is what the resort was founded on in the nineteenth century and still does properly. There is a water park, the concert hall has an indoor season, and there are small museums in the old wooden villas. And the pine woods behind the dunes are rather good in the rain, which is just as well, because this is the Baltic coast.

What is at Kemeri, and is it worth the extra stops?

Yes, and it is the best thing in Jurmala that is not the beach. Kemeri is the far end of the railway line and the edge of a national park: a boardwalk loop across a raised bog with its own black pools and stunted pines, sulphur springs, and the enormous abandoned Kemeri sanatorium sitting empty among the trees like something from a film. Go in the morning when the mist is still on the water. There is almost nowhere to stay out there, so treat it as a trip rather than a base.
