Where to Stay in Bratislava

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Bratislava is the only national capital in the world with land borders onto two other countries, and once you know that, the city makes sense. Austria is on the far bank of the Danube; Hungary is twenty minutes down the road; Vienna is an hour away by bus and is often the cheaper way to fly in. The city itself is two things stitched together: a small, dense, thoroughly restored old town of pastel houses and cobbled lanes under a white castle, and a working post-socialist capital of embankments, tram lines and the enormous Petrzalka housing estates across the river. The old town can be walked end to end in fifteen minutes, which is why so many people see it as a four-hour stop off a Danube cruise or a Vienna day trip. That is the mistake. Give it two nights, eat and drink properly, get out to Devin where the rivers meet, and it is one of the better-value city breaks in Europe.

Where to stay in Bratislava, area by area

The parts of Bratislava a visitor actually chooses between, and who each one suits.

The Guild does not name a hotel until an Explorer has confirmed it on the hotel’s own site and on Trip.com. Until then these are areas, not recommendations: pick the district that suits you, then browse what our partner actually holds there.

Stare Mesto: the old town inside the walls

Most centralWalk everywhereCobbles

The square half-mile that holds nearly everything a first visit wants: the main square with the old town hall, Michael’s Gate and the one surviving stretch of the medieval walls, St Martin’s Cathedral where eleven Hungarian kings and eight queens were crowned between 1563 and 1830, the Primate’s Palace with its hall of English tapestries, and the cafe terraces on Hviezdoslav square. It is compact, it is almost entirely pedestrian, and it is cobbled throughout in the original uneven way. Stay here if it is your first visit and you want to walk out of the door into the city. The trade is noise: the lanes around Rybarska brana and Sedlarska carry the stag-party trade on a Friday and Saturday night, which is a real thing in Bratislava and worth asking about when you book.

The castle hill and the embassy streets below it

QuieterViewsA climb

The slope between the old town and the castle, running up through Zamocka and the streets around Palisady, is where the embassies and the better small hotels are. It is five to ten minutes’ walk down into the old town and a steady climb back up, which is the whole trade-off. You get quiet nights, trees, the castle terrace with the best view in the city over the Danube and the Petrzalka towers, and the Slavin war memorial on the hill above. The one thing to check before booking is how much of the climb your particular street involves, because some of it is steps.

The riverside at Eurovea and the Danube embankment

ModernFlatRiverside walk

East of the old town the Danube bank has been rebuilt as Eurovea, a long run of newer hotels, apartments, offices, a shopping centre and the national theatre, with a wide flat promenade along the water. It is the obvious choice if you want a modern, large, lift-served hotel room rather than a converted townhouse, if you are travelling with a wheeled case and would rather not fight cobbles, or if the river is the point. It is ten to fifteen minutes on foot to the main square along the embankment, level the whole way. It has no old-town atmosphere at all, which for some visitors is exactly the appeal.

Petrzalka, across the bridge

CheapestTram and busNot pretty

The vast concrete housing district on the south bank, built in the 1970s and 1980s and home to something like a fifth of the city. It is where the cheapest rooms are, it is a short walk or a few minutes on the bus or tram across the SNP bridge into the centre, and it is genuinely safe and ordinary — people live here, it is not a problem district. It is also, if you have any interest in twentieth-century Europe, one of the most instructive pieces of urban history in the region, and the Sad Janka Krala park along the river is the oldest public park in central Europe. Pick it for the price and the river walk, not for the charm.

The Small Carpathian wine villages, north of the city

VineyardsCar usefulQuiet

A line of villages runs up the eastern slope of the Small Carpathians immediately north of Bratislava — Raca inside the city boundary, then Svaty Jur, Pezinok and Modra — and they are the oldest wine country in Slovakia. Svaty Jur is a tiny walled town with a Roman ruin and a nature reserve above it; Pezinok and Modra have the bigger cellars and the open-cellar weekends. Trains and buses run up from the city in twenty to forty minutes. Stay out here if you want vineyards and village pensions rather than a capital city, and treat Bratislava as a day out. Trnava, half an hour further on, is a walled town of a dozen churches and makes a better base again if you have a car.

Getting around Bratislava

Trams, trolleybuses and buses

One integrated network and no metro — the metro was started in the 1980s and abandoned, which is why the SNP bridge has a bus deck under the road. Trams cover the centre and the embankments, trolleybuses do the hills, buses do everything else and cross to Petrzalka. Tickets are bought from machines at the stops or by app, and are time-based rather than journey-based, so one ticket covers changes within its window. Validate it when you board. In practice a visitor staying in or near the old town walks almost everywhere and uses the tram twice.

Getting to and from Bratislava Airport

Bratislava Airport (BTS) is about nine kilometres north-east of the centre, and city bus 61 runs from the terminal to the main railway station in roughly twenty-five minutes for the price of an ordinary city ticket. Taxis are cheap by western European standards; book through an app rather than taking the first car at the rank. The terminal is small and the timetable is modest, mostly low-cost routes to Britain, Italy and the eastern Mediterranean.

Vienna Airport, which is often the better answer

This is the single most useful thing to know about arriving in Bratislava. Vienna Airport is about 50 kilometres away and direct coaches run from the terminal to Bratislava city centre in around an hour, several times an hour through the day. Vienna has far more European and long-haul connections and the fares are frequently lower. Check both airports before you book, and if you are flying from North America, Vienna will almost certainly be the sensible way in.

Trains and coaches to the neighbours

Bratislava hlavna stanica is the main station, a few minutes above the old town, and Bratislava-Petrzalka is the second one, used by some Vienna services. Vienna is about an hour by train, with two different routes and a cheap day-return ticket that covers Vienna city transport as well. Budapest is around two and a half hours, Prague about four, Kosice five to six on an InterCity. Coaches cover the same routes, often faster and cheaper on the Vienna and Budapest runs.

The Danube boat, and getting out to Devin

In the warmer half of the year passenger boats run down the Danube to Devin, where the Morava joins the river under a ruined castle on the Austrian border, and upstream to Vienna. Devin is also a half-hour city bus ride and is the best single excursion from Bratislava — a Great Moravian stronghold, a Roman outpost before that, blown up by Napoleon’s troops in 1809, and for forty years the edge of the Eastern bloc with a memorial to the people shot trying to cross. Check the boat season before you plan around it; it does not run in winter.

When to come

Bratislava works in any month, which is not true of the Slovak mountains, so the question is what you want. May, June, September and early October are the best weeks: warm days, the embankment and the square full of tables, the Danube boats running and the vineyards above the city at their best. July and August are warm to hot — the Danube lowland is the warmest part of Slovakia — and busy with cruise passengers and day trippers from Vienna, though the city empties of locals. Late November and December bring the Christmas markets on the main square and Hviezdoslav square, which are smaller, cheaper and far less crammed than Vienna’s an hour away and are a genuine reason to come in winter. January to March is grey, cold and the cheapest time of year in the city.

One practical note for a short visit: the old-town lanes around Rybarska brana carry a loud weekend drinking trade, and the cheap flights that feed it land on Friday. If you are a light sleeper and your dates include a Friday or Saturday, ask the hotel which side of the building your room is on, or take the castle hill or the embankment instead.

Slovakia has used the euro since 1 January 2009 — it was the first of the central European countries to adopt it and the second post-communist state after Slovenia, so there is no Slovak koruna to change and anything quoting one is describing the country before 2009. Cards are taken almost everywhere a visitor goes and contactless is normal on city transport; keep a little cash for a mountain hut, a village pub, a chairlift ticket office in the smaller valleys and a church collection box.

Getting in: what applies, and what does not

Slovakia is in the European Union, in the Schengen area since 21 December 2007 and in the euro since 1 January 2009, so arriving from Austria, Czechia, Poland or Hungary is an internal journey with no routine passport check and nothing to change. The rules below apply when you first enter the Schengen area from outside it.

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen external border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS - the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years - is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today - nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave. United States, Canadian, British and Australian citizens may visit as tourists without a visa for up to ninety days in any one hundred and eighty, counted across the whole Schengen area rather than against Slovakia alone.

We could not read the United States travel advisory for Slovakia on the day this page was written — the State Department’s site would not answer us — so we are not printing a level here rather than quoting one from memory. Read the current advisory yourself at travel.state.gov before you go; the level and the wording change, and it is a two-minute check.

Bratislava — questions

Is Bratislava worth more than a day trip from Vienna?

Yes, and the day-trip framing is the reason so many people come away unimpressed. Four hours gets you the main square, the cathedral and a coffee, which is pleasant and not much. Two nights gets you Devin where two rivers and three countries meet, the castle terrace at dusk, the Slavin memorial, the Blue Church, the Danube embankments, the Small Carpathian vineyards twenty minutes north, and an eating and drinking scene that is much better and much cheaper than its reputation. It is also the base for the rest of Slovakia, which is the real prize.

Which area should I stay in?

First visit, two or three nights, happy on cobbles: the old town, and you will walk to everything. Want quiet and do not mind a climb: the castle hill streets. Want a modern lift-served hotel, a flat approach and a wheeled case: the Eurovea riverside. Want the cheapest bed in the city and do not care how it looks: Petrzalka across the bridge, which is ordinary, safe and a few minutes from the centre. Want vineyards instead of a city: Svaty Jur, Pezinok or Modra up the slope to the north.

Should I fly into Bratislava or Vienna?

Price both. Bratislava Airport is nine kilometres from the centre with a twenty-five-minute city bus, which is convenient, but its timetable is small and mostly low-cost. Vienna Airport is about 50 kilometres away with direct coaches to central Bratislava in around an hour, and it has vastly more routes including the long-haul ones. For most visitors from outside Europe, Vienna will be both cheaper and quicker despite being in another country — and because both are inside Schengen, there is no border formality either way.

How do I get to Devin Castle?

City bus 28 from the Novy Most stop below the SNP bridge takes about half an hour and runs frequently; in the warmer half of the year a passenger boat goes down the Danube from the embankment, which is the nicer way and takes a little longer. The site is a hilltop ruin with a steep cobbled approach and steps, so it is a real walk rather than a stroll. Allow a half day including the journey and check the opening season, because it closes for the winter.

Is the old town noisy at night?

In parts, yes. Bratislava picked up a weekend stag-party trade on the back of cheap flights, and it concentrates in a few lanes in the old town — Rybarska brana and the streets around it — on Friday and Saturday nights. It is not a safety problem and it is nothing like the whole old town, but a room with a window over the wrong lane will know about it. Ask when you book, or stay on the castle hill or the embankment.

Do I need cash?

Not much. Slovakia has used the euro since 2009, cards are taken almost everywhere and contactless works on the trams and in the ticket machines. Keep a few coins for a public toilet, a church collection box, a market stall at the Christmas markets or a village pub up in the vineyards.

Can I walk to Austria?

You can cycle there easily and walk there with some effort, which is the sort of thing only Bratislava offers. The Danube cycle path runs from the city to the Austrian village of Hainburg and on to Vienna, and there is a cycle and foot bridge at Devinska Nova Ves over the Morava into Austria, built on the piers of a bridge destroyed in 1945 and reopened in 2012. Both are inside Schengen, so there is nothing to show and nobody to show it to. Take a passport anyway.

What should I eat and drink here?

Start with bryndzove halusky, the sheep-cheese dumplings, and with lokse, the thin potato flatbreads, which in Bratislava are street food and at Christmas come with goose fat or poppyseed. The old town has a good run of modern Slovak restaurants that are a fraction of Vienna’s prices. Drink the local beer, and then the wine: the Small Carpathian vineyards start inside the city boundary at Raca, and a Bratislava trip that does not include an afternoon in a Pezinok or Modra cellar has missed something.
