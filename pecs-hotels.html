Pécs

Where to Stay in Pécs

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Pécs sits in the warm south of Hungary, under the Mecsek hills and about three hours from Budapest, and it has had more lives than most cities its size. It was Roman Sopianae, and the painted early Christian tombs left from that time are a UNESCO site; it was Ottoman for a century and a half, and the mosque of Pasha Qasim still stands on the main square, converted into a Catholic church with its dome and mihrab intact; and it made its money in the nineteenth century from coal and from the Zsolnay porcelain works, whose glazed tiles are on roofs all over the country. The old town is small, walkable and almost entirely flat; the hills start at its back door.

The old town — Széchenyi tér and the streets inside the old walls

Most centralWalk everywhereMild evening noise

The right choice for almost every visitor. Széchenyi tér slopes up to the green dome of the former mosque, and the pedestrian streets off it — Király utca with its cafes, Ferencesek utcája, Janus Pannonius utca — hold the restaurants, the bars and most of the small hotels. Everything a short visit wants is inside ten minutes’ walk: the mosque, the synagogue, the cathedral quarter, the museums on Káptalan utca and the remaining stretch of the city wall. Rooms are mostly in converted townhouses and are inexpensive. Pécs is a university city, so Király utca has life in it until late in term time, but it is student noise rather than stag parties and it does not carry far.

The cathedral quarter — Dóm tér and Káptalan utca

QuietestMuseums at the doorA few minutes further out

The north-western corner of the old town, where the four-towered cathedral stands on its terrace above the Roman tombs and the bishop’s palace faces it across the square. Káptalan utca, running east from it, is a single street of museums, including the Zsolnay collection and the Vasarely house. The Cella Septichora visitor centre below the square takes you down into the painted burial chambers that earned the UNESCO listing. Staying here means silence at night, a five-minute walk to the main square and the best of the city on your doorstep. There are fewer rooms and fewer places to eat, so it suits a second visit or a traveller who would rather be quiet than central.

The Zsolnay Quarter — the old porcelain works

Family-friendlyParkingTwenty minutes to the square

East of the centre, the Zsolnay factory site was rebuilt in 2010 as a cultural quarter: workshops, a planetarium, a children’s museum, the family mausoleum and a great many of the firm’s eosin-glazed ceramics in the open air. A few larger, newer hotels sit in and around it, with lifts, parking and family rooms of a kind the old town cannot offer. It is a flat twenty-minute walk or a short bus ride into the middle. Choose it if you are driving, travelling with children or want a modern room; do not choose it if your plan is to wander out for dinner, because the evening life is all back in the old town.

Up the Mecsek — Tettye and the hill above the city

Views and woodsCooler in summerA real climb

The Mecsek hills rise immediately behind Pécs and the streets climb into them within a few hundred metres of the cathedral. Tettye, with its ruined renaissance villa and small park, is the first terrace up; above that the road winds into beech woods and out to the lookout tower on Misina, with the whole city and the plain beyond it laid out below. A handful of guesthouses and small hotels are scattered up here. They are quiet, cool in the summer heat and often excellent value, and the walk down into town takes ten or fifteen minutes. The walk back up is the catch, and it is steep; a bus and a taxi both solve it cheaply.

Getting around

Pécs is a walking city. The old town is small, largely pedestrian and almost flat, and the furthest you are likely to walk inside it is ten minutes. The hills behind are the only gradient worth thinking about, and the Zsolnay Quarter is the only sight that is a real distance from the middle.

Buses cover everything else, including the climb to Tettye and the Mecsek, the university campuses and the Zsolnay Quarter. Tickets are sold at kiosks and machines or bought on board at a higher price, and are validated as you board. There are no trams in Pécs.

Trains from Budapest take about three hours from Budapest-Déli or Kelenföld; buses take a similar time and run frequently. Both stations are a short walk or one bus stop south of the old town. A car is worth having for the Villány wine villages, Siklós and the thermal baths at Harkány, all within half an hour to the south, and for the Mecsek roads; in the old town it is a nuisance, with paid parking and pedestrian streets.

When to come, and what it costs

Pécs is the warmest city in Hungary and the season runs longer here than anywhere else in the country. Late April to June is beautiful, with the Mecsek green and the squares in use. September and October are our pick, because the Villány harvest is on half an hour down the road and the city is still warm into the evening. July and August are hot and can be heavy, though the hills behind the city give you somewhere cooler to go. Winter is milder than Budapest but grey, and the city is quiet outside term time.

Pécs is inexpensive. Rooms in the old town cost a good deal less than Budapest, the student cafes keep prices honest, and museum tickets are cheap, with combined tickets available for the Káptalan utca houses. The weeks to book early are the September harvest weekends and the university’s own busy dates. Hungary uses the Hungarian forint (HUF, written Ft), not the euro. Hungary has been in the European Union since 2004 but has kept its own currency and has set no date for changing it, so euro notes are not legal tender. A few hotels and tour desks quote in euros and some will take euro cash, almost always at a poor rate. Pay in forint. Cards and phones are taken nearly everywhere, including on buses, trams and at ticket machines, and you need very little cash — a small amount is handy for market stalls, thermal-bath lockers, church donation boxes and rural buses. Prices in shops include tax. In Budapest restaurants a service charge is often printed on the bill; check before adding a tip, and where there is none, ten per cent is plenty. Decline any card machine offering to charge you in dollars and let your own bank convert. Avoid the standalone exchange booths near the main tourist streets and at the airport, which are far worse than a bank machine or an ordinary exchange office in town.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS — the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years — is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today — nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Hungary is a straightforward case. It has been in the European Union since 2004 and in the Schengen area since the end of 2007, so everything above applies here in full, and flying in from Vienna, Munich or Amsterdam involves no passport control at all. What Hungary has not joined is the euro, so the currency is the forint. Its borders with Austria, Slovakia, Slovenia, Croatia and Romania are all internal Schengen crossings now and you will usually drive or ride straight through them. The borders with Serbia and with Ukraine are external Schengen frontiers, where there are proper checks and the queues can be long. None of that affects an ordinary trip to the places in this guide.

Staying in Pécs — questions

Is Pecs worth the three-hour journey from Budapest?

Yes, if you give it two nights. Pecs packs an unusual amount into a small flat old town: painted early Christian burial chambers from Roman Sopianae that carry a UNESCO listing, an Ottoman mosque that became a Catholic church and still looks like a mosque, a four-towered cathedral, a street of good museums and the Zsolnay porcelain works. Add the Villany vineyards half an hour south and it is a proper short break rather than a day trip.

Where should I stay in Pecs?

The old town around Szechenyi ter for a first and a short visit: you will walk to everything and eat where the locals do. The cathedral quarter if you want quiet and the museums on the doorstep. The Zsolnay Quarter if you are driving, have children or want a modern room with parking. Up the Mecsek if you want views and cool air and do not mind a climb or a short bus ride.

What is the mosque on the main square?

The mosque of Pasha Qasim, built in the sixteenth century during the Ottoman occupation on the site of an earlier church, and converted back into a Catholic church after the Ottomans left. The dome, the prayer niche and the Ottoman proportions are all still there under the Christian fittings, which makes it one of the most striking buildings in Hungary and the symbol of the city. It is in use as a church, so dress and behave accordingly and check service times.

What are the Roman tombs?

Pecs was the Roman town of Sopianae, and a cluster of fourth-century Christian burial chambers survives beneath the cathedral square, several with painted walls. They were listed by UNESCO in 2000 and the main group is shown through the Cella Septichora visitor centre, where walkways let you look down into the chambers. Allow an hour or so; it is cool down there, which is welcome in August.

Can I visit the Villany wine region from Pecs?

Easily. Villany is about thirty kilometres south and is Hungary's best known red-wine district, with a main street of cellars in the village itself and more in Villanykovesd nearby. There are buses and a train, but they are infrequent and awkward for a tasting, so most visitors either drive, which raises the obvious problem, or book a trip with a driver from Pecs. Harkany, with its thermal baths, and the castle at Siklos are on the same road.

How long does the train take from Budapest?

About three hours from Budapest-Deli or Kelenfold, with direct trains through the day; intercity buses take a similar time. Both the railway and bus stations are a short walk south of the old town. Fares are low, and a seat reservation is worth having on a Friday or Sunday.

Is Pecs hilly?

The old town is not; it slopes gently and is easy to walk. The hills begin immediately behind it, and anything above the cathedral - Tettye, the Mecsek lookout, the upper guesthouses - involves a real climb. Buses and cheap taxis handle that for anyone who would rather not walk up. The old town's streets are paved rather than roughly cobbled, which makes Pecs easier underfoot than Eger or the Buda castle quarter.

How many days do you need?

One full day covers the mosque, the cathedral, the Roman tombs and Kaptalan utca. Two nights lets you add the Zsolnay Quarter and an evening on Kiraly utca without hurrying. Three if you want a day in Villany or up in the Mecsek. It is a city that rewards slowing down more than most.
