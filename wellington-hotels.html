Wellington

Where to Stay in Wellington

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Wellington is the most walkable city in New Zealand and the windiest, and both facts come from the same geography: a harbour city squeezed between hills and the mouth of Cook Strait. The flat part of town, which is where you want to stay, is a strip perhaps a mile and a half long between the railway station and Courtenay Place, and nearly everything a visitor wants is in it. The real choice here is not distance but character: the business end, the waterfront, or the one that stays up late.

Where to stay in Wellington, area by area

Four parts of a very compact capital, and who each one suits.

The Guild does not name a hotel until an Explorer has confirmed it on the hotel’s own website, so this guide sets out the areas and sends you to the full, current list for each one rather than printing a shortlist we cannot stand behind yet. Named picks will be added to this page as they are checked.

Lambton Quay and the business centre

Most convenientStep-freeWeekend value

The old shoreline, now the main shopping and office street, running from the railway station to the top of town. This is the practical base: the station for the Cook Strait ferry bus and the Northern Explorer train, the cable car up to the botanic gardens, the parliament buildings and the main bus routes are all on it or just off it, and the ground is flat the whole way. It is also where most of the larger hotels are, which means rates drop at weekends and over the summer when the public servants and the conferences go home. Quiet on a Sunday, in both the good and the dull sense.

The waterfront and Te Papa

Harbour viewsMuseumsWalk to everything

A continuous paved promenade runs the length of the harbour from the station past the lagoon to Oriental Bay, with Te Papa, the national museum, in the middle of it. Staying on or just behind this strip gives you the best of the city on your doorstep and a completely level walk in both directions, which makes it our first suggestion for anyone who would rather not take on Wellington's hills. Oriental Bay at the eastern end is the handsome residential corner, with a small beach and the best views back at the city. Rooms with a harbour view cost accordingly.

Te Aro, Cuba Street and Courtenay Place

Food and barsLivelyNoisy at weekends

The southern end of the flat city, and the reason Wellington has the reputation it does for coffee, small restaurants and bars. Cuba Street is the city's best street for walking, with its bucket fountain, record shops and cafes; Courtenay Place at its eastern end is the late-night strip. Accommodation is boutique hotels, apartments and hostels rather than towers, and it is five to ten minutes' flat walk from Te Papa and fifteen from the station. The honest warning is noise: Friday and Saturday nights around Courtenay Place are loud until the small hours, so ask for a room away from the street.

Thorndon, Kelburn and the ferry side

QuieterGardensHills or the cable car

North and above the centre. Thorndon is the oldest part of the city, with timber villas and a village high street, close to parliament and the start of the walk up to the botanic gardens; Kelburn sits on the ridge above it, reached by the cable car, with the university and the best outlook over the harbour. Rooms here are mostly guesthouses and apartments and are generally better value for space, and the Interislander ferry terminal is a short drive north. The trade-off is the obvious one: these are hills, and the climb home is real.

Getting around

Wellington is the one New Zealand city where walking genuinely covers it. The flat strip from the railway station to Courtenay Place takes about twenty-five minutes end to end and almost every museum, theatre and restaurant is on or beside it. Buses radiate from Lambton Quay and the station to the suburbs, paid with a Snapper card or by contactless card, and four suburban train lines run out of the station up the valleys. The cable car from Lambton Quay to Kelburn is both a tram and the easy way to the top of the botanic gardens - ride up, walk down.

The airport is a short drive south-east through a tunnel, with a bus and plentiful taxis; there is no train. Two things to know about the weather rather than the transport: Wellington's wind is not a joke, and a gale can cancel flights and ferry sailings at short notice, so leave a margin around a Cook Strait crossing or a connection. Take a jacket you can walk into a headwind in, and do not bother with an umbrella - the locals do not.

When to come, and what it costs

Summer, December to February, is the best of Wellington: the harbour at its bluest, Oriental Bay in use, the outdoor festivals running and the city emptier than usual as locals take their own holidays. March is our pick, with settled weather, warm water and the city back at work. Winter, June to August, is mild by most standards and genuinely wet and windy, but Wellington is an indoor city at heart - museums, theatres, bookshops, long lunches - and it does winter better than most.

On price, this is a government and conference town, so midweek is dearest and weekends are the bargain, especially outside summer. Avoid the weekends of a major event at Sky Stadium, when the hotels fill and rates jump. Our month-by-month guide to New Zealand sets out the seasons across both islands.

Getting in: what you need, and what you pay

Most visitors from visa-waiver countries need a New Zealand Electronic Travel Authority, the NZeTA, requested before you fly, and most also pay the International Visitor Conservation and Tourism Levy, which is normally collected at the same time. A separate New Zealand Traveller Declaration is completed shortly before you arrive and has replaced the old paper arrival card.

Both the NZeTA fee and the levy have changed more than once in recent years, so we do not print the figures here — read them off the official page at immigration.govt.nz when you book, and request the NZeTA through Immigration New Zealand’s own app or website rather than any agent offering to do it for you. Australian citizens need neither; Australian permanent residents who are not citizens do need the NZeTA. The airline checks it before you board, so do it when you book rather than at the airport.

Take the biosecurity questions on arrival seriously: declare all food, and clean the soil off boots, tents and fishing tackle before you pack them. Declaring something costs nothing; not declaring it can cost you an instant fine. The full picture is in our New Zealand travel guide.

Staying in Wellington — questions

Is Wellington really that windy?

Yes. The city sits in the funnel between the North and South Islands and the average wind speed is among the highest of any capital in the world; a day of forty knots is not remarkable. It is a practical matter rather than a danger: wear something windproof, expect your hair to lose, do not buy an umbrella, and build a little slack into any plan that depends on a flight or a ferry sailing, because both are cancelled for wind several times a year.

How many days do you need in Wellington?

Two full days covers the city comfortably: one for Te Papa, the waterfront and Cuba Street, one for the cable car, the botanic gardens, parliament and Zealandia or the Weta workshop tours. A third day lets you add the Wairarapa wine country over the hill, a harbour ferry to Days Bay, or the seal colony out at the south coast. Many visitors give it one night on their way to the ferry, which is a pity - it is the best small city in the country for eating and museums.

Where should I stay if I am taking the Cook Strait ferry?

Anywhere in the flat centre. Both ferry companies run a connecting shuttle from near the railway station, and the terminals are a few minutes north of the city by road, so staying near Lambton Quay or the waterfront is simplest. Give yourself more time than the check-in notice suggests: the sailing can be delayed or cancelled for wind, and if it is, you want to be somewhere with hotels and lunch rather than parked at a terminal.

Is the city walkable if I am not steady on hills?

The part you want is, entirely. The waterfront promenade from the railway station past Te Papa to Oriental Bay is level, paved and continuous, and the shops, museums and most restaurants are on the flat behind it. Wellington's famous hills start the moment you turn inland, and they are steep - but the cable car does the main climb for you, and the botanic gardens are then a downhill walk back into town. Stay on the waterfront or Lambton Quay and you need never take on a hill.
