Where to Stay in Taipei

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Taipei is one of the easiest big cities in Asia to stay in and a slightly confusing one to choose in, because the metro is so good that almost everywhere is convenient and the districts therefore differ by character rather than by distance. The short version: Zhongshan and the main station are the practical choice, Da’an is the pleasant one, Ximen is the cheap and lively one, Xinyi is the modern one and Beitou is the quiet soak. Below is what each is actually like to walk out of in the morning.

Zhongshan and Taipei Main Station — the practical choice

Most convenientAirport trainStep-free

If you are here for three nights and want to waste none of them, stay here. Taipei Main Station is the hub of everything: both original metro lines, the conventional railway, the high speed rail to the south, the long-distance buses and — the detail that matters most on arrival — the terminus of the Airport MRT, which runs from Taoyuan in about forty minutes. Zhongshan, one stop north, is the nicer half of the same area: a long underground bookshop-and-boutique arcade runs beneath the road, the Japanese-era department stores are up top, and there are more good small restaurants per street than anywhere else this central. The honest drawback is that the blocks immediately west and south of the station are the scruffiest part of central Taipei and the area is busy rather than charming.

Da’an and Yongkang Street — the pleasant choice

Best for eatingTree-linedQuieter at night

The part of Taipei that Taipei people like. Da’an spreads south and east of the centre around Yongkang Street, Dongmen and Daan Park, and it is low-rise, shaded, full of tea houses, bakeries, bookshops and small restaurants, with the big green park in the middle of it and two metro lines through it. The original Din Tai Fung is here and so is a great deal of better food that has no queue outside it. It is five to ten minutes on the metro from the main station, which is close enough that you lose nothing, and it is noticeably calmer after dark. Rooms skew towards smaller design hotels and serviced apartments rather than the big international names, and prices are a little above Ximen and a little below Xinyi.

Ximen and the old west side — cheap, lively, and the oldest Taipei

Cheapest bedsNight lifeHistoric

Ximending is the pedestrianised crossroads of young Taipei: neon, street performers, cinemas, cheap clothes and food until very late, with a metro station under it. South and west of it lies Wanhua, the oldest part of the city, where Longshan Temple, the herb market and the Bopiliao lanes are, and this is the most interesting square kilometre in Taipei for anybody who likes old things. Beds here are the cheapest in the city centre. Two honest cautions: the streets around Ximen are loud until the small hours, and the area immediately around Longshan Temple station is the roughest in central Taipei after dark — not dangerous by any international standard, but not everybody’s idea of a holiday street.

Xinyi — the modern end, under Taipei 101

Newest hotelsShoppingGood for a view

The planned eastern district: wide boulevards, department stores, the convention centre and Taipei 101 itself, with the Elephant Mountain trail starting a few minutes from the metro and giving the classic photograph of the tower for the price of a twenty-minute climb. This is where the newest and largest hotels are, where the big international brands cluster, and where a room with a view of the tower is actually possible. It is also the least characteristically Taiwanese part of Taipei: the ground floors are malls, the streets are built for cars and the small neighbourhood eating that makes the city special is a metro ride away. Good for a first-timer who wants comfort and the famous view; less good for a second visit.

Beitou — the hot-spring suburb

Hot springsQuiet40 minutes out

At the end of a short branch of the red line, Beitou is a whole hot-spring town inside the city limits, developed by the Japanese a century ago and still doing the same job: sulphur steam drifting out of the valley, public baths, a wonderful little museum in the old bath house, and hotels whose point is the water rather than the location. It is about forty minutes from the main station, which rules it out as a sightseeing base but makes it an excellent one or two nights at either end of a trip, particularly in the cool damp months from November to March when a hot spring is exactly what the northern weather calls for. Rooms with a private spring-fed bath are the thing to look for.

Getting around

The metro, which Taipei calls the MRT, is the reason this is such an easy city. Six lines, trains every few minutes, lifts at every station, bilingual signs and announcements, and it is spotless because eating and drinking on it are genuinely not done. Buy an EasyCard at any station or convenience store the moment you arrive: it pays for the metro, the buses, the local trains, the YouBike hire bicycles and your coffee, and it gives a small discount on each metro ride and on bus transfers.

From the airport, the Airport MRT runs from Taoyuan to Taipei Main Station in about forty minutes on the express service, for a fraction of a taxi fare, and it runs late. Within the city, taxis are plentiful, metered, cheap by European or American standards and easy to hail, which makes them the sensible answer for a late night or a hill. The buses are extensive and fine once you have the card, though the stop announcements are less consistently bilingual than the metro’s.

Taipei is flat in the middle and steep at the edges, and the riverside paths and the park network are genuinely good for walking and cycling. What catches visitors out is the covered arcades along older streets: shaded and welcome in the heat, but uneven, stepped and often parked full of scooters. Look both ways at every driveway.

When to come

Taiwan’s weather is split in two by the mountain range down its middle, and Taipei sits on the northern side of it. October and November are the best weeks of the year here, as they are everywhere on the island: warm, dry, clear and comfortable, with the typhoon season finished. Late March and April are the next best, before the humidity arrives and before the plum rains of mid-May to mid-June.

Summer, June to September, is hot and very humid in the Taipei basin, which traps the heat, and it is the typhoon window; it is also the cheapest time for a city room. Plan the outdoor part of the day before ten in the morning. Winter is the season to know about here: from December to February the northeast monsoon settles over the north of the island and Taipei goes damp, overcast and twelve to eighteen degrees for weeks at a time. It is the best season for the Beitou hot springs and for eating, and the worst for photographs.

The one week to book around, wherever you are going, is Lunar New Year, in late January or February: the whole island travels at once, trains sell out, room rates roughly double and many small restaurants close for several days. Getting in

Citizens of the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, the European Union and Schengen countries, Australia, New Zealand, Japan and South Korea, among others, are admitted to Taiwan visa-free for ninety days, with no fee and no application. Some other nationalities get thirty or fourteen days and some need a visa in advance, and the list is revised from time to time, so check your own passport at boca.gov.tw. There is also a free Taiwan Online Arrival Card to complete shortly before you land; use the official government site and not an agent charging for the same form.

Buy an EasyCard or an iPASS in the arrivals hall and keep it in your pocket for the rest of the trip: it pays for the metros, the buses, the local trains, the ferries and the hire bicycles the length of the island, and for your coffee in the convenience store. Taipei hotels FAQ

Which district should I pick for a first visit to Taipei?

Zhongshan or the main station area. You will be on top of the Airport MRT, both original metro lines, the high speed rail and the long-distance buses, which means no journey in the whole trip starts badly, and Zhongshan itself has the best concentration of ordinary good restaurants in central Taipei. If you have been before, or you want the city to feel like a neighbourhood rather than a hub, move to Da'an.

How do I get in from Taoyuan airport?

Take the Airport MRT. The express service reaches Taipei Main Station in about forty minutes, costs a small fraction of a taxi and runs from early morning until late at night. Buy an EasyCard at the station in arrivals and use it for the fare and for everything else afterwards. Airport buses are slower but cheaper still and run through the night; a taxi is worth it only with a lot of luggage or at an awkward hour. Songshan, the second airport, is inside the city and on the metro.

Is Taipei a good place to stay without a car?

It is one of the best in the world. Six metro lines with lifts at every station, frequent trains, bilingual signs, cheap metered taxis for the gaps and a hire-bike scheme on the same card. Nobody visiting Taipei should rent a car; you would spend the trip looking for parking.

Where should I stay for the hot springs?

Beitou, at the end of a branch of the red line and about forty minutes from the centre. It is a genuine hot-spring town with public baths, private spring-fed hotel rooms and a lovely museum in the old Japanese bath house. Stay there for a night or two rather than for the whole trip, and prefer the cool damp months from November to March when it is at its best. Jiaoxi, in Yilan about an hour away by train, is the other well-known option.

Is Taipei noisy at night?

It depends entirely on the street rather than the district. Ximending is loud until the small hours by design, the main station area is busy rather than rowdy, and Da'an and the Xinyi side streets are quiet. The thing that actually wakes people is scooters on a narrow lane rather than people, so when you book, look at which way the room faces and prefer a higher floor on a back elevation. Taiwanese hotels are generally well soundproofed and the city is not a late-drinking one by Asian standards.

Is it safe?

Very. Taipei is one of the safest large cities anywhere: street crime is rare, walking home late is normal, women travelling alone generally report feeling comfortable and lost property has a real habit of being handed in. The ordinary precautions about crowds and pickpockets apply at the night markets and nowhere much else. Traffic, and scooters in particular, are the genuine hazard rather than people.
