Budapest

Where to Stay in Budapest

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Budapest is really two old cities facing each other across the Danube, and the river is the first thing to settle. Flat, grand, busy Pest on the east bank holds the parliament, the boulevards, nearly all the restaurants and almost everywhere you will want to walk at night. Hilly Buda on the west holds the castle, the quiet and the views. Nearly every visitor should sleep in Pest, and the real question is which part of it — because the difference between District V and District VII is the difference between a quiet grand boulevard and a street that is still going at three in the morning. What follows is the five parts of town visitors actually stay in, and who each one suits.

District V — Belváros and Lipótváros, the inner city

Most centralGrand and quietDearest rooms

The flat heart of Pest along the river, running from the Great Market Hall up past the pedestrian Váci utca to the Parliament. This is the Budapest of the postcards: St Stephen’s Basilica, the Danube promenade with the castle lit up on the far bank, the Chain Bridge, the Gresham Palace, and the shoes on the embankment that memorialise the Jews shot into the river in 1944. It is handsome, safe, extremely walkable and well served by three metro lines meeting at Deák Ferenc tér. It is also the most expensive district to sleep in, the restaurants on Váci utca are the worst value in the country, and the northern end around the Parliament goes quiet in the evening in a way some people like and others find dull. Choose it for a first visit, for a short stay, or if you want to walk out of the door into the view.

District VI — Terézváros, along Andrássy út

Opera and boulevardsGood restaurantsOur usual pick

The district behind Andrássy út, the tree-lined avenue laid out on the Paris model that runs from the inner city out to Heroes’ Square, with the Opera House halfway along it and the 1896 Millennium Underground — the oldest underground railway on the European continent, and a listed monument you can ride with an ordinary ticket — running beneath. The side streets hold a great many of the city’s better small hotels and most of its serious restaurants, and you are ten minutes’ walk from the basilica one way and the City Park the other. It is calmer than District VII and cheaper than District V, which is why it is what we would usually book. The caveat is that the southern edge shades into the party quarter, so look at exactly which street before you commit.

District VII — Erzsébetváros, the old Jewish quarter and the ruin bars

Cheapest central roomsRestaurants and barsLoud at weekends

The most interesting part of Budapest to walk around and the most difficult to sleep in. This was the Jewish quarter and then the wartime ghetto; the Dohány Street synagogue, the largest in Europe, stands at its edge with the memorial garden behind it, and the ordinary streets carry their history plainly. Since the 2000s the same streets have become the ruin-bar quarter, where derelict courtyard buildings were filled with mismatched furniture and left that way — Szimpla Kert was the first and is still the one everybody visits. It has the cheapest central rooms, the best eating for the money and the most life. It is also genuinely loud from Thursday to Sunday, and a window over Kázmér, Kazinczy or the Gozsdu courtyard will keep you awake. Stay here for the atmosphere, and ask for a courtyard room.

District I — the Castle Quarter in Buda

ViewsVery quiet at nightCobbles and a hill

Up on the limestone ridge across the river: the royal palace, Matthias Church, the Fishermen’s Bastion and a grid of small baroque streets that empty completely once the coach parties leave. The views over the Danube and the Parliament at dusk are the best in the city and the quiet at night is total. The costs are real, though. The whole quarter is cobbled and on a hill, there are very few ordinary shops or restaurants, prices are aimed at visitors, and getting down to Pest means the funicular, a bus or a long walk over a bridge. It suits a returning visitor, a honeymoon or a traveller who wants calm more than convenience; it is a poor choice for a first short trip.

District XIV — the City Park and the Széchenyi baths

Green and calmBaths on the doorstepFamily rooms

The far end of Andrássy, past Heroes’ Square, where the city opens into the Városliget — a large park holding the great yellow Széchenyi bath house, the mock-medieval Vajdahunyad castle, the zoo and several of the city’s museums. Rooms here are quieter, greener and cheaper than in the centre, often with space enough for a family, and the M1 metro runs the length of the avenue into town in about ten minutes. It is also the obvious base if the baths are the reason you came, since you can be in the water before the day trippers arrive. The trade-off is that you are out of walking range of the restaurants and the river, and the immediate streets have little going on after dark.

Getting around

Budapest is a walking city in the middle and a public-transport city beyond it, and the system is excellent. Four metro lines cross under Pest and meet at Deák Ferenc tér; the yellow M1, opened in 1896, is itself a sight. Trams fill in the rest: the 2 runs along the Pest embankment with the castle in the window and is the best cheap sightseeing in the city, and the 4 and 6 circle the Grand Boulevard around the clock. Buses and trolleybuses reach the hills in Buda where the rails do not.

One ticket covers metro, tram, bus and trolleybus, and is sold singly, in blocks or as a travelcard by the day. Buy from the machines at every station and major stop, which take contactless cards and have an English setting, or in the transport app. Validate a single ticket when you board or at the metro gate; inspectors are frequent, particularly at the metro barriers, and fines are issued on the spot. A travelcard for the days you are there saves the fuss.

From the airport, a direct express bus runs into the inner city and needs its own ticket rather than an ordinary one; a cheaper combination of ordinary bus and metro does the same journey more slowly. Taxis in Budapest are regulated, metered and a fixed rate per kilometre by law, so the honest advice is to order one through the official rank, your hotel or an app rather than flagging one in the street. A car in Budapest is a nuisance: the centre is largely paid parking, several streets are pedestrian, and you will not use it.

When to come, and what it costs

Budapest is a genuine year-round city, which not every European capital is. Late May, June and September are the best weeks: warm, long and comfortable, with the terraces out and the queues sensible. July and August are hot — often in the low to middle thirties Celsius, the nineties Fahrenheit — and the stone city holds the heat into the evening, so check your room is air-conditioned and plan the afternoon around a bath. Mid November to early January brings the Christmas markets and some of the best atmosphere of the year, and January and February after the holidays are cold, quiet and much the cheapest time to come.

On price, Budapest is no longer the bargain it was a decade ago and in high summer and over the market weekends it is priced much like any popular European city. Where it is still very good value is midweek out of season, in District VI and District VII rather than District V, and in everything that is not the hotel room — eating, drinking, transport and museums are all cheap by western European standards. One week to book early and expect to pay for is the week of the twentieth of August. Hungary uses the Hungarian forint (HUF, written Ft), not the euro. Hungary has been in the European Union since 2004 but has kept its own currency and has set no date for changing it, so euro notes are not legal tender. A few hotels and tour desks quote in euros and some will take euro cash, almost always at a poor rate. Pay in forint. Cards and phones are taken nearly everywhere, including on buses, trams and at ticket machines, and you need very little cash — a small amount is handy for market stalls, thermal-bath lockers, church donation boxes and rural buses. Prices in shops include tax. In Budapest restaurants a service charge is often printed on the bill; check before adding a tip, and where there is none, ten per cent is plenty. Decline any card machine offering to charge you in dollars and let your own bank convert. Avoid the standalone exchange booths near the main tourist streets and at the airport, which are far worse than a bank machine or an ordinary exchange office in town.

Getting in: what changed, and what has not

Europe’s Entry/Exit System is now in force. Since 10 April 2026 it has operated at every Schengen border crossing and passport stamps have been discontinued. On your first crossing you will have your fingerprints taken and your photograph made at the border, which adds a few minutes; after that the check is quicker. There is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay.

ETIAS — the travel authorisation you may have read about, expected to cost around EUR 20 and last three years — is not in force yet. No applications are being taken, and the European Union has not announced the exact start date; it has said it will give several months’ notice before the system goes live. It has been postponed repeatedly and no start date is confirmed, so treat it as coming rather than cancelled, and ignore any website offering to sell you one today — nobody can issue one yet. When it does begin there will be six months in which you can travel without one, then six months in which only first-time arrivals are exempt.

What this means for your trip right now: you do not need ETIAS. You do need a passport issued within the last ten years and valid for at least three months beyond the date you plan to leave.

Hungary is a straightforward case. It has been in the European Union since 2004 and in the Schengen area since the end of 2007, so everything above applies here in full, and flying in from Vienna, Munich or Amsterdam involves no passport control at all. What Hungary has not joined is the euro, so the currency is the forint. Its borders with Austria, Slovakia, Slovenia, Croatia and Romania are all internal Schengen crossings now and you will usually drive or ride straight through them. The borders with Serbia and with Ukraine are external Schengen frontiers, where there are proper checks and the queues can be long. None of that affects an ordinary trip to the places in this guide.

Staying in Budapest — questions

Which district should I stay in in Budapest?

District V if you want to be in the middle of the postcard and do not mind paying for it; District VI, along and behind Andrassy avenue, if you want good restaurants, calm streets and a lower bill, which is what we would usually book; District VII if you want the cheapest central rooms and the most life, and can sleep through a Saturday night or will ask for a courtyard room. District I on the castle hill is beautiful and quiet but inconvenient, and District XIV by the City Park suits families and anyone coming for the baths.

Is Buda or Pest better for a visitor?

Pest, for almost everybody. It is flat, it holds nearly all the restaurants, bars, shops and nightlife, and the metro and tram network is dense there. Buda is hilly, quieter and residential apart from the castle quarter, and crossing the river at the end of every evening gets old faster than people expect. Stay in Pest and visit Buda; the view of Buda is one of the reasons to be on the Pest side anyway.

Are the ruin bars worth it, and are they noisy?

They are worth seeing once, and yes, they are noisy. The ruin bars are old courtyard buildings in District VII filled with salvaged furniture and left deliberately shabby; Szimpla Kert is the original and is busy every night of the week. The streets around them - Kazinczy, Dob, the Gozsdu courtyard - carry that noise until the early hours from Thursday to Sunday. If you are staying in District VII, ask specifically for a room off the street.

How do I get from the airport to the city?

There is a direct express bus from the terminal into the inner city, which is the simplest way and needs its own ticket rather than a standard one; buy it from the machine at the stop. A cheaper route takes an ordinary bus to the end of the blue metro line and the metro from there, and takes longer. Taxis are regulated at a fixed rate per kilometre, and the honest way to take one is from the official rank outside the terminal, through your hotel or by app.

Do I need to book the thermal baths in advance?

Not usually, but it helps at the busiest ones. Szechenyi in the City Park and Gellert take large numbers and you can generally turn up, though a ticket bought online saves the queue on a winter weekend. The smaller Ottoman baths, Rudas and Kiraly, fill quickly and have had single-sex sessions on particular days, so check the day you plan to go. Take a swimming costume, flip-flops and a towel, and bring a padlock or hire a locker.

Is Budapest safe?

Yes, by the standards of any large European city, and violent crime is rare. The things to watch are ordinary: pickpockets on the busiest trams and in the market hall, taxi drivers who are not from a proper rank, and the long-running bar scam in which a stranger invites you to a particular place and the bill arrives enormous. Use the official taxi ranks or an app, choose your own bar, and keep your bag in front of you on tram 2 and the M3.

How many days do I need in Budapest?

Three full days covers the inner city, the castle hill, one long bath and a proper dinner or two. Four or five lets you add the City Park, a museum day, a cruise or a walk on Margaret Island without rushing. If you have a week, give Budapest four nights and take the train to Eger or out to Lake Balaton for the rest - the country beyond the capital is cheaper and quite different.

Is a car any use in Budapest?

No. The inner districts are paid parking throughout, several of the best streets are pedestrian, the traffic on the boulevards is heavy, and public transport goes everywhere you want to go for very little. If you are hiring a car for the rest of Hungary, pick it up on the day you leave the city rather than on arrival, and leave it outside the centre if you come back.
