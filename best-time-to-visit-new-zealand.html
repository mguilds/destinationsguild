New Zealand, month by month

Best Time to Visit New Zealand

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

The first thing to get straight is that the calendar is inverted: Christmas falls in high summer and July is the depth of winter. The second is that New Zealand is two long islands with a mountain range down the middle of one of them, so “the weather” is really four or five different climates, and the honest answer to when you should go depends on which of them you are going for. Below is the month-by-month picture, the light, and the three or four dates that genuinely matter for prices.

The short answer

Four good answers, depending on what you came for.

For the best all-round trip

March, or late October to November. Settled weather, everything open, prices and crowds both off their peak.

For beaches & swimming

February. Warmest sea and driest weather, with the local holidays already over. Book early.

For skiing

July and August. Queenstown, Wanaka and Mount Ruapehu. Check the field’s own snow report first.

For value & empty roads

May, and June outside the ski towns. Cold, quiet, often brilliantly clear, and half the price.

Month by month

Weather, daylight and what is actually running. Where the two islands part company, both are described.

January

Peak summer and peak crowds. Warm, long days, every boat and track running - and the first half is the New Zealand holiday, so coastal towns are full and rates are at their highest. Superb if you have booked; frustrating if you have not.

February

The warmest and driest month, and the best for swimming, with the school holidays over and the country back at work. Still expensive, still busy in Queenstown, but the weather is as good as it gets.

March

Our pick. Settled, warm, sea still holding summer heat, the vineyards in harvest and Central Otago beginning to turn. Prices and crowds both step down from the middle of the month.

April

Autumn proper, and beautiful in the south: the poplars and willows around Wanaka, Arrowtown and Queenstown are the photograph everyone brings home. Daylight saving ends early in the month and the evenings shorten quickly. Easter is busy.

May

Quiet, cheap and often clear. Snow arrives on the tops, the high passes are still open, and the cities are at their most liveable. Some alpine tracks and a few seasonal boat trips stop running.

June

Winter begins. Short days, the ski fields opening late in the month, and Matariki - the Maori new year - now a public holiday. Fiordland and the West Coast are wet; Central Otago is cold, still and lovely.

July

Deep winter and the heart of the ski season. Reliable snow at Queenstown, Wanaka and Ruapehu, frost in the inland basins, and very few visitors anywhere else. Milford Road can need chains.

August

Winter’s wet end in the North Island and still good skiing in the South. The cheapest month in most of the country, and the one we would skip unless you ski or want the cities to yourself.

September

Spring, and underrated. Lambs, blossom, waterfalls at full snowmelt and the Alps still white. Weather is changeable - four seasons in a day is a local saying, not a joke. Daylight saving starts late in the month.

October

A fine month to travel. Gardens out, rivers high, tracks reopening, days lengthening fast and prices still modest. The mountains keep their snow for the photographs and the valleys are green.

November

Late spring, and our second choice after March. Long evenings, warm enough for the coast, everything open again, and the summer rush not yet arrived. Good for the Great Walks if you have booked.

December

Summer arrives and so does everybody else. The first three weeks are excellent and still reasonably priced; from about the twentieth the country shuts down for Christmas, the beaches fill and accommodation goes to holiday rates.

The two islands, and the two sides of the Alps

The North Island is the mild one. Its far north, around the Bay of Islands, is close to subtropical, winters are soft, frost is rare on the coast and rain falls in every month rather than in a season. Auckland is humid in February and grey in August, and neither is a reason to stay away. The central plateau around Taupo and Tongariro is the exception: it sits high, it is genuinely cold in winter, and the volcanoes hold snow.

The South Island is colder, clearer and far more extreme from one side of the island to the other. The Southern Alps catch the weather coming off the Tasman Sea, which is why the West Coast and Fiordland are among the wettest places on earth while Christchurch and Central Otago, in the lee of the same mountains, are dry enough to need irrigation. A westerly front can mean a downpour at Franz Josef and a warm, dusty afternoon in Alexandra on the same day.

What this means in practice is that you plan the South Island around which coast you will be on, not around the month. It also means that the sensible response to a bad forecast is often to drive two hours rather than to cancel the day.

And one warning that applies in every month: the ultraviolet here is among the strongest in the world, because the ozone layer is thin over the southern latitudes and the air is unusually clear. People burn badly in September and on overcast February afternoons. A hat and sunscreen are not a summer item in New Zealand; they are what the locals carry all year.

Holidays, school terms and what they do to prices

Four dates do most of the damage to a budget. The Christmas and New Year fortnight, roughly 20 December to 10 January, is the big one: this is the national summer holiday, every coastal town fills with New Zealanders, holiday-home and motel rates rise steeply and the campgrounds book out months ahead. Easter is a four-day weekend and busy wherever the weather is good. The July school holidays fill the ski towns. And Waitangi Day on 6 February and Matariki, the Maori new year, which falls on a Friday in June or July and moves with the lunar calendar, each make a long weekend.

Outside those, the pattern is simple and predictable: January and February are dearest, March and November are good value for the weather you get, and May, June and August are the cheapest months to be in the country. Queenstown is the one place that is expensive in both summer and ski season, so if the budget is tight, visit it in May or November.

Getting in: what you need, and what you pay

Most visitors from visa-waiver countries need a New Zealand Electronic Travel Authority, the NZeTA, requested before you fly, and most also pay the International Visitor Conservation and Tourism Levy, which is normally collected at the same time. There is a separate New Zealand Traveller Declaration to complete shortly before arrival, which has replaced the old paper arrival card.

Both the NZeTA fee and the levy have changed more than once in recent years, so we do not print the figures here — read them off the official page at immigration.govt.nz when you book, and request the NZeTA through Immigration New Zealand’s own app or website rather than any agent offering to do it for you. Australian citizens need neither the NZeTA nor the levy; Australian permanent residents who are not citizens do need the NZeTA.

Allow a little time: the NZeTA is usually granted quickly but not instantly, and the airline will check it before you board. And treat the biosecurity questions on arrival seriously — declare all food, and clean the soil off boots, tents and fishing tackle before you pack.

When to visit New Zealand — questions

What is the best month to visit New Zealand?

March, if we have to name one. The sea is still warm from summer, the weather is as settled as it gets, Central Otago is turning gold, the January crowds have gone home and rooms cost noticeably less than they did a month earlier. February is the warmest and driest month and is the right answer if a beach holiday is the point, but it is also peak price. For snow on the mountains with green in the valleys, try late October and November instead.

What is the worst time to visit New Zealand?

There is no bad month, but there are two awkward ones. The fortnight from Christmas to about the middle of January is when the entire country takes its own holiday: the coastal towns are full, holiday-home rates double, and anything you have not booked is gone. And August, in much of the North Island, is simply the wet end of winter with nothing seasonal to recommend it unless you ski. If you only have those dates, go anyway and base yourself in the cities.

Are the seasons really upside down?

Yes, completely. Summer is December, January and February; autumn is March to May; winter is June, July and August; spring is September to November. Christmas is a beach-and-barbecue affair, the school year runs February to December, and the long summer holiday falls over the New Year. Daylight saving begins in late September and ends in early April, which gives summer evenings that run past nine o'clock.

How different is the weather between the two islands?

Enough to plan around. The North Island is subtropical in the far north and mild everywhere, with its rain spread through the year; Auckland is humid in summer and rarely sees frost. The South Island is colder, drier on the eastern plains and extraordinarily wet on the western side of the Alps, where Fiordland takes several metres of rain a year. The practical rule is that the North Island asks what month it is and the South Island asks which side of the mountains you are on.

Do I need to worry about rain in Fiordland?

Plan for it rather than worry about it. Milford Sound is one of the wettest inhabited places on earth and rain is the normal condition, not a ruined day: the walls of the fjord grow hundreds of temporary waterfalls within an hour of a downpour, and many visitors who get a wet sailing come back saying it was the better one. Take a proper waterproof, accept that the peaks may be in cloud, and book the boat anyway.

When can I see the southern lights?

The aurora australis is possible from the far south of the South Island and Stewart Island, best between about April and September when the nights are long, and it is a much less reliable proposition than the northern lights in Scandinavia. Treat it as a bonus rather than a reason to travel. What the southern skies do deliver dependably is stars: the Aoraki Mackenzie reserve around Lake Tekapo is one of the darkest accessible places in the world, and the Milky Way there on a clear winter night is the real show.

When is ski season?

Roughly late June to early October, with the most dependable snow in July and August. The commercial fields are around Queenstown and Wanaka in the south and on Mount Ruapehu in the central North Island, and conditions vary a great deal from year to year because the fields are low by Alpine standards and depend on the weather of that particular winter. Check the field's own snow report before committing to a trip built around it.

How far ahead should I book?

For a trip in December or January, six months is not too early for accommodation and campervans, and for the Great Walk huts it is not early enough - those open for booking months in advance and the Milford and Routeburn tracks sell out within days. For March, April, October and November, two or three months is comfortable. For midwinter outside the ski towns you can very nearly turn up. Hire cars in summer are the thing people leave too late.
