Why visit Morocco

Few countries pack so much variety into one trip — imperial cities, high mountains, desert and two coastlines, all within a few hours of each other.

The imperial cities

Marrakech, Fes, Meknes and Rabat — walled medinas, palaces and madrasas, and souks that have traded spice, leather and silver for a thousand years.

The Sahara & the Atlas

Cross the High Atlas by the Tizi n'Tichka pass to the kasbahs of Aït Benhaddou and Ouarzazate, and on to the golden dunes of Merzouga and the desert night.

Two coastlines

Atlantic surf and breeze at Essaouira and Agadir, and the Mediterranean and the Strait at Tangier, where Africa looks across to Spain.

Food & the art of the riad

Tagines and couscous, mint tea and pastries, and the quiet courtyard riads — traditional houses turned into some of the most romantic small hotels anywhere.

How the country divides up

Marrakech & the Atlas

Most trips begin in Marrakech, and it should. The great square of Jemaa el-Fnaa fills each evening with food stalls, musicians and storytellers, the souks spill out behind it, and the Koutoubia minaret presides over it all. Beyond the palm groves rise the snow-capped High Atlas, crossed by the dramatic Tizi n'Tichka pass to the mud-brick kasbah of Aït Benhaddou.

The Imperial Cities

Fes holds the world's largest living medieval city — a car-free maze of some 9,000 lanes, the Qarawiyyin (one of the oldest universities on earth) and the famous tanneries. Nearby Meknes guards the monumental Bab Mansour gate, and the seaside capital Rabat pairs a walled kasbah with the calm of a modern administrative city.

The Atlantic Coast

Casablanca is Morocco's economic heart, an Art Deco port city crowned by the vast Hassan II Mosque rising over the ocean. Down the coast, walled Essaouira catches the trade winds for kite- and wind-surfing, and Agadir anchors the sunniest bay in the country with a long crescent beach and resort after resort.

The North & the Blue City

Tangier sits where the Mediterranean meets the Atlantic, a fabled port of writers and spies now smartly reborn, with Spain visible across the Strait. Inland in the Rif rise the cobalt-and-white lanes of Chefchaouen, the photogenic "Blue City" and one of the loveliest small towns in the country.

Top things to do in Morocco

The experiences worth building a trip around.

Jemaa el-Fnaa & the souks

Marrakech's legendary square at dusk — grilled food, gnaoua drummers and snake charmers — and the labyrinth of souks selling lanterns, leather and spice.

The medina of Fes

Lose yourself in the world's greatest medieval maze: the Qarawiyyin, the Bou Inania madrasa, and the vats of the Chouara tanneries seen from a leather-shop terrace.

A night in the Sahara

Cross the Atlas to Merzouga or Zagora, ride a camel over the dunes at sunset, and sleep in a desert camp under a blaze of stars.

Aït Benhaddou

The tiered mud-brick ksar above the Ounila valley, a UNESCO site and a favourite film location, glowing gold in the late-afternoon light.

The Hassan II Mosque

Casablanca's ocean-front giant, one of the largest mosques in the world, with a 210-metre minaret and a hall that opens to the sky and the sea.

Chefchaouen, the Blue City

Wander the blue-washed alleys of the Rif mountain town, all indigo walls and terracotta roofs, and one of the most photographed places in Morocco.

Best time to visit & getting there

When to go

March–May and September–November are the sweet spots: warm, dry days and comfortable evenings, ideal for the cities, the Atlas and the desert.

June–August is very hot inland, especially Marrakech and the Sahara — though the Atlantic coast at Agadir and Essaouira stays cooler and breezy, and prices in the cities ease.

See our month-by-month guide to the best time to visit Morocco for weather, crowds and prices through the year.

Getting there

Casablanca Mohammed V (CMN) is the main long-haul gateway, with the widest choice of intercontinental flights, including direct services from North America.

Marrakech Menara (RAK) is the busiest for holidaymakers, with Agadir (AGA), Fes (FEZ) and Tangier (TNG) handy for the regions. Fast trains link Tangier, Rabat, Casablanca and Marrakech.

The currency is the Moroccan dirham. Carry cash for souks, taxis and tips; cards work in hotels and larger venues. Most visitors do not need a visa for short stays — confirm the current rules with the official Moroccan authority before booking.

Morocco travel FAQ

When is the best time to visit Morocco?

Spring (March to May) and autumn (September to November) are the sweet spots — warm, dry days and comfortable evenings, ideal for Marrakech, Fes and the desert. Summer is very hot inland, especially in Marrakech and the Sahara, though the Atlantic coast at Agadir and Essaouira stays cooler and breezy. Winter is mild and pleasant on the coast and in the cities, but cold in the Atlas Mountains, where the high passes and ski slopes see snow.

What currency is used in Morocco?

The Moroccan dirham (MAD, often written dh or DH). It is a closed currency, so you generally change money on arrival rather than beforehand. Cards are accepted in hotels, larger restaurants and shops in the cities and resorts, but carry cash for souks, taxis, tips and smaller towns. ATMs are widespread in cities. Keep small notes for markets and tipping.

Do I need a visa to visit Morocco?

Many visitors, including US, UK, Canadian, Australian and EU citizens, do not need a visa for tourist stays of up to 90 days — you simply get a stamp on arrival. Your passport should be valid for the length of your stay. Requirements can change, so always confirm the current rules with the official Moroccan authority or your nearest consulate before you travel.

How many days do you need in Morocco?

For a first trip, eight to ten days is ideal: two or three days in Marrakech, a couple in Fes for the medieval medina, and a two- or three-day loop over the Atlas to Aït Benhaddou and a night in the Sahara. With only a week, pair Marrakech with a desert excursion, or combine Marrakech with a few relaxed days on the coast at Essaouira or Agadir.

Is Morocco safe for travellers?

The main tourist cities — Marrakech, Fes, Casablanca, Tangier, Rabat and the coast — welcome millions of visitors a year and are generally safe, with a visible tourist-police presence. Take normal city precautions against petty theft, and expect firm-but-friendly haggling with touts and faux guides in the medinas. Women travellers may attract attention; dressing modestly helps. Check your government's current travel advisory before you go.
