When to go

Best Time to Visit Canada

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated November 2026

Canada has a real four-season year and six time zones to spread it across, so “when to go” depends as much on which Canada you mean as on the calendar. The short answer for a first trip is June to early October, and September if you can choose. Below is the year month by month, and then the parts of it worth planning around.

The short answer

Four ways to use the year, depending on what you came for.

Best overall — September

The crowds go home after the Labour Day weekend, the mountain west stays clear, the sea is still warm and the eastern maples start to turn from mid-month. Prices come down with the crowds.

Best for the outdoors — late June to mid-September

Everything is open, every trail and boat is running and the days are long. July and August are the warmest and by a wide margin the busiest and dearest, particularly in the Rockies.

Best for snow — January to March

The ski season in British Columbia, Alberta and Quebec, the winter carnivals at their coldest and best, and the northern lights. Properly cold away from the Pacific coast.

Cheapest — November and early April

Between seasons, with nothing much on and hotels at their lowest. Good for a city trip, poor for anything that needs either snow or leaves.

Canada month by month

The year as it actually feels, with the honest low points left in.

January

The coldest month almost everywhere, and properly cold away from the Pacific: Toronto and Montreal often below freezing all day, the prairies and the interior colder again. It is also the cheapest month for a city room, the best of the ski season, and prime northern-lights weather. Vancouver sits out of it, mild and wet.

February

Still deep winter, with the clearest, brightest cold of the year and the winter festivals at their best — Quebec City in particular is built for this month. Ski conditions are usually the best of the season. Days are lengthening noticeably by the end of it.

March

The turn. Skiing holds in the mountains and the Laurentians through most of it, the cities thaw into slush, and Quebec and Ontario tap the maple trees. School holidays fall somewhere in here and fill the ski towns for a week. An unglamorous month in the cities and a cheap one.

April

Mud season, and the one month we would not plan a trip around. The snow goes, the ice breaks up, the trails are wet, many seasonal attractions and mountain roads are not open yet and the ski hills are closing. The compensation is low prices and no crowds, and the Pacific coast gardens are already out.

May

A genuinely good month and an underrated one. The cities are green and warm enough to sit outside, the gardens are at their best, Niagara and the St Lawrence are in full flow, the whale season is beginning on both coasts, and almost nothing is crowded yet. Some high mountain roads and trails are still under snow.

June

Summer arrives and the country opens. Long days — very long in the north — warm weather, every boat, gondola and park service running by the middle of the month, and prices still below the July peak until the schools break up at the end of it. One of the two best months for the Rockies.

July

High summer. The warmest, busiest and dearest month, with the Canada Day weekend at the start of it and the national parks at capacity. Book Banff, Lake Louise and Vancouver well ahead or accept what is left. In exchange you get reliable weather, the longest days and everything open.

August

As warm as July and as busy, with the sea at its warmest on both coasts and the lakes swimmable. The aurora season starts again in the north late in the month. Smoke from forest fires can affect the mountain west and the interior in a dry summer, which is worth knowing rather than worrying about.

September

Our own pick. Within a fortnight of the Labour Day weekend the crowds thin out, the Rockies turn cool and clear, the sea is still warm, prices step down, and from the middle of the month the maples of Ontario, Quebec and the Maritimes begin to turn. If you can only come once, come now.

October

The first half is the peak of the autumn colour through Ontario, Quebec and the Atlantic provinces and is magnificent; the second half is bare trees, grey skies and the quietest, cheapest weeks of the year. Thanksgiving falls on the second Monday and fills the country roads for a long weekend. Snow is possible in the mountains throughout.

November

The low month. Cold, dark, wet and between seasons, with the autumn colour gone and the ski hills not yet open. Hotels are at their cheapest. If you are coming for a city, the museums, the restaurants and the hockey are all at their best and you will have them to yourself.

December

Winter proper, and the cities make a great deal of it: Christmas markets, lights, skating rinks in the public squares. The ski season opens through the month. The fortnight around Christmas and New Year is busy and dear everywhere; the first two weeks of December are quiet and good value.

Where, and when

The country is too big for one answer, so take it region by region. For the Rockies — Banff, Lake Louise, Jasper and the Icefields Parkway — the season is late June to the middle of September, and September is the pick of it. For Ontario and Quebec, May to October, with late September and early October the colour weeks and the winter carnivals a separate reason to come in February. For British Columbia and the Pacific, May to September; Vancouver and Victoria are mild all year but wet from November to March.

For the Atlantic provinces — Nova Scotia, New Brunswick, Prince Edward Island and Newfoundland — the window is narrower: late June to early October, with the Cabot Trail at its best in the first half of October and the icebergs off Newfoundland in late May and June. For the North, two quite different seasons: the long light of June and July for the land and the rivers, and the dark months for the aurora.

One practical note that applies everywhere: Canadian school holidays run from the end of June to the start of September, and that is when the country is on the road. If your dates are flexible by a fortnight either side of that, you will pay less and queue less for the same weather. Our Canada travel guide sets out what is where.

Weeks to book around

Christmas and New Year

The fortnight from about the twentieth of December is the busiest travel period of the year. Flights, trains and ski accommodation sell out and rates roughly double.

Canada Day, 1 July

The national holiday and the start of the high season. The long weekend around it fills the cities, the parks and the campgrounds.

Labour Day, first Monday in September

The last weekend of the summer and a busy one. The week after it is one of the best weeks of the year to arrive.

Thanksgiving, second Monday in October

Canadian Thanksgiving, not the American one. It lands in the middle of the autumn colour and fills the country inns and the colour-country roads.

The March school break

A week in March, varying by province, when the ski towns are full of families and rates rise with them.

Quebec’s construction holiday

Two weeks in late July when a large part of Quebec takes its holiday at the same time. Worth knowing if you are travelling in that province.

Getting in, whatever month you choose

Most visitors from visa-exempt countries arriving by air need an Electronic Travel Authorization, an eTA, applied for online before they fly and tied to the passport they travel on. Citizens of the United States do not need one but do need proof of citizenship. Nationals of visa-required countries need a visitor visa instead, which takes considerably longer. An eTA is not required if you arrive by land or sea.

We do not print the fees or the processing times here, because both have been revised more than once and a stale number is worse than none: read them off the official pages at canada.ca when you book, and apply there rather than through an agent charging a mark-up for the same form. Apply at the time you book rather than at the airport — approval is usually quick, but not always.

Two things worth checking against your own circumstances well before you travel: past criminal convictions, including an old drink-driving conviction, can make a traveller inadmissible to Canada, and there is a formal process for it that is not quick; and taking cannabis across the Canadian border in either direction is a federal offence whatever is legal at home.

Best time to visit Canada FAQ

What is the single best month to visit Canada?

September, for most people and most of the country. The summer crowds have gone within two weeks of the Labour Day weekend, the mountain west is cool and clear, the sea is still warm on both coasts, room rates have come down, and the autumn colour starts in the eastern hardwoods from about the middle of the month. June is the other strong answer: long days, everything open and prices not yet at their July peak.

When is the autumn colour at its best?

Late September to the middle of October, moving from north to south and from high ground to low. Quebec, eastern Ontario and the Maritimes are the places to be, and the Laurentians, the Charlevoix, Algonquin Park and Cape Breton are the classic drives. Peak colour in any given valley lasts a week or two and shifts by a week or more from year to year, so pick the region and keep the dates loose rather than the other way round.

When is the cheapest time to go?

November, and the first half of April. Both are between seasons: the autumn colour has gone and the ski hills are not open in November, and in April the snow has gone but the summer has not arrived. City hotels are at their lowest in both. Late January and early February are also inexpensive in the cities, though not in the ski towns.

When should I come for the Rockies?

Late June to the middle of September for the full programme, with July and August the warmest, busiest and dearest. September is the sweet spot if you can take it: the trails are open, the lakes still hold their colour and the coach traffic has eased. Check what Parks Canada is running on timed entry or shuttles before you set out, because the arrangements at the busiest lakes change from season to season.

When can I see the northern lights?

From late August through to about April, with the long dark nights of the winter months giving the best odds. The Yukon and the Northwest Territories are the reliable places, and Yellowknife in particular. You need darkness, a clear sky and patience: three or four nights in one place gives you a far better chance than one.

Is winter a bad time to visit?

No, as long as you go for winter rather than in spite of it. The skiing in British Columbia, Alberta and the Quebec Laurentians is world class, the city festivals and the frozen carnivals are at their best in the coldest weeks, the museums and restaurants are at their liveliest, and the aurora is a winter business. What you need is the clothing: a windproof coat, a hat, gloves and boots with grip. Vancouver and Victoria are the mild exception, wet rather than cold.

Which weeks should I book around?

Four. The fortnight around Christmas and New Year, when the whole country travels; the Canada Day long weekend at the start of July; the Labour Day long weekend at the start of September, which is the last of the summer; and Thanksgiving on the second Monday of October, which fills the colour-country roads and inns. Add the March school break if you are going anywhere near a ski hill.

How different is the weather across the country?

Enormously, and it is the thing visitors most often get wrong. On the same February day Vancouver can be nine degrees and raining while Winnipeg is minus twenty-five; in July the Okanagan and southern Ontario are hot while the Newfoundland coast is in fog at fifteen. Pack for the region you are actually going to, and if you are crossing the country in one trip, pack for two climates and expect to use both.
