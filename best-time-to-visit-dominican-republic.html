Best Time to Visit the Dominican Republic

Best Time to Visit the Dominican Republic — Month by Month

Curated by Our Guild Explorers · Updated August 2026

Most guides to this country give you one answer. That is the mistake. The Dominican Republic has two opposing weather calendars, and which one applies to you depends entirely on which coast you have booked.

The east and south-east — Punta Cana, Bávaro, La Romana — are driest from December to April. The north coast and Samaná face the Atlantic on the windward side of a mountain range, so the trade winds bring their rain in late autumn and winter: November and December are their wettest months, exactly when the east is at its finest. Booking Puerto Plata for Christmas sun is the commonest mistake travellers make here.

Below: every month judged honestly, then each coast on its own terms, plus the three things that actually decide a Caribbean holiday — hurricanes, seaweed, and the whales.

The Short Answer

If you read nothing else on this page, read this.

February to April is the safest bet anywhere in the country

$$$ Peak but worth itDry on every coastWhales to 31 MarchNo storm risk

These three months are the only stretch when every coast is at or near its best: dry in the east, dry in the north, low humidity, the calmest Atlantic of the year, no hurricane risk whatsoever, and the humpback whales in Samaná Bay until the end of March. February is the finest single month in the country.

If price matters more than perfection, April is the value pick — once Easter passes, the crowds thin and rates fall while the weather stays essentially dry-season. And the first two weeks of December are a quiet, well-priced window on the east coast before the Christmas rush.

And the month to avoid, if you have the choice

August–SeptemberHottest & wettestPeak storm risk

August is the hottest, stickiest and wettest month on the east coast, with sargassum still about and hurricane risk climbing hard. September contains the statistical peak of the season on the 10th and is the cheapest week of the year for exactly that reason. Both are perfectly survivable with a flexible booking — but if your dates are free, spend them elsewhere in the calendar.

Month by Month

Temperatures barely move here — highs sit between 81 and 89°F all year. It is the rain, the crowds and the price that change.

January

$$$ PeakEast: driestNorth: still wetWhales from 15th

The east coast is at its driest — Punta Cana sees barely an inch of rain and highs around 81°F — and it is priced accordingly. The north coast is a different story: still in its wet spell, cloudier and greyer. The humpback whale season opens in Samaná Bay on the 15th.

February

$$$ PeakBest all-roundPeak whalesLow humidity

Arguably the single best month in the country. Dry and comfortable on every coast, humidity low, the Atlantic at its calmest, and peak humpback sightings in Samaná Bay. Prices are high and the good resorts fill early, so book well ahead.

March

$$$ PeakDriest month eastWhales to the 31stEaster varies

The driest month of the year on the east coast, with the highest daily sunshine, and excellent everywhere else too. Whale season runs to the 31st. The one thing to plan around is Semana Santa — Dominican Holy Week — when the whole country goes to the beach at once.

April

$$ ValueBest valueStill dryPost-Easter dip

Our pick for value. Once Easter has passed the crowds thin and rates drop, while the weather is still essentially dry-season on the east coast and very good on the north. If you want February’s holiday at something closer to September’s price, come in late April.

May

$$ ValueFirst rains eastWettest in the capitalQuiet

The first real rains arrive on the east coast, and May is the wettest month of the year in Santo Domingo. Showers are typically short and mostly in the afternoon, so a beach holiday still works — and it is quiet and cheap. Highs climb to about 83°F.

June

$$ ValueNorth coast’s driestHurricane season opensSargassum begins

An underrated month, and the driest of the year on the north coast — if Puerto Plata or Samaná is your destination, June is genuinely good. Hurricane season opens on the 1st, though June activity is historically rare. Sargassum seaweed starts arriving on the east-facing beaches.

July

$$ ShoulderHotSargassum peak eastFamily holidays

Hot and humid, with more rain on the east coast and sargassum at or near its peak on those east-facing beaches. The north coast is drier and the better bet this month. Prices firm up with the school holidays without reaching winter levels.

August

$$ ShoulderHottestWettest eastStorm risk climbing

The hottest, stickiest month, the wettest on the east coast, and the point at which hurricane risk starts climbing in earnest. Sargassum is still about. Honestly, this is the hardest month of the year for us to recommend to our readers, and the one to skip if you have a choice.

September

$ CheapestPeak hurricane seasonWettestLowest prices

The statistical peak of Atlantic hurricane season falls on the 10th, and September is the cheapest week of the year for exactly that reason. Rain is frequent and the resorts are quiet. If you travel now, book a flexible rate and buy insurance before any storm is named.

October

$ ValueStorm season easingWetBargains

Still inside storm season, though the risk declines steadily through the month, and still wet on every coast. The bargains are real and the beaches empty. A gamble that often pays off — but only with a refundable booking behind it.

November

$$ MixedEast improvingNorth coast’s worstSeason ends 30th

The two halves of the island part company sharply. The east coast improves through the month as the dry season returns; the north coast has its wettest month of the year. Hurricane season ends on the 30th. Go east this month, not north.

December

$$$ PeakEast dry againNorth wetChristmas rush

The first two weeks are one of the best-value windows of the whole year on the east coast: dry-season weather, modest prices, few crowds. After about the 18th it becomes the most expensive fortnight of the year. The north coast, meanwhile, is at its wettest — do not book Puerto Plata for Christmas sun.

Best Time by Coast

The country is really four travel regions with different calendars. Time your trip by where you are actually going.

The east — Punta Cana, Bávaro & La Romana

Best: Dec–AprDriest: MarchWettest: August

The south-east coast is the country’s driest and the reason it fills up every winter. December to April brings highs of 81–86°F, low rainfall and calm seas, with March the driest month of all. August and September are the wettest and the least comfortable.

The trade-off is sargassum seaweed, which lands on these east-facing beaches from roughly June to August. Bayahíbe and Playa Dominicus, facing south behind reef, are the exception and stay clear almost all year.

The south — Santo Domingo

Best: Jan–MarWettest: MayWarm year-round

The capital runs a two-peak rain pattern: driest from January to March, wettest in May, then wet again from September to November. Highs run 84–89°F, warmer by day than the coasts and cooler at night.

Because the Zona Colonial is a walking destination, heat matters more here than rain. Sightsee in the morning, retreat to a courtyard at midday, and go out again in the evening — that is how the city is meant to be used.

The north — Puerto Plata, Sosúa & Cabarete

Best: Feb–AprWettest: Nov–DecAlso good: Jun–Jul

This is the calendar most travellers get wrong. The Amber Coast faces the Atlantic on the windward side of the Cordillera Septentrional, so the north-east trades push its rain into late autumn and winter. November and December are its wettest months, with January still high — precisely when the east coast is at its best.

February to April is the window: about ten rainy days a month, highs of 81–86°F, low humidity, calm sea and no storm risk. June and July are drier still but hotter. Rain here usually falls overnight or early and clears to sun. Sargassum is close to a non-issue on this coast.

The north-east — the Samaná peninsula

Best: Feb–AprWhales: 15 Jan–31 MarGreen all year

Samaná shares the north’s Atlantic exposure — wetter and greener than the south-east, with rain spread through the year and a wetter spell from November to January. February to April is both the driest stretch and the tail of whale season, which is a fortunate overlap.

July and August are hot and fine for the beaches but sit inside hurricane season. The peninsula’s north-facing beaches are largely spared sargassum.

Hurricane Season, Honestly

Neither alarming nor something to wave away — here is what the record actually shows.

The dates, and the real odds

1 June – 30 NovemberPeak: 10 SeptemberSharp end: mid-Aug to mid-Oct

The Atlantic season runs 1 June to 30 November, but the risk is not spread evenly across it: the concentrated window is mid-August to mid-October, and the climatological peak is 10 September. June and the second half of November carry meaningfully lower odds.

In practice, roughly six tropical systems a year come within the Dominican Republic’s sphere of influence and about half never reach land at all. A genuinely damaging landfall is a once-in-several-years event, not an annual one — Fiona in September 2022 was the most consequential recent strike. And because the island’s mountains wring storms out, damage here is more often from rainfall and flooding than from wind.

What we would actually do

Flexible ratesInsure early3–5 days’ warning

Book a refundable rate, or a resort with a published hurricane guarantee — many Dominican all-inclusives have one. Buy travel insurance before a storm is named: once a system has a name it becomes a known event and cover for it is void. That single point catches more travellers out than anything else.

You will get three to five days of usable forecast, and airlines almost always issue change waivers forty-eight to seventy-two hours ahead, so nobody is caught unaware. Resorts are built to code and staff are drilled. The realistic downside of travelling in season is a shortened or rearranged holiday, not danger.

The Seaweed Question

For a beach holiday, sargassum disrupts more trips than hurricanes do — and unlike hurricanes, you can plan around it precisely.

When it comes, and which coasts escape it

Peak: Jun–AugClearest: Dec–FebEast-facing beaches worst

Sargassum drifts west across the Atlantic and lands on east-facing shores. It builds from March to May, peaks roughly June to August, then tapers through the autumn; December to February is the clearest window of all.

Worst affected: Bávaro, Uvero Alto and Macao, which face due east. Largely spared: the entire north coast — Puerto Plata, Sosúa, Cabarete — which sits sideways to the drift and stayed clean even through the record years of 2025 and 2026; Samaná’s north-facing beaches; and above all Bayahíbe, Playa Dominicus, Saona and Catalina, which face south behind sheltering reef and are the most dependably clear water in the country.

Two things worth knowing even in a bad week: it is a shoreline phenomenon, and the water is usually clear a few hundred metres out; and the large resorts rake their beaches every morning. Within Punta Cana itself, Cap Cana and Playa Juanillo stay noticeably cleaner than Bávaro.

The Whales of Samaná Bay

If you can travel between mid-January and the end of March, this is worth reshaping a trip around.

15 January to 31 March, and why it is world-class

Season: 15 Jan – 31 MarPeak: mid-Feb to early Mar95%+ sightings

Samaná Bay is the principal breeding and calving ground for the entire North Atlantic humpback population — whales arrive here from Iceland, Norway, Greenland, Canada and New England. They come to mate, give birth and nurse rather than to feed, which makes them unusually surface-active and easy to watch.

The bay sits inside a twenty-five-thousand-square-kilometre marine mammal sanctuary, the first established in the Atlantic, and it is properly regulated: a capped number of licensed operators, no more than three boats on any group, a minimum approach of eighty metres from mothers and calves, and a thirty-minute limit. Sighting rates exceed ninety-five per cent at peak.

Trips leave the Santa Bárbara waterfront and run three to four hours. Two-metre swells are normal in February — take something for seasickness an hour beforehand and book the earliest departure, when the sea is calmest. See where to stay in our Samaná guide.

The Cheapest Time to Go

Three windows where the price falls faster than the weather does.

Late April and May — the value sweet spot

$ Best valuePost-EasterShort afternoon rain

Once Semana Santa has passed, crowds thin and rates drop while the east coast is still running on essentially dry-season weather. May brings the first real rains, but they fall as short afternoon showers rather than all-day washouts. This is where we would send a reader who wants February’s holiday without February’s bill.

The first two weeks of December — the quiet window

$$ Good valueEast coast dryBefore the rush

Hurricane season has ended, the east coast’s dry season has returned, and the Christmas surcharge has not yet arrived. After about the 18th of December it becomes the most expensive fortnight of the year, so the first half of the month is a genuine bargain on the east coast. Not the north coast, which is at its wettest.

September and October — cheapest, and why

$ CheapestPeak storm riskRefundable only

These are the lowest prices of the year and the resorts are quiet, but the reason is written on the calendar: the peak of hurricane season and the wettest weeks on the east coast. It can be a wonderful, near-empty holiday. Only book it on a refundable rate with insurance bought in advance, and go in knowing the plan may have to move.

Frequently Asked Questions

The questions Guild readers ask most when timing a trip to the Dominican Republic.

What is the best time to visit the Dominican Republic?

It depends on the coast, and this is the single most useful thing to know. For Punta Cana, La Romana and the south-east, the dry season is December to April, with March driest. For Puerto Plata, Sosúa, Cabarete and Samaná the pattern reverses — November to January is their wettest stretch and February to April is their best. February is the one month that is excellent everywhere, and it is peak whale season too.

What is the cheapest time to visit the Dominican Republic?

September is the cheapest week of the year, and it is cheap because it is the statistical peak of hurricane season and the wettest part of it. A better bargain for most travellers is late April and May, once Easter has passed — near dry-season weather at low-season prices — or the first two weeks of December on the east coast, before the Christmas rush lifts rates to their annual high.

How risky is hurricane season in the Dominican Republic?

The Atlantic season runs 1 June to 30 November, concentrated from mid-August to mid-October and peaking around 10 September. About six systems a year come within the country’s sphere and roughly half never reach land; a genuinely damaging landfall is a once-in-several-years event rather than an annual one. You get three to five days of usable forecast and airlines issue waivers well ahead, so you will not be caught unaware. Book a flexible rate and buy insurance before a storm is named — once it is named, cover for it is void.

When is the seaweed worst, and which beaches escape it?

Sargassum builds from March to May and peaks roughly June to August, tapering through the autumn; December to February is clearest. It lands on east-facing shores, so Bávaro, Uvero Alto and Macao take the brunt. Largely spared: the whole north coast (Puerto Plata, Sosúa, Cabarete), Samaná’s north-facing beaches, and above all Bayahíbe, Playa Dominicus, Saona and Catalina, which face south behind reef. Within Punta Cana itself, Cap Cana and Playa Juanillo stay noticeably cleaner.

When can I see the humpback whales in Samaná?

The 2026 season runs 15 January to 31 March and the dates are strictly enforced, with peak sightings from mid-February to early March. Samaná Bay sits inside a twenty-five-thousand-square-kilometre marine mammal sanctuary — the first in the Atlantic — and is the principal breeding and calving ground for the whole North Atlantic humpback population. Sighting rates exceed ninety-five per cent at peak. Trips leave from the Santa Bárbara waterfront; book the earliest boat for the calmest sea.

Does it rain all day in the Dominican Republic?

Usually not. Outside the wettest weeks, rain tends to arrive as a heavy but short afternoon shower that clears to sunshine, and mornings are often the brightest part of the day. On the north coast it more often falls overnight or early. That is why green-season travel works better than the rainfall charts suggest — sightsee and swim in the morning, and keep your plans flexible after lunch.

When should I avoid the Dominican Republic?

August and September if you have a free choice — hottest, wettest on the east coast, sargassum still about and hurricane risk at its highest. Beyond that, avoid the mismatches: do not book Puerto Plata or Samaná in November or December, which are those coasts’ wettest months however peak the season is elsewhere, and expect crowds and top prices at Christmas, New Year and Semana Santa.
